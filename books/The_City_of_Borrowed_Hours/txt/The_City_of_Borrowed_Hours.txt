THE CITY OF BORROWED HOURS
Barış Kısır

Chapter 1: Yesterday's Dead

The first memory Mara Vale opened that morning contained a dead man complaining about the soup.

There was nothing unusual about the dead appearing in deposits. They came through the archive every day, kissing children whose children were old, walking into shops demolished before Mara was born. Death did not invalidate a memory. Usually it increased the owner's interest in surrendering it.

What made Mara put down her tea was the date.

The experience had been recorded yesterday, at sixteen minutes past eleven.

She checked the small brass clock above her desk. Its pendulum was moving. The building was cold. Outside the narrow window, rain softened the roofs of Bellwether into an assortment of wet grey shoulders. Everything continued to behave as it should.

She checked the deposit again.

Nera Pell, seventy-three, retired textile machinist. Voluntary relief, six minutes and nine seconds. Presenting concern: persistent anxiety associated with a medical consultation. Recording verified by an independent intake reader. Time signature intact.

In the memory, someone lifted a spoon. The soup had a thin yellow skin, and the spoon made a small ceramic sound against the bowl. The owner of the hand was looking down, so Mara saw no faces. She felt, faintly, the ache in Nera Pell's knuckles. She smelled boiled onions.

Then the man across the table said, "If this gets any thinner, we can use it to predict the tide."

Her brother had been dead for nine years.

Mara lifted the contact band from her temples. The archive returned around her: green desk lamp, half-filled transfer ledger, a cracked blue cup. She pressed her palms together until the pressure hurt. When she was little, Ivo had made that joke about their mother's soup every winter, continuing long after anyone laughed. She remembered his pleasure in the endurance of a bad joke. She had forgotten the exact downward turn of his voice on the last word.

The voice in the deposit had that turn.

There were reasons. A relative. A recording playing in the background. Neurological substitution, in which an old voice attached itself to a recent event. Mara had worked in municipal memory storage for seventeen years. She had corrected experiences that included impossible weather, children older than their parents, a man certain he had died beneath a tram. Memory was an account given by a witness who could not be cross-examined without changing the testimony.

She knew all the reasons. Her hands still shook.

Across the aisle, Pei was distributing the day's collection sleeves. He stopped beside her desk with a paper parcel under one arm.

"You look as though somebody left you a wedding."

"Bad sleep."

"It's the pressure. Weather office says the next surge will make the equinox look polite."

He placed a sleeve on her blotter. Inside its transparent skin, a filament hung like a piece of captured smoke. A label promised thirteen minutes of bereavement from a tram inspector in South Bank. Mara initialed the receipt without looking at it.

"You eating with us?" Pei asked.

"Later."

He considered that answer, then took a small bun from his parcel and set it beside her tea. He had done this often enough that neither of them mentioned it anymore.

When he left, she locked her terminal to a diagnostic screen and lowered the band again.

Six minutes. She could endure six minutes.

The room in the memory had no windows. Its ceiling made a low regular hum. Nera was cold despite something thick around her shoulders. Her right leg wanted to move, but she was trying to keep it still. A woman out of sight asked whether the headaches had become less frequent.

"Depends what you call a headache," Nera said.

Mara experienced the words as intention and vibration together. She was never fully inside another person; the archive translated only certain sensory channels. But the nearness remained unsettling. To remember somebody else's pain was to discover that your own body had available rooms you had never entered.

The man made his joke. Nera looked up.

Mara stopped breathing.

Ivo sat across the table wearing a blue work shirt with the left cuff folded twice. He was older. The hair above his ears had thinned. A pale scar divided one eyebrow, and his beard contained silver. Yet the narrow crooked mouth was his, and so was the way he sat angled away from the chair, as if he might be called to help move something.

He touched his spoon to Nera's bowl.

"They give you any bread?"

"Only if I behave."

"Then we'll starve."

A door opened beyond Nera's shoulder. Ivo's eyes moved. His expression changed so quickly that Mara nearly mistook it for an interruption in the recording.

"Tell your reader," he said softly, "the blue door opens inward."

The memory ended with a bright taste of fear.

Mara remained in her chair. The band had gone cool against her skin. She could hear the archive's ventilation drawing air through the walls and Pei laughing at something near the transfer hatch. She wanted to play the six minutes again immediately, and knew she must not. Repeated access bruised a filament. More than three readings required a preservation order. She had already used two.

She opened the paper ledger instead of the terminal.

Nera's collection number belonged to Saint Brigid's Municipal Recovery House, a clinic on the northern terrace. Its deposits arrived through a medical courier. The clinic had marked the memory for thermal disposal after quality review, an option intended for the most private material. Tomorrow the filament would go into a furnace. Nera would retain the gap where these six minutes had been, but nobody else would possess them.

Except that the intake sheet held a return address.

Mara copied it onto the paper wrapper from Pei's bun. Then she changed the filament's classification to calibration hold, a routine delay requiring only her own signature. She hated how easy that was. Seventeen years of correct procedure had given her the authority to do something wrong without attracting attention.

At noon she took her coat and left by the records entrance.

Bellwether stepped down toward the sea in five broad terraces, each enclosed by a wall. Rainwater rushed through street channels under little iron bridges. On clear days, from the archive steps, she could see the drowned church tower beyond the eastern barrier. Today there was only mist and the low bruised sky.

A public speaker clicked above the tram stop.

"Your contribution protects our tomorrow," said a woman's warm recorded voice. "Voluntary deposits support medical research, flood prediction, and the care of future generations."

Mara had once helped choose those words.

She crossed the square before the message repeated.

Nera Pell lived above a shuttered shoe repair shop. The stairs smelled of damp leather. At the top, a yellow door bore three additional locks and a handwritten notice asking callers to speak loudly before knocking. Mara gave her name. Something scraped inside. The door opened the width of a chain.

"I didn't buy anything," the old woman said.

"I'm from the archive. About yesterday's deposit."

"You people said you wouldn't come here."

The chain tightened as Nera tried to shut the door. Mara put her hand flat against the wood, without pushing.

"There was a man in it. His name is Ivo."

Nera stopped.

For several seconds neither spoke. A pipe knocked somewhere behind the wall. Then Nera unhooked the chain and let Mara into a room packed with upholstered furniture protected by sheets. On the table stood a bowl of soup, untouched and cold.

"I don't remember yesterday," Nera said. "That's what I paid for."

"I know."

"No. You know the mechanics. That's different."

She reached under a cushion and produced a folded piece of paper. Her hand was steady. The writing on the outside read: IF A WOMAN COMES ASKING ABOUT THE SOUP.

Mara recognized the cramped capital letters before Nera unfolded it.

Inside was a map drawn on the back of a meal schedule. A square, a staircase, three arrows. Under them, in the handwriting she had last seen on a birthday card nine years ago, Ivo had written a sentence.

Do not ask the city where I am.


Chapter 2: The Things We Leave Ourselves

Nera put water on to boil because, she said, she refused to conduct a disaster without tea.

Mara sat on the edge of a covered sofa while the old woman worked at the stove. Every flat surface carried a note. CLOSE BOTH TAPS. THE GREEN TABLETS ARE FOR EVENING. LENA CALLS ON THURSDAYS. One note, above a photograph of a young woman holding a fish, simply said: YOU LIKE HER.

"My granddaughter," Nera said, following her glance. "We had a quarrel I no longer possess. I kept the conclusion."

"That's a dangerous way to use relief."

"You sound exactly like the person who approved it."

Mara lowered her eyes to Ivo's map.

"Why did you agree to carry this?"

"I don't know. Yesterday's me had reasons. She might have written those down too, if your brother hadn't used the back of the lunch menu."

Nera set two cups on the table. She moved carefully around a chair whose upholstery had worn through at both arms, giving it the space one gave another person. Mara guessed who had once sat there and did not ask.

"What do you remember about the clinic?"

"Yesterday? Nothing after the tram and before the return journey. Other days, a great deal. Clean floors. Bad food. Young doctors explaining things they haven't experienced. I attend twice a month for peripheral tremor."

"Your intake form said anxiety."

"It generally does. They subsidize the treatment if you donate distressing experiences. Tremor isn't fashionable research. Distress is."

She raised her cup in both hands. The tremor appeared then, slight but unmistakable, making a small tide against the porcelain.

Mara knew that subsidy program. She had written a recommendation praising it in the previous year's internal review. Access to memory relief should not depend on income, she had said. She had never considered what it meant to receive medicine only after finding something painful enough to surrender.

"Did you know my brother before yesterday?"

Nera looked toward the note above the photograph.

"I can't give you that answer. I know I have been to the lower floor. I know there are people who don't leave when the rest of us leave. Once I saw a man being taught to button a shirt. He was about your age. The nurse said he'd had a stroke."

"Did you believe her?"

"I wanted my tablets."

The bluntness made Mara look up. Nera met her eyes without apology.

"People imagine fear is noisy," the old woman said. "Usually it's very considerate. It makes room. It tells you the nurse is busy and you can ask another day."

Mara turned the map. The square might have been the clinic. The steps led down, then west. Ivo had always drawn north as an arrow with two feathers, copying their father's survey charts. On this map the arrow pointed toward the bottom of the page.

She rotated the paper.

Suddenly the shape became familiar: the old customs house by the eastern wall, its courtyard cut diagonally by a service channel. Their mother had cleaned offices there. Mara and Ivo used to wait on the back stairs after school, racing bottle caps along the channel until she finished. The map did not lead into Saint Brigid's. It led under the customs house.

"Can I take this?"

"That's why it exists."

"Someone may come asking whether I was here."

Nera sipped her tea. "Someone already did."

Mara felt the cup become heavy in her hands.

"When?"

"This morning. Man with a municipal badge. He asked if I had brought home clinic property. I said I steal biscuits, mostly. He looked in my kitchen anyway."

"What did you tell him about the note?"

"Nothing. I hadn't found it yet. I put it somewhere safe yesterday. That tends to make a thing disappear for several days."

She smiled, but it did not last.

Mara stood. She wanted to give useful advice, a phone number, instructions. Every possibility passed through a city office. Instead she asked whether Nera could stay with Lena for a few nights.

"If I decide I want to. You must stop explaining my life to me before you've finished your tea."

Mara sat down again.

They drank in silence. A tram bell sounded through the rain. By the time Mara left, Nera had agreed to telephone her granddaughter, and Mara had agreed not to pretend that this was a rescue.

Outside, the street had acquired the attentive emptiness of an afternoon when most people were working. She walked uphill, away from the customs house. In a bakery window she checked the reflection behind her. An umbrella. A woman carrying vegetables. Nobody who resembled a municipal investigator, which meant nothing. Investigators had vegetables too.

She went home first.

Her apartment stood on the third terrace, in a building whose foundations had been raised twice. The rooms were narrow and expensive. On the kitchen wall hung a photograph of Ivo at twenty-eight, squinting into sunlight beside a half-repaired pump. She had selected it for the memorial because he looked happy. Afterward she had begun to resent its happiness, the permanent assertion that she ought to remember him that way.

The man in Nera's memory would be forty-three now. Almost forty-four.

Mara took the photograph down. Behind it, tucked beneath the backing, was a paper memorial certificate. IVO VALE. LOST IN SERVICE DURING THE EASTERN EVACUATION. PRESUMED DECEASED. She had read those words so many times that she no longer normally saw the word presumed.

No body. No grave. A municipal payment she had used to settle their mother's debts.

She called the one person she knew who had searched the eastern ruins for bodies.

Tamsin Rook answered on the sixth ring, with wind hammering the receiver.

"If it's about the invoices, the harbor office already has them."

"It's Mara Vale."

The wind filled the pause.

"Well," Tamsin said. "That took nine years."

They had not parted kindly. During the evacuation, Tamsin had brought seventeen survivors through a gate the city ordered shut. Mara, temporarily assigned to casualty registration, had refused to list the names until they could be verified. One woman died before verification. Tamsin had never forgiven the delay. Mara had never found a defense that did not sound worse when spoken aloud.

"I need to get beneath the old customs house."

"Take a shovel."

"I think Ivo is alive."

The reply did not come quickly. Mara listened to a rope striking something hollow at the other end.

"Do you think that," Tamsin finally asked, "or do you have a reason?"

"I saw him in a memory recorded yesterday. He sent a map."

"Don't send it over the line."

"I wasn't going to."

"You work for people who put their whole brains in filing cabinets. I don't assume good judgment. Meet me at the fish stairs in forty minutes. Bring boots."

The call ended.

Mara packed a torch, a contact reader, and the little mechanical recorder her father had used for field notes. The recorder took wax-coated steel wire. It could not connect to anything. Its batteries were nearly dead, but the crank on the side still turned.

Then she opened the cupboard above her bed.

Ivo's remaining possessions occupied one box: a scarf, three notebooks, a socket wrench, a tin containing buttons nobody had needed in a decade. Underneath lay the last card he had sent, a crude drawing of Mara buried beneath papers. Happy birthday to the Ministry of My Sister, he had written. Please authorize one drink.

She had been too busy that evening. They had planned to meet the following week.

Mara put the card back and shut the box. She would not carry him as a relic into a place where he might still be a person.

At the door she hesitated, then wrote a note on the back of an old utility bill.

You saw him. The recording was real. Nera Pell knows.

She placed it inside her left boot, beneath the insole, and went out into the rain.


Chapter 3: A Boat with Two Names

The boat said MERCY on its port side and TRY ASKING on its starboard. Tamsin had registered it under the first name and apparently considered the second an operating principle.

It lay below the fish stairs between a dredger and a floating vegetable stall. Rain tapped the wheelhouse roof. Tamsin stood on the aft deck in a red waterproof coat, coiling a rope with the deliberate economy of someone whose joints charged for every unnecessary movement. A streak of white ran through her dark hair. Mara remembered her with both eyebrows; now a scar occupied most of the left one.

"Map," Tamsin said.

Mara climbed aboard and handed it over.

Tamsin looked at it, then at her. "Who else has seen this?"

"Nera Pell. Perhaps someone at the clinic."

"Perhaps. A beautiful word. You can drown a whole crew in it."

She took the paper into the wheelhouse. Inside were two seats, a kettle restrained by a wire bracket, and so many paper charts that the windows seemed almost decorative. A boy of about sixteen lay on a narrow bench reading a repair manual.

"Off," Tamsin told him.

"I just got dry."

"An experience to treasure. Go home."

He marked his place with a length of solder and stood. He gave Mara a curious look but asked no questions. When he had stepped ashore, Tamsin cast off.

"Apprentice?" Mara asked.

"Nephew. The distinction mostly affects how much his mother complains."

The engine settled into an uneven knock. Mercy passed beneath the market bridge and entered a channel between warehouses. Above them, people hurried along covered walkways, their faces appearing briefly between the posts. The city smelled of fuel, fish scales, and wet stone.

Tamsin set the map beside the compass.

"This entrance is below high water. We have seventy minutes before the turn. Forty if the weather office is lying, which is its principal tradition."

"You recognize it?"

"Customs refrigeration intake. I've salvaged three compressors from there. The fourth is holding up a wall."

"There should be a blue door."

"There are doors of every color down there. Mostly horizontal."

Mara wanted to explain the memory, the voice, the spoon. Instead she held the rail while the boat scraped past a fender and realized that she had expected Tamsin to make belief easier. Tamsin was making logistics possible. It was a more useful kindness.

They tied up inside the shadow of the eastern barrier. Through a maintenance opening, Mara could see the upper stones of the customs house on the other side, dark with spray. She had forgotten how close the old streets were. From the upper terraces, the ruins looked like a distant country. Here, a person could throw a stone into her childhood.

Tamsin lowered a ladder over the bow.

"Three rules. Step where I step. If I say turn around, turn around. If you hear someone calling from deeper in, don't follow the voice. Sound travels through the drains."

Mara nodded.

"Say them back."

She did. Tamsin looked satisfied in a grudging way and went down first.

The intake tunnel smelled of old salt and warm machinery. That warmth was the first surprise. The customs house had been officially abandoned since the flood. Yet somewhere beneath it, equipment was working hard enough to heat the walls.

They followed a dry ledge beside flowing water. Tamsin's torch found numbered pipes, a rusted trolley, a child's plastic shoe lodged in a grille. Mara kept her eyes on the captain's boots. When she looked away, the dark offered her too many possible rooms.

At the first junction, Tamsin stopped.

Someone had swept the floor.

The clean passage led toward a grey steel door with a new keypad. Beside it stood a white cylinder marked MEDICAL GAS. No dust covered the cylinder. The wheels beneath its trolley were wet.

Mara unfolded the map. "Not that way."

Ivo's arrow pointed toward a narrower corridor half blocked by fallen brick. They climbed over it and reached a wooden door painted blue. Its bottom edge was swollen against the floor. Mara pulled the handle, then stopped herself.

The blue door opens inward.

She pushed with her shoulder. The wood gave abruptly, and she stumbled into a little room that smelled of oilcloth.

It had once been a watchman's cupboard. A cracked mirror hung above a dry sink. On a shelf sat a sealed food tin, a hand lamp, and a pair of work gloves. Behind the mirror, taped carefully to the wall, was another envelope.

MARA, it said.

Her name in that cramped hand made her angrier than the map had. He had been close enough to write envelopes. He had been alive through nine birthdays, through their mother's last year, through mornings when Mara could not get out of bed. He had put a letter in a wall.

She tore it open.

There were two sheets and a small transparent strip. The strip carried the iridescent weave of a memory filament, but thicker than the archive kind, reinforced along both edges.

The letter began: If this worked, you are furious. Good. Stay that way until you have read everything.

She sat on a broken crate.

I am under the eastern pumping station, where the old storm cistern joins the clinics. There are sixty-four of us on the current roster. I do not know how many there were before. Some came willingly. That sentence will be used to excuse the next nine years. Don't let it.

The second paragraph sloped down the page.

I tried to send messages before. They review our outbound language. This room is outside the serviced perimeter, but I can reach it only during mechanical assignments. Nera offered to carry a recording through medical relief because its privacy classification prevents routine content inspection. She knew the risk. Please don't treat her like she wandered into this by mistake.

Mara pressed the letter flat against her knee.

The strip is a proof of location and a roster checksum. It needs an old registry reader, not an archive terminal. Find Jonas Sen. He was dismissed after the hearing. He kept the original authorization book. I don't know whether he'll help you. Tell him I still count the steps.

At the bottom, in smaller letters, Ivo had added: You signed something. So did I. Neither of us signed this.

Tamsin had been watching the passage. Now she turned.

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know."

The answer frightened Mara more than anything else in the letter. She remembered the flood in pieces. Working at a registration desk. The smell of damp blankets. Ivo leaving to repair a gate actuator. Later, Director Venn offering her clinical relief for the worst hours. Mara had agreed. She had retained facts. She had deliberately given up experience.

Facts were supposed to be sufficient.

Beyond the door, metal struck metal.

Tamsin extinguished her torch and caught Mara's wrist. A white beam moved across the junction outside. Two people were talking, their words indistinct under the water noise. One wore hard-soled shoes. The other pushed something with a squeaking wheel.

"Hold it there," a man said. "He's wandering again."

Another voice answered, mild and exhausted. "I'm looking for the stairs."

Mara knew that voice too, but it was not Ivo's. She had heard it every morning for years, announcing the tide over the public speakers. The words used a different rhythm now. They belonged to someone cold and lost.

Tamsin's grip tightened before Mara moved.

A door closed. The trolley went away.

They waited until the water was the loudest thing again. Then Tamsin leaned close enough that her breath touched Mara's ear.

"Bring the whole envelope. We leave now."

They reached Mercy with twelve minutes before the turn. Mara's boots were full of water. As Tamsin started the engine, the public speaker above the maintenance opening crackled.

"The eastern approaches remain closed for your safety," the familiar voice announced.

Mara listened to its perfect composure and thought of the man in the passage searching for stairs.


Chapter 4: The Clerk Who Kept the Book

Jonas Sen repaired clocks in a room above a betting shop. The sign on his door promised reasonable rates and made no claims about punctuality. Three clocks visible through the glass showed different times; a fourth had no hands.

He opened the door before Mara knocked.

"I don't service municipal equipment."

"I'm not here for the archive."

"Your coat disagrees."

Mara looked down. She had forgotten the small stitched emblem on its breast: a cupped hand holding a star. After seventeen years, the uniform had become a kind of weather she stopped noticing.

Tamsin stood behind her on the stairs. "Can we do this indoors? There's a man downstairs trying to explain a very profitable horse to me."

Jonas glanced at Tamsin, then back at Mara. His hair was close-cropped and his shirt carefully ironed. He looked younger than Mara expected, until he moved his right arm and she saw the stiffness that changed the movement into several separate decisions.

"Who sent you?"

"Ivo Vale. He still counts the steps."

Jonas closed his eyes.

Then he let them in.

The workshop was crowded but exact. Tiny gears lay in shallow trays, arranged by size. A magnifying lens hung over a bench. Near the window, a kettle sat on a hotplate beside one cup and a jar of instant coffee. There were no photographs.

Mara handed him the strip. He did not touch it immediately.

"Where did you get this?"

She told him. He listened without interruption, then slid open a drawer beneath the bench and took out a reader with a cracked cream casing. The device had a slot for registry strips and no network socket. Jonas connected two loose leads to a battery.

"Ivo shouldn't have been able to remove this."

"He's good at fixing things."

"That was always the problem."

The reader's screen filled with numbers. Jonas wrote some down, entered a second sequence, and waited while the cooling fan complained. A roster appeared: identification numbers, load assignments, medical codes. Most names were abbreviated. One entry read VALE, I. ACTIVE. CHAMBER 4. COGNITIVE DUTY: 19.2 HRS.

Mara put both hands on the edge of the bench.

"Nineteen hours a day?"

"It used to be twelve."

"Doing what?"

Jonas did not answer until he had unplugged the reader.

"Predicting water."

Outside, a tram passed with a clatter that seemed too ordinary for the room.

"The archive trains forecasting systems from human experience," Mara said. "Everyone knows that. Recognition of risk, crowd movement, local knowledge. The memories are processed. They aren't people."

"The ordinary forecasting systems, yes. But flood behavior became unstable after the estuary changes. Too many interacting failures. The old models could describe a disaster afterward beautifully. During one, they were poor company. The Continuity project connected living brains to the forecasts. People who knew pumps, streets, hospitals, tides. They were very good at noticing what didn't fit."

Tamsin leaned against the door. "How good?"

"Good enough that the council decided they couldn't stop."

Jonas went to the wall behind a rack of clock cases. He lifted away a narrow panel and withdrew a thick book wrapped in oilcloth. Its red spine bore the municipal seal and a faded handwritten title: EMERGENCY HUMAN SYSTEMS AUTHORIZATIONS.

Mara recognized the format. She had numbered books like this as a junior clerk.

"There was a hearing," she said. "After the flood. An inquiry into missing emergency personnel."

"There was an announcement of a hearing. Those are different products."

He opened the book near the middle. The page carried six signatures and a block of emergency legislation. Mara found her own name before she read anything else.

MARA VALE, DEPUTY CIVIL REGISTRAR, TEMPORARY APPOINTMENT.

Beside it, Ivo's signature crossed its own lines in the familiar impatient way. The authorization allowed seventy-two hours of neural assistance, renewable only by direct consent after a minimum recovery period. The signatory registrar certified that each participant had been informed of the risks and could withdraw.

Under the printed wording was a handwritten note in Mara's script.

No extension by proxy. Family relationship does not constitute consent.

She touched the edge of the page. The handwriting was hers. The memory of writing it was absent.

"I insisted on that," she said.

"You did."

"And I forgot."

Jonas looked at her for a long time. "You surrendered a block of traumatic experience four months later. I don't know exactly what was removed. I was already under disciplinary review."

"Why didn't you come to me?"

"I did. Twice. You reported the second visit to employee welfare."

Mara stepped away from the book.

She remembered a man outside the archive. A complaint she had found confused and distressing. A supervisor saying that some former staff had become fixated on the disaster. She had accepted that explanation gratefully. It let her remain a person who had suffered, without becoming a person who had to do something.

"I thought you needed help."

"I did."

Tamsin crossed the room and examined the page without touching it. "You kept an original. Why haven't you shown it to anyone useful?"

"I showed it to a magistrate. He issued a preservation order. The following week his medical records were leaked. He had been receiving relief for thoughts about harming himself. His competence was challenged. The order was stayed. After that, people stopped returning my calls."

"So you repair clocks."

"Clocks admit when something is broken."

Mara hated the neatness of the line, then saw the shame in his face and understood that he hated it too. It was something he told strangers. It made defeat sound like a preference.

He turned several pages. Renewal certificates filled the book's latter section. They were printed on different paper, with an authorization code that referred to a regulation passed after the emergency. PROTECTIVE CONTINUATION IN CASES OF COMPROMISED CAPACITY.

"They exhausted the participants until the doctors said they lacked the capacity to withdraw," Jonas said. "Then they appointed guardians to approve continued participation."

"Who were the guardians?"

"The department."

For a moment Mara could hear only the workshop clocks, all those mechanisms disagreeing in tiny measured ways.

She took out her father's wire recorder. "Tell me again. From the beginning."

Jonas stared at the machine. "You think a recording will protect you?"

"No. I think it will help me remember accurately."

He looked at her differently then. It was not forgiveness, or trust. It was perhaps the recognition of a tool being used for its proper purpose.

He sat down. Mara turned the crank until the spring held. Tamsin drew the curtains, leaving a narrow opening through which she could watch the street.

Jonas began with the date of the first storm, and the number of people who had walked downstairs believing they would come back after three days.


Chapter 5: The Director's Garden

Director Ada Venn sent for Mara the following morning at nine. The invitation arrived on paper, carried to her desk by a woman who waited while Mara opened it. There was no possibility of pretending she had not seen it.

Venn's office occupied the former botanical house on the highest terrace. The council had retained the trees when it converted the building, so visitors walked past lemon branches on their way to discussions about drainage bonds. Mara had always liked that absurdity. Now she wondered how much public trust had been purchased with the smell of leaves.

The director was pruning a small tree when Mara entered. She wore a dark skirt, an old cardigan, and gardening gloves with the fingertips cut away. At sixty-eight she had the upright bearing of someone who had learned long ago that tiredness could be used against her.

"Thank you for coming," Venn said.

"Your assistant made the alternatives seem theoretical."

Venn put down the shears. "She has a gift for that. Sit."

There were two chairs beside a low table. Neither stood behind a desk. Another carefully chosen kindness, Mara thought, and immediately disliked herself for once having admired it.

"You placed a clinical deposit on calibration hold yesterday," Venn said. "Then you left before the end of your shift."

"Am I being disciplined?"

"That depends on what happened."

"I saw my brother."

She had considered approaching the subject indirectly. All the way up the hill she had composed careful questions. Sitting beneath the lemon tree, she discovered that she could not bear another polite layer.

Venn did not look surprised. She removed her gloves finger by finger.

"I hoped he would choose another way."

Something cold moved through Mara's chest. Until this moment, she had retained a narrow possibility that Jonas was wrong, that the roster was outdated, that the city had not knowingly kept Ivo from her. Venn's sentence closed it.

"You told me he was dead."

"The civil register listed him as missing."

"You attended his memorial."

Venn folded the gloves. "Yes."

Mara stood, then remained standing because sitting again would require a decision.

"My mother asked you whether he suffered. You held her hand."

"I remember."

"Did you tell him when she died?"

Venn looked toward the glass wall. A gardener outside was dragging a hose between rows of winter greens.

"He was informed."

Mara had not understood until then that anger could make the hands feel empty. She wanted an object to break and hated the room for offering only fragile living things.

"I want to see him."

"That can be arranged."

"Today."

"With a medical liaison, yes. But first you need to understand what he does."

Venn touched a control on the table. A model of Bellwether appeared on the wall: terraces, gates, canals, the pale spread of the estuary. Blue lines crawled through the lower streets. The model shifted, and those streets filled with red.

"This is the forecast without Continuity during the last major surge. Thirty-one probable breaches, twelve thousand displaced, between eight hundred and two thousand dead. Here is the actual outcome."

The red retreated. Four buildings remained marked.

"Eleven injuries," Venn said. "No fatalities. Your brother recognized a resonant failure in Gate Six from a pump sound. The automated models missed it because the pressure readings were normal."

"Then pay him. Let him go home."

"Some participants cannot sustain ordinary consciousness after prolonged integration. Their sense of sequence has altered. They experience an empty room as a loss of limbs. We are treating that."

"After causing it."

"Yes."

The admission disrupted Mara more than a denial would have. Venn continued before she recovered.

"The first extensions were indefensible. I approved them. I expected weeks, not years. Each month there was a reason to postpone separation. A storm, a failed replacement model, a patient who deteriorated when removed. None of those reasons vanished because the original decision was wrong."

"You keep making it. Every morning."

"And every morning there are seventy thousand people behind those walls."

Mara looked at the model. She recognized her apartment, Nera's street, the school where she and Ivo had learned arithmetic while rain rattled on a tin roof. It was a cruel diagram because it was accurate. Those people existed. Their dependence existed. Venn had built a prison around both facts.

"You're asking me to help conceal it."

"I'm offering you a place on the transition committee. Full access to your brother. Independent review of care. Resources you won't obtain by climbing through drains with Tamsin Rook."

There it was: the quiet announcement that Mara had been watched.

"How long would the transition take?"

"Our current estimate is eighteen months."

"What was the estimate nine years ago?"

Venn's mouth tightened. "I won't insult you with another answer."

Mara sat down again, because her knees had begun to shake.

"I want the original consent documents and my own relief record."

"Your relief record is private."

"It is mine."

"The clinical content was destroyed under your instructions. The summary remains."

Mara had known that might be true. Hearing it made the missing hours feel newly stolen, although she had authorized their destruction. She could not decide where her own decision ended and the institution's convenience began.

Venn slid a folder across the table. Inside lay a proposed confidentiality agreement and a visitor's pass dated that afternoon. Mara read the agreement. It restricted disclosure of identities, technical details, medical conditions, and any information that might undermine essential public confidence. It would make even telling Nera what she had found a disciplinary offense.

She left the pen where it lay.

"I need time."

"You have until tomorrow morning. The coming surge will require every available attention. I cannot let this become a distraction."

"My brother is a person."

"I know his name, Mara. I know all their names."

That was perhaps the worst thing Venn said. She did know. The harm had survived knowing.

Outside, Mara stopped beneath the arcade and tried to slow her breathing. A message had arrived on her wrist terminal: her archive access was suspended pending a health review. The suspension was described as supportive. Her salary would continue.

She turned the terminal off.

At the bottom of the garden steps, a school group was lining up around a teacher with a yellow flag. The children had come to see the old trees. One boy was carrying a lemon that had fallen from a branch. He held it out to Mara as she passed, proud of its size.

She smiled because he deserved a smile, then walked until the botanical house was hidden by roofs.

Tamsin was waiting at a tea stall two streets away. Mara had told her the appointment time, and Tamsin had said only that public buildings usually had back doors.

"Well?"

"She offered me access if I sign away the right to tell anyone."

"You could sign. Then tell people anyway."

"She would move him before I got him out."

"Possibly. But don't confuse refusing paper with saving a man."

Mara accepted the criticism. She needed it. Her indignation was beginning to feel like work, and it was not work yet.

"She says some of them can't leave safely."

Tamsin stirred her tea. "Then we need someone who knows how to leave safely."

Across the street, a public clock advanced with a small mechanical shudder. Mara thought of Jonas's workshop, of the roster's medical codes, and of the old woman who knew the lower floor better than either of them.

"We need to ask the patients," she said.


Chapter 6: A Place at the Table

Nera had gone to Lena's, but only after leaving a complaint taped to her own door. It informed municipal visitors that entering without permission would damage the lock and that the lock had cost more than their manners were worth.

Lena lived above a bakery on the second terrace with her husband, two daughters, and an elderly dog that greeted every visitor as an accusation. Nera opened the door holding a rolling pin. Behind her, one granddaughter was cutting pastry into shapes that suggested a personal dispute with geometry.

"I told you I would call when I knew something," Nera said.

"We know something now," Mara replied.

The rolling pin lowered.

They sat around the kitchen table after Lena took the children downstairs. Tamsin occupied the chair nearest the landing. Jonas arrived ten minutes later carrying the authorization book in a shopping bag. He apologized for being late; he had changed trams twice to see whether anyone followed him.

"Did they?" Tamsin asked.

"I don't know."

"Good answer. Keep using it when appropriate."

Mara described Venn's admission. She did not soften the risks of disconnection or the threat from the storm. Nera listened with her arms folded, watching Jonas rather than Mara.

"Were you one of them?" she asked when Mara finished.

Jonas looked down at his right hand.

"For eleven days. I was part of an early administrative trial. They wanted to know whether registrars could integrate population records with evacuation forecasts. I had seizures. They removed me before the main emergency."

"And afterward you kept their books."

"Yes."

He sounded as if he had been waiting years for someone to arrange those facts in that order.

"What does leaving feel like?" Mara asked.

Jonas rolled the handle of the shopping bag between his fingers. "The first morning, I could hear a tram coming three streets away. Not with my ears. I knew its weight, where the rails were worn, how many people waited at the next stop. Then the knowledge disappeared. I vomited because I couldn't understand how something so large had been taken out of me."

"Did it get better?"

"It became different. I still dream in districts sometimes. The neurologist said that was poetic language. I stopped seeing her."

Nera reached for the tea without looking away from him. "There's a nurse at Saint Brigid's. Elian Doss. He leaves questions unfinished when the cameras can hear. Last month he asked whether I ever felt my relief was becoming compulsory. Then another nurse came in and he began talking about the weather."

"Can you contact him?"

"I have a treatment appointment tomorrow."

"You can't go back there," Mara said.

Nera set down her cup hard enough to spill it.

"That is the second time you've confused concern with authority."

Mara swallowed her next sentence. The kitchen smelled of warm dough. Downstairs, a child laughed with the reckless confidence of somebody who expected adults to manage the world.

"I'm frightened for you," Mara said instead.

"So am I. We can both be frightened while I decide."

They worked out a message Nera could deliver without using names. Jonas supplied three medical terms from the roster: sequential grounding, low-load separation, home-context response. If Doss understood them, he might be willing to speak. If he did not, Nera would ask about her tremor and come home.

There would be no request to surrender the meeting afterward. Mara insisted on that, and Nera agreed, with a look that made clear whose agreement mattered.

While Jonas and Nera examined the clinic schedule, Mara went onto the narrow balcony. Tamsin followed. Below them, the bakery queue curled along the pavement. People were buying extra flour ahead of the storm warning. A man had brought a sack in a baby carriage; his child sat on his shoulders eating the corner of a roll.

"You were right yesterday," Mara said.

"Be more specific. I like to collect these occasions."

"About the registration delay. During the evacuation. I knew the rule was hurting people. I hid inside it."

Tamsin did not answer immediately. She watched a delivery cart negotiate the crowd below.

"The woman who died was called Rhea Dall. She taught music. She had insulin in a bag we lost when the boat struck the barrier. We needed the hospital to dispense more. They wanted an identity confirmation."

"I remember."

"Do you? Or do you remember the complaint?"

Mara opened her mouth and closed it.

Tamsin nodded once. "That's what I thought."

"I remember a woman under a blanket. I remember telling the hospital I couldn't confirm the name."

"Rhea wasn't asking you to solve fraud. She was asking to remain alive while you solved it."

The street below blurred. Mara gripped the balcony rail until a flake of rust broke beneath her thumb. She had imagined apologizing as the end of a hard conversation. Instead it had opened the door to the actual conversation, which offered no convenient ending.

"I won't ask you to forgive me," she said.

"Good. I wouldn't know what to do with that. I'm not Rhea."

For a while they stood together without speaking.

Then Tamsin said, "I brought a boat through a gate with seventeen people aboard. Eighteen had reached the quay. I could hear the last one running, but the water was turning us broadside. I cut the rope."

Mara looked at her.

"I don't know his name," Tamsin said. "I remember his boots. Every time people tell the story about my heroic little boat, I remember the boots."

"You couldn't have waited."

"You don't know that. Neither do I. That's what living with a thing looks like."

Inside, Nera called for more hot water. Tamsin left the balcony first.

That evening Mara returned to the apartment she now understood had never been entirely private. Nothing appeared disturbed. Her book lay open where she had left it. The cup in the sink carried the same tea stain. But the cupboard above her bed stood a finger's width from the frame.

She had closed it firmly.

Mara did not open the cupboard. She backed into the hall and stood with her keys pressed between her fingers, listening. No movement came from the rooms. Whoever had visited had wanted her to notice only after enough hesitation to doubt herself.

She left with the overnight bag she had packed that morning.

At Jonas's workshop, Tamsin had spread a city chart across the floor. Four circles marked the old manual gate stations. Two were still accessible by water. One lay beneath a shopping arcade. The fourth had been converted into a municipal exhibit called The Age of Floods.

"I asked around," she said. "There are still people who know how the gates worked before Continuity. Most of them have been paid to retire."

"Can they operate them again?"

"If the machinery hasn't been removed. If the channels are clear. If someone can tell us how much time we have."

Mara put down her bag. She understood now why Tamsin had begun drawing circles while Mara went home to stare at a cupboard. Saving Ivo required a place for him to arrive. Saving the people beneath the city required a city capable of releasing them.

She knelt beside the chart.

"Tell me which records you need."

Outside, the rain stopped. In the sudden quiet, every clock in the workshop could be heard at once. None struck the hour together.


Chapter 7: The Lower Ward

Elian Doss came to the workshop carrying a box of pastries. He had bought them across the street because, he explained, people noticed a nurse visiting a stranger's house less when he seemed to be bringing breakfast. The box contained nothing but plain rolls. He had forgotten to choose.

He was thin, perhaps thirty, with a healed burn on the side of his neck. Nera followed him up the stairs, breathing hard and objecting to the height of every riser. She had made him accompany her directly from the clinic. Trust, she said, would not improve with the opportunity to reconsider.

"I can get one person inside," Doss told them. "There's a vacant attendant position on the afternoon transfer. Not a real vacancy. I've made it look like one. You would have ninety minutes before the next shift verifies the roster."

"Why now?" Jonas asked.

Doss looked at the covered authorization book on the bench. "Because yesterday a patient asked me to remember the color of his daughter's eyes. He could remember that he loved her. He couldn't find her face. I said I would look in his file. That was the answer I gave him."

He rubbed both palms against his trousers.

"I have worked there four years. I could give you a reason from any of those years. I don't know why this one finally became enough."

Mara did not demand a cleaner explanation. She had none for herself.

They spent half an hour learning the transfer routine. Mara would enter through the clinic loading bay in a grey attendant's coat, pushing a trolley of linen. Her borrowed badge would identify her as a contract employee from an agency whose office had closed the previous winter. Doss had retained its credentials because the payroll system had not noticed. A small administrative neglect, turned briefly toward mercy.

Tamsin disliked everything about the plan. She disliked it most because she could offer no quicker alternative. Before Mara left, she tucked a small waterproof beacon into the seam of the coat.

"Press it twice if you need us. It won't tell us how to help. It will only tell us where you made the mistake."

At Saint Brigid's, the loading bay smelled of disinfectant and overripe fruit. A driver was arguing about a missing signature. Doss pushed Mara's trolley past him with the irritated confidence of somebody who had already completed too much of a shift. Nobody asked for her face. At the lift, a guard scanned the badge and glanced at the folded sheets.

"Any pillows?"

"Tomorrow," Doss said.

"They keep saying tomorrow."

The guard stepped aside.

The lift descended farther than Mara expected. A vibration entered through the wheels of the trolley, passed into her hands, and settled somewhere behind her teeth. When the doors opened, warm damp air met them. Pipes crowded the ceiling. The corridor was painted a soft green that might have been chosen from a brochure about recovery.

There were pictures on the walls. Boats. Gardens. A mountain lake. None showed Bellwether.

Doss led her through a door marked HYDROLOGICAL REHABILITATION. Beyond it, a long ward curved around the interior of an enormous cylindrical chamber. Beds stood behind glass partitions. Between them ran thick bundles of translucent tubing and cables. Each bed had a chair beside it. Most chairs were empty.

Mara had imagined restraints. Instead she saw slippers, drinking cups, a paperback lying open on a blanket. The ordinariness made it harder to understand the cables rising from the padded collars around the patients' necks.

A woman in the nearest bed was knitting with no yarn. Her fingers moved patiently around invisible needles.

"Motor rehearsal," Doss murmured. "She used to maintain the western sluices. We encourage familiar sequences during low load."

The woman looked at Mara. "You're carrying too much water in your left pocket."

Mara stopped.

"She means the trolley," Doss said. "The laundry load is uneven."

He moved two folded sheets to the other side. The woman relaxed and returned to her knitting.

At the next partition, a man with white hair sat on the floor arranging buttons into streets. He had made a remarkably accurate version of the second terrace. Where Lena's bakery should have been, he had placed a chipped blue button twice the size of its neighbors.

"Is that his map?" Mara whispered.

"That is how he asks us to check a blocked drain."

Doss spoke to an aide, who wrote something down and disappeared through a service door. Mara watched the man's hand hover over the blue button. His lips moved, but no sound emerged.

The corridor continued around the chamber. Through its central glass wall, Mara saw levels below them: more beds, equipment platforms, a column of falling water enclosed in a clear pipe. The whole structure seemed suspended inside the old cistern. She had passed above it on trams for most of her life.

"How many can walk?" she asked.

"Forty-one with assistance. Nine independently. Fourteen need full transfer support. Walking is not the main problem."

"Separation."

"And what comes after. Some have no home to return to. Some families were told they died. Some families know and accept a stipend. Please don't imagine every door outside opens kindly."

He said it without defending the ward. It was another practical fact, and Mara made herself retain it.

They stopped beside a small recreation room. Two patients sat at a table with a chessboard between them. All the pieces were pawns. One of the players, a broad woman with close-cropped silver hair, looked up.

"You have the same mouth," she said.

Doss stiffened.

"I'm not announcing it," the woman added. "I have other registers."

Mara knew her voice. Without the public system's smoothing filters, it had a rough edge and an unexpected musical rise.

"You're the tide announcer."

"I was a singer once. Sella Orr. They discovered I could hear pressure changes in the machinery. Now I tell everyone to stay indoors. A narrower repertoire."

The other player slid a pawn sideways and took three of hers.

"He cheats," Sella said. "It's the healthiest thing about him."

Doss put a hand on Mara's elbow. They had already spent twenty minutes. She wanted to stay and ask Sella every question she had never thought to ask the voice above her street. Instead she nodded and continued.

Ivo was in a room near the far end of the ward.

He stood at a sink washing a metal cup. For a moment Mara saw only his back: narrow shoulders, blue shirt, a collar leading to a cable in the wall. His hair was longer than in the memory. He had tied it carelessly with a strip of cloth.

"Ivo," Doss said. "Your visitor."

He turned.

Mara had thought she might run to him. She had thought she might slap him, or say his name until it became a normal word again. Instead she stood beside the trolley, unable to cross the distance between the brother she had lost and the man whose face was waiting to learn whether she recognized him.

"You got the map," he said.

She nodded.

"I thought the arrows might be bad."

"They were always bad."

His mouth moved toward a smile. Then he looked away, and she saw that he was crying without making a sound.

Mara crossed the room. She put her arms around him carefully, afraid of the cable. He felt thinner than he looked. His shirt smelled of clean linen and a faint electrical heat. For a little while he held her with the unmistakable weight of a living person.

Then his arms loosened.

He looked over her shoulder at something she could not see.

"Gate Three," he said. "The lower bearing is walking. Tell them not to trust the green indicator."

Doss went to the wall and pressed a call button.

Mara remained inside the circle of her brother's arms while the city borrowed him again.


Chapter 8: The Shape of a Brother

Ivo apologized when he returned to the room. That was how Mara experienced it: his eyes finding hers again, his hands remembering the embrace after the rest of him had gone elsewhere.

"It's usually less rude," he said.

Doss brought chairs. He told them they had forty-three minutes, then stayed outside the glass partition with a chart held open in front of him. Mara suspected he was reading none of it.

Ivo sat with his knees apart and his hands resting on them. The cable from his collar had enough slack to reach the bed, the table, and the sink. A line of discoloration on the floor showed where it had once been shorter.

"I want to take you home," Mara said.

"Which home?"

"Mine. Anywhere."

"I wasn't being difficult. I don't know what's still there."

She told him about the apartment, about the old market converted into a covered schoolyard, about their mother's building demolished after the foundations shifted. He listened with a concentration that made each ordinary fact feel consequential. When she mentioned the bakery near the south tram loop, he interrupted.

"They still make the little black cakes?"

"Yes."

"Good. I couldn't tell if I was remembering them or inventing them."

Mara wanted to promise him a box. She stopped herself before she turned food into proof that everything could be repaired.

"Why didn't you leave when you went to the customs house?"

He looked down at his hands. "The mechanical assignments happen during low load. I have a portable support unit. About forty minutes. After that the confusion starts. I tried once. I got as far as the barrier stairs and forgot which side of the water I was supposed to be on. They found me trying to climb into a drain."

"Doss says there are separation treatments."

"There are. The treatments take days. Days mean replacing our work with something else. They always need us for one more tide."

He rubbed at the skin beneath his collar. Mara saw small scars along the edge.

"What is it like when you're connected?"

"Hard to describe without sounding important. Most of it is boring. You feel a problem before you know where it is. Then you search. Like hearing a tap running in a house with a thousand rooms. Sometimes someone else finds it. Sometimes you argue about whether it's a tap."

"Someone else?"

"The others. We don't hear full thoughts. More like attention moving. Sella hears strain. Anil sees routes. I notice sequences that don't complete. A pump starts, a valve answers, a pressure wave should follow. If it doesn't, I feel the missing step."

"And the archive memories?"

"Local knowledge. Thousands of borrowed afternoons. Where a shopkeeper stored sandbags. Which stair sticks in damp weather. We use them as context. Sometimes I remember things that happened to people I've never met. Usually I know the difference."

Usually sat between them.

Mara asked about the original emergency. Ivo's gaze moved to the glass, checking Doss's position.

"You told me not to volunteer."

"I saw the form. I added a restriction."

"Before that. You said I'd already worked thirty hours and someone else could do it. I said there wasn't anyone else. You called me arrogant. We had a proper fight in front of six very frightened clerks."

Mara tried to find it in herself. She could imagine the scene too well, but imagination was not recovery. She had to resist accepting a plausible picture as evidence.

"Did I apologize?"

"No. You made the doctor explain the procedure again. Then you wrote your sentence."

"No extension by proxy."

He nodded. "I trusted the sentence. I thought if you'd written it, somebody would have to listen."

She covered her mouth with her hand. Ivo reached across the table, then stopped before touching her, as if he had forgotten whether comfort was permitted.

"You didn't know," he said.

"I let them take the hours in which I did know."

"You were ill."

"I was useful when I stopped asking. Both can be true."

They sat quietly. In the corridor, someone laughed, then coughed. A trolley wheel squeaked. Mara thought of nine years measured in these sounds, and knew she could not comprehend them by concentrating harder.

"Mother," Ivo said.

Mara looked up.

"Did she ask for me?"

She had feared that question from the moment she saw his face in Nera's memory. She could offer a merciful simplification. Their mother had been sedated at the end; that was true. She had also asked, several times, whether the boat had come back.

"Yes," Mara said. "She asked whether you were still working. I told her you were safe."

Ivo closed his eyes.

"She wasn't alone," Mara added. "I was there. Her friend Bess came every morning. She complained about the hospital curtains until the last week."

His breath caught at that, a sound almost like laughter. Their mother had believed that ugly curtains were an insult deliberately delivered to the sick.

Mara told him about the funeral. Not the official memorial, not Venn's hand on her shoulder. The small service, the rain stopping, Bess bringing the wrong flowers because she insisted their mother had preferred yellow. She gave him details because details were what he had been denied, and because grief without details could be made to mean anything.

When she finished, he wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"I don't know if I can be who you came for."

"You don't have to settle that today."

"You say that now."

"Then you can remind me later."

Doss tapped the glass. Twelve minutes.

Ivo leaned forward. "There is something else. They will tell you the whole city dies if we stop. That isn't exactly true. The modern system needs us because they closed the older options. There's a spill basin north of the third wall. Dry now. They built the Trade Hall over its inlet. Reopen it and you can take the first surge without the predictive network."

"Tamsin is checking the manual gates."

He smiled properly for the first time. "Of course she is. Tell her the east counterweight has a cracked pin. I reported it six years ago. They bypassed the alarm."

"Could the manual system hold through this storm?"

"With evacuation of the lowest terrace, maybe. Not comfortably. There are uncertainties. I won't give you a heroic number and let someone else drown inside it."

He asked for paper. Mara had only the back of her borrowed shift sheet. He drew a sequence of gates, wrote three intervals, and crossed the last one out.

"This changes if the outer tide arrives early. Sella thinks the coastal forecast is wrong. She feels a long pressure cycle the offshore buoys haven't resolved."

"How early?"

"Tomorrow night. Not three days from now."

The room seemed to contract around the table.

Doss opened the door. "We need to move."

Mara stood reluctantly. There were years of questions left, and they had spent almost their whole visit learning how little time remained.

At the door, Ivo caught her sleeve.

"Don't just open the beds. Ask people. Some will want to stay until the danger passes. Some are more afraid of outside than of this. They still get to choose."

"I know."

"Know it when it becomes inconvenient."

She held his gaze, then nodded.

Doss took her out through a service passage. Near the lift, they passed a disposal cart loaded with sealed blue sleeves. Each contained memories surrendered that morning. Mara saw the archive's routing marks, her own department's neat categories, and understood how many separate ordinary jobs had been assembled into the mechanism around her brother.

At the loading bay she removed the grey coat and stepped into the afternoon. The sky had become an unfamiliar green-grey. On the terrace above the clinic, laundry snapped in a wind arriving from the wrong direction.

She found Tamsin beside the fish stairs and handed her Ivo's drawing.

"We have until tomorrow night," she said.

Tamsin read the intervals once, then again. She folded the paper carefully enough to keep every line visible.

"Then we had better stop calling them old gates," she said. "They're the gates we've got."


Chapter 9: An Inventory of Absence

Pei met Mara behind the archive at four, in the little yard where delivery workers smoked beneath a leaking awning. He brought her blue cup in a paper bag.

"I thought you might want it," he said.

"I'm not leaving forever."

"They packed your desk."

Rainwater fell steadily from one corner of the awning. Mara took the bag. She had expected that her suspension was a threat awaiting a response. The speed with which her desk had become available to somebody else made her understand that the archive had its own momentum, independent of Venn's negotiations.

"Did they destroy the Pell deposit?"

"It was collected by clinical security. Your calibration hold was canceled."

"When?"

"An hour after you left yesterday."

So the original recording was gone from her reach. Mara leaned against the wet brick wall and felt the stupidity of leaving it behind. She had obeyed preservation rules while the people who wrote them sent a courier.

Pei watched her face. "What was in it?"

She told him about Ivo. She kept Nera's address and the other patients' names out of the account. Pei listened with the little crease between his eyebrows that usually appeared when he found conflicting dates in a deposit.

"You're sure it was recent?"

"I saw him today. In person."

The crease disappeared. His expression became much worse.

"What do you need?"

"Records of clinical destruction orders from the four months after the evacuation. My own relief summary. And the public works archive for the old spill basin."

"Your summary is restricted. The destruction index isn't. The works files are in the public reading room. You could have asked for those yourself."

Mara almost laughed. She had begun to think like a fugitive so completely that she had forgotten the existence of ordinary doors.

"They haven't banned me from the reading room?"

"Not yet. I'll sit with you. Visitors need supervision if their staff access is suspended."

They entered through the public door. The reading room was half empty: two students, an elderly man studying property boundaries, a woman tracing a ship's passenger list with a gloved finger. Mara signed the visitors' book. The attendant looked at her name, looked at Pei, and handed her a pencil without comment.

For two hours, they read.

The old spill basin appeared on a survey dated thirty-six years earlier. It had been built to accept a controlled overflow from the north canal, sacrificing warehouses and a stretch of market gardens to protect the residential terraces. In a later revision, the basin became a seasonal recreation ground. Then a logistics yard. Finally the inlet vanished beneath a white rectangle labeled TRADE HALL EXTENSION.

There was an engineer's objection attached to the planning permission. The objection said that retaining a hydraulic route was essential even if predictive control reduced its anticipated use. A handwritten response called the route an obsolete redundancy.

Mara copied the engineer's name: Orla Finch.

The destruction index was worse.

Hundreds of clinical relief deposits had been disposed of after the flood. That was expected. What was not expected was the identical retention exception attached to seventy-nine files: ASSOCIATED CIVIL ACCOUNT TO BE REPLACED BY APPROVED SUMMARY. A replacement summary should never erase the existence of the original consent record. Yet the index showed both events under a single authorization.

Mara found her own number halfway down the second page.

She had surrendered eleven hours and forty-two minutes, spread across six days. The public summary said only that she had participated in emergency registration and experienced bereavement-related distress. The destruction index, more precise because nobody expected a patient to read it, listed the source events: participant authorization, unsuccessful withdrawal inquiry, lower-ward access refusal, departmental review.

She read the words several times.

Unsuccessful withdrawal inquiry.

She had asked. After the seventy-two hours, after Ivo should have been released, she had asked for him. Then she had accepted relief that removed the experience of asking.

Pei placed a sheet of blotting paper over the page when he saw her hands begin to shake. It was an archivist's reflex, protecting the record from damage. He looked ashamed a moment later.

"It's all right," Mara said. "The paper didn't do it."

She could not remember a single detail. No corridor returned, no voice rose from the blank. The gap remained a gap. She had wanted a revelation to bring back the whole scene and make her feelings trustworthy. Instead she had an index, a sequence of dates, and evidence that somebody had approved a very convenient description of her recovery.

"There may be a witness," Pei said.

"Jonas tried. I reported him."

"Then let him tell you now."

At closing time, the attendant collected their pencils. Mara requested certified copies of the public documents and paid the fee with shaking fingers. The attendant stamped each sheet, a small official thunderclap. It was strange to receive help from the same building that had packed her desk, but buildings were full of people, and people did not all move together.

On the pavement outside, Pei gave her another envelope.

"This was in your drawer. I didn't open it."

Inside lay an old photograph of the archive staff, taken after the evacuation. Mara stood near Venn, wearing a black band around one sleeve. At the edge of the picture, almost cut off, Jonas was holding a folder. His mouth was open as if he had called her name.

She remembered the photograph being taken. She remembered being irritated by someone beyond the frame. For nine years that irritation had seemed insignificant. Now it belonged to a man trying to return her brother to the world.

At the workshop, Jonas confirmed the dates without triumph. He described her first request for Ivo's discharge, the medical refusal, the promise that a specialist would review the case. He had no record of what Venn said privately. He would not guess.

"Did I know he was alive when I took relief?" Mara asked.

"I don't know. By then they were telling families that several participants had died during complications. You may have believed he was among them."

"May have."

"Yes."

It was a difficult mercy, his refusal to give her certainty she had not earned.

They compared the old authorizations with the current roster. There had been one hundred and twelve participants assigned during the emergency. Sixty-four remained. Twenty had confirmed discharges. Twenty-eight were recorded as deceased, their deaths dispersed across ordinary medical registers with no mention of Continuity.

Jonas wrote the number on a separate sheet. Twenty-eight.

For each, there would be a family, or an empty address, or someone who had stopped waiting. Mara thought of the public memorial's polished names and the quieter deaths underneath them. The city had not merely hidden living people. It had made their dying administratively unremarkable.

They stayed up sorting evidence into three packets: public infrastructure, institutional decisions, and private patient records. The last packet went into a locked case. Mara wrote on its cover that no name should be disclosed without the person's agreement or an independent advocate's review.

Tamsin arrived after midnight, smelling of engine oil.

"Found your engineer," she said. "Orla Finch. She runs the flood museum. Apparently retirement was insufficient punishment."

Mara looked up from the table.

"Will she help?"

"She told me to bring gloves and somebody who could lift more than a grievance. We start at dawn."


Chapter 10: The Age of Floods

The Age of Floods opened at ten for school groups. At six in the morning, it belonged to dust, sleeping display screens, and Orla Finch, who was trying to remove a brass plaque with a crowbar.

"Hold that," she told Mara as they entered.

Mara caught the plaque before it struck the floor. It read: MANUAL CONTROL WHEEL, RETIRED IN THE INTEREST OF PUBLIC SAFETY.

Behind it, a large iron wheel occupied a recess in the wall. Its spokes had been painted blue for the exhibit. Children had polished the lower ones with their hands.

"Still connected?" Tamsin asked.

"They paid to disconnect it. They didn't pay me to supervise. Contractor took out the indicator shaft and left the drive. Cheaper. Today we appreciate his lack of ambition."

Orla was short and broad, with silver hair cut close to her head and a hearing aid that whistled whenever she bent near metal. She had brought a toolbox whose handle was repaired with sailcloth. Tamsin's nephew, Kit, carried a second box and tried to appear untroubled by its weight.

Mara glanced at Tamsin.

"His mother knows," the captain said. "He's working the dry controls."

Kit set down the box with an exaggerated gentleness that betrayed the effort. "I can hear you."

"Then hear the instructions."

They opened the floor hatch beneath a diorama of the great flood. Stairs led down to a chamber where an enormous screw shaft disappeared into the wall. Grease had hardened around its threads. The room smelled of stale water and old iron. A maintenance log on a hook contained its last entry from seven years earlier: RECOMMEND RETAINING ACCESS.

Orla read it and snorted. "I used to be very restrained."

They spent the first hour clearing debris and inspecting the mechanism. Mara carried tools, held lamps, and wrote down measurements. It was work she understood only in pieces, but the pieces were concrete. A bolt either turned or did not. Water stood a certain distance below the sill. The certainty was a relief until she remembered what a small part of the whole problem they were touching.

At seven-thirty, Orla sent Tamsin and Kit to the upper wheel. Mara remained below beside a red stop lever. The test would lift the gate only a hand's breadth, enough to prove that the mechanical connection still worked. The north canal was at low stage. There should be no significant flow.

"Should," Orla said into the speaking tube. "That's why we test. Quarter turn when I call."

The shaft began to move with a deep squeal. Flakes of dried grease fell like black snow. Behind the wall, something heavy shifted.

Water hissed through a pipe near the floor.

Orla held up a hand. "Stop there."

The shaft stopped. The hiss continued, growing louder.

Mara looked at the pressure gauge. Its needle trembled just above zero, then jumped.

"That isn't canal pressure," Orla said.

She crossed the chamber quickly, crouched beside the pipe, and touched it with two fingers. Her face changed.

"Close. Close it now."

Above them, the wheel turned in reverse. The shaft shuddered. A crack sounded inside the wall, sharp enough to make Mara duck. Water burst through a corroded joint and struck the opposite stairs. Orla grabbed the railing before the spray knocked her backward.

"Stop lever!"

Mara pulled. The lever moved halfway and jammed. She put both hands on it, braced a foot against the wall, and pulled until something in her shoulder burned. The lever dropped. The shaft locked.

The water kept coming.

Orla waded toward a smaller wheel partly hidden behind a display crate. She turned it three times. The jet weakened to a thick run, then stopped. Silence arrived in pieces, broken by dripping water and someone swearing upstairs.

Mara's trousers were soaked to the waist. Her shoulder throbbed. Orla was sitting on a step, breathing hard, the hearing aid whistling against the rail.

"What happened?"

"They connected the old equalization line to a high-pressure return. It isn't on my drawing."

"Could it have killed us?"

Orla looked at the broken joint. "Yes."

Upstairs, Kit was cradling one hand. The wheel had kicked when the shaft jammed, catching two fingers against the frame. They were swelling rapidly. Tamsin wrapped them with a cloth and sent him to a clinic with a museum caretaker who had arrived to unlock the public entrance.

Kit tried to protest. Tamsin said his name once, and he went.

Mara stood beneath a mural celebrating the technological end of catastrophic flooding. Painted citizens watched a golden sunrise while stylized gates held back a blue wall of water. She wanted to sit down inside the falseness of it and give up for five minutes.

Orla spread the newer plans on a bench.

"The gate is usable," she said. "After we isolate that return and replace the joint. The test worked. It found the thing we needed to find."

"Kit is hurt," Mara said.

"Yes. And I should have traced the line physically before moving anything. Being right about the council doesn't make my drawing current."

She wrote the mistake in the maintenance log. No qualification, no passive voice. Mara watched the pencil move and thought about how rare that simple action had become in the offices above them.

Tamsin returned from the doorway. "Two fingers. Probably broken. His mother is going to remove my head."

"I'll speak to her," Orla said.

"You'll get in line."

They continued after breakfast, slower now. Orla checked every pipe by sight. Tamsin crawled through a service passage with a mirror and torch. Mara photographed labels and compared them with the revised schematics. By ten, they had identified the bypass and isolated it. By eleven, the gate moved through its full required travel while all four of them stood outside the dangerous part of the chamber.

The iron wheel turned under human hands. On the gauge, the water level changed as predicted.

Nobody cheered. Tamsin called the clinic for news of Kit. Orla sat on the bench and drank cold tea. Mara wrote down the successful test time, then the injury, then the modifications still required at the other stations.

There was an answer here, but it was made of work, uncertainty, and people who could be hurt. It would never fit inside Venn's clean projection of eleven injuries and no fatalities.

At noon, they went to inspect the Trade Hall.

The former basin inlet lay beneath a loading platform occupied by stacks of imported stone. Beyond it stretched the old depression, now a parking yard full of freight trailers. A line of small houses had been built along its northern edge. On the maps, those houses were classified as temporary storage structures.

Mara counted curtains, flowerpots, a child's bicycle. At least twenty families lived in the place the plans proposed to fill with water.

Orla lowered the drawing.

"I didn't know those were homes."

Tamsin looked at the occupied windows. "Then we start by knocking."


Chapter 11: Names for the Water

The first woman who answered accused them of surveying for demolition. The second said she had paid rent on time for six years and was not leaving because somebody had changed the name of her street. At the third house, a man listened to the explanation, went inside, and returned with a folder of unanswered complaints about damp.

Mara stood on his narrow doorstep while he showed her photographs of mold behind his daughter's bed. He wanted the city to acknowledge that his home had been wet for years before it arrived to explain that the place was supposed to be underwater.

She looked at every photograph.

By the time they reached the seventh house, the first six households had gathered behind them. Questions overlapped. Who would pay? Where would the furniture go? Was the storm warning official? Could they return afterward? A woman in a supermarket uniform asked whether she would lose her job if she missed the evening shift.

Mara had brought engineering drawings. None answered those questions.

Tamsin called three boat owners. Orla called a retired building inspector who knew which upper-terrace halls had working toilets. Mara called the archive reading room and asked Pei for the emergency accommodation register. She did not ask him to break access restrictions. The register was public, updated monthly, and had been ignored by precisely the people who needed it.

Lena's bakery supplied tea. Nera arrived with a notebook and began recording names, household sizes, pets, mobility needs. When a resident refused to give his surname, she wrote down the number on his door and continued. Nobody had to prove deservingness before she would count them.

The folder of mold photographs belonged to a man named Yusef Bair. He became their guide because the other residents trusted him enough to argue with him. He led them through side passages to homes hidden behind freight containers. The twenty families became thirty-seven. The official survey listed zero.

At one house, a girl of eight asked whether she could take her fish.

"Yes," Mara said.

Her mother began to object. There was too much to carry. The girl looked down at a chipped glass bowl, and Mara saw how easily an adult could turn necessity into an absolute rule simply by being tired.

"A lidded jar would be safer," she said. "I'll find one."

She found it at Lena's bakery. Returning with a pickle jar while a storm approached felt absurd, until the child accepted it and began packing her other things. One small certainty had made the larger uncertainty possible.

By two, the loading yard had become a meeting. People stood between trailers, holding cups, while Orla explained where water would go if the inlet opened. She used chalk on the pavement. The simplest lines were the hardest to draw: here was a bedroom, here a slope, here the level at which the lower wall might fail if they did nothing.

"Who decided this?" Yusef asked.

"Nobody has authorized opening it yet," Mara said.

"But you want us to leave before they decide."

"Yes. Because leaving afterward may be impossible."

He considered that. "Then write down that we aren't abandoning the houses. We're evacuating them. There is a difference when compensation comes."

Mara wrote it down. She asked him to read the sentence and sign only if it matched what he meant. He changed one word before signing. She kept the change.

They needed official transport, medical assistance, and access to the inlet. They also needed the wider city to know why. The evidence packets waited at Jonas's workshop. It was time to let other people inspect them.

The Bellwether Ledger occupied two rooms above a print shop and published three days a week when paper was available. Its editor, Kez Amadi, had spent the previous month investigating inflated dredging invoices. She was not eager to set that work aside for a story about secret minds beneath the city.

"People bring me a conspiracy every Thursday," she said. "Usually it turns out to be procurement."

"This one includes procurement," Jonas replied, putting the book on her desk.

She read the original authorization. Then the continuation orders. Then the destruction index. Her expression changed less than Mara expected, but she stopped checking the wall clock.

"I need independent confirmation of the ward."

"The nurse can speak," Mara said. "And two patients have authorized their names. My brother, Ivo Vale, and Sella Orr. The rest remain private."

"You can't ask me to report an institution of sixty-four people on two names and your assurance."

"You can inspect the roster under a confidentiality agreement that protects the patients. Publish the total and the evidence of detention. The people in those beds shouldn't lose their medical privacy because we're trying to stop another violation."

Kez considered her, then nodded. "I'll need a second reader present. Someone outside your group. And I won't promise a conclusion before I verify it."

"Good," Mara said, surprised by the relief she felt.

The editor called a civil-rights solicitor who had previously sued the city over housing inspections. While they waited, she examined the public works plans and the basin survey. This part she understood immediately. A flood route built over, occupied homes missing from the register, a contract awarded to a council donor. The secret ward was extraordinary. The land underneath the Trade Hall was ordinary corruption, which made the larger account easier to test.

Doss called from a public terminal at three-fifteen. He confirmed the roster, the separation constraints, and the change in the surge forecast. Kez asked whether the patients could leave if they wished.

"Not under the current orders," he said.

"Have any been refused?"

"Yes. I have personally documented nineteen requests in the last year."

"Will you put your name to that?"

A long pause followed. Mara could hear people moving near the terminal, and somewhere behind them a cheerful announcement about visiting hours.

"Yes," Doss said.

The solicitor arrived with a camera and a bag of groceries. She put the groceries under the desk and began checking dates. For forty minutes, the room contained only turning pages and the scratching of notes. Mara wanted urgency to make the process faster. She also knew that the careful slowness was what separated their work from a rumor that Venn could dismiss.

At four, Kez called the director's office for comment. She gave Venn twenty minutes because the weather warning was changing and the first edition had to go to print. Mara listened to the editor describe the allegations without decoration. The plainness made them more terrible.

Venn's response arrived in twelve minutes. The program was essential, clinically supervised, and subject to emergency law. Historical irregularities were under review. Premature interference could place every resident at risk.

Kez included it in full.

She did not publish patient recordings. She did not use Ivo's face. Her headline named the institution and the question: WHO MAY LEAVE THE CITY'S LIVING FLOOD SYSTEM?

The presses began downstairs at five. The sound traveled up through Mara's feet. At the same time, the Ledger sent the verified documents to three out-of-city newsrooms and posted copies to its public board. Seizing one office would no longer erase the record.

Mara expected to feel triumph. Instead she worried about Doss walking back into the ward, Ivo hearing security arrive, Sella realizing her name had crossed the city. Evidence did not protect bodies merely by existing.

Her terminal rang. She had turned it back on only for the editor's calls.

The caller was Venn.

"Come to the municipal chamber," the director said. "Bring your engineer. We have perhaps twelve hours before first impact, and I would like to know whether your alternative exists outside a newspaper."


Chapter 12: What the Forecast Leaves Out

The municipal chamber had been built for debates in a quieter century. Its windows faced inland, away from the sea, and its acoustic ceiling made every cough sound like a contribution. Mara arrived with Orla, Jonas, Tamsin, and a roll of plans too large to fit through the security scanner.

The guard made them unroll it anyway.

Inside, Venn sat at the central table with the city engineer, the hospital director, two council members, and a magistrate called Leto Sar. Kez stood near the back wall taking notes. Somebody had called the meeting public but provided only twelve chairs for observers. The corridor outside was already crowded.

Venn did not waste time on greetings.

"The offshore data now confirms an accelerated surge. Expected first impact is before dawn. Our priority is preventing a second eastern evacuation."

"It was a flood," Tamsin said. "Evacuation is what people did afterward."

The director accepted the correction with a small nod and asked Orla to present the alternative.

Orla spread the maps. She described the three remaining manual gate stations, the fourth station that could be operated through its emergency shaft, and the north spill basin. She described the broken fingers at the morning test and the undocumented pressure connection. She did not pretend that proving one mechanism meant proving all of them.

The city engineer, a tired man named Havel, examined her calculations. Twice he interrupted to correct old pipe diameters. Both times Orla asked him to write the correction on the plan. The argument became, briefly, useful.

"This protects the upper four terraces," Havel said. "It deliberately floods the lowest service belt and the basin yard."

"Most of the service belt is already evacuation territory under your own extreme-event plan," Orla replied. "You never announced it because Continuity kept avoiding the threshold."

"Avoiding the threshold is the point."

"At the cost of making everything beyond it unusable."

Venn asked for probable casualties. Orla refused to give a single number.

"If you provide transport now, clear the basin, and finish the gate repairs, we can reduce exposure substantially. If we spend the next four hours arguing, every estimate worsens. The uncertainty is larger than your published forecast admits."

Havel looked down at his tablet. "She's right about the uncertainty."

It was the first official fracture Mara had heard.

The hospital director raised the question of patients who might not survive separation. Doss's treatment notes, supplied to the solicitor with identifiers removed, described a staged reduction over at least forty-eight hours for the most dependent participants. They had less than twelve. Abrupt disconnection could produce seizures, loss of autonomic regulation, severe confusion.

"Then we don't disconnect them abruptly," Mara said. "We take the flood burden off them and move the medical support with them."

"Portable support exists for only twenty beds," the hospital director replied.

"How many can remain on local support in the ward if the forecasting interface is isolated?" Jonas asked.

Havel frowned. "All of them in principle. The medical circuits and the gate-control circuits are separate."

"In principle," Venn repeated. "The old cistern is below the extreme flood line. Continuity itself is part of its protection."

The room fell silent.

Mara looked from Venn to the engineer. "You put the people keeping the city dry in the place most likely to drown if they stopped."

"The infrastructure predated the long-term program," Venn said.

"And every year afterward?"

No one answered.

The magistrate asked to see the original authorizations. Jonas placed the red book in front of her. She read Mara's handwritten restriction, then the later continuation order. Her finger rested on the phrase compromised capacity.

"Who assessed capacity independently of the program?"

The hospital director named a committee. Three of its five members reported to Venn. The other two had been appointed by the department.

"That is not independent," Sar said.

Venn leaned forward. "Magistrate, if you issue a broad shutdown order tonight, people may die before any appeal is heard."

"I am aware of the clock. It does not make these signatures say something different."

From the corridor came raised voices. The guard opened the door, spoke to someone, and looked uncertainly toward the table. A woman pushed past him holding a folded newspaper. She was perhaps fifty, wearing a cleaner's uniform beneath her coat.

"My husband is on this list," she said.

Mara's stomach tightened. They had published only two names.

The woman unfolded the paper. "Sella Orr says there are sixty-four. My husband went to the storm works and never came back. Is he there? His name is Anil. Anil Dev."

Mara remembered the man with buttons on the floor.

She did not answer across the room. She went to the woman, introduced herself, and asked her to step into the side office with the hospital director. The woman resisted until Mara said that the answer, whatever it was, belonged to her before it belonged to the chamber.

When Mara returned, the debate had shifted. The magistrate was drafting an emergency order. It would suspend involuntary cognitive duty, require individual decisions with independent advocates, preserve all records, and authorize immediate preparation of alternative defenses. It did not direct a simultaneous disconnection. It did require the department to begin releasing people from work the moment safe support could be arranged.

Venn read the draft, then placed it flat on the table.

"I cannot certify this plan as equally protective."

"You are not being asked to," Sar said. "You are being asked to operate within the limits of lawful consent."

One council member objected that the city had not voted on the risk. Tamsin, who had been silent for several minutes, leaned forward.

"Did it vote on those people?"

The member began a reply about emergency powers. Venn stopped him with a raised hand.

She looked suddenly older. Not softened, not redeemed, simply a person whose familiar argument had reached the edge of what it could do.

"The inlet clearance requires demolition of the Trade Hall loading platform," she said. "That will take equipment we do not have on standby."

"The dredging contractor has two excavators there," Kez said from the back. "I know because they billed the city for keeping them available all month."

Havel almost smiled.

The practical decisions began. Transport to the upper halls. Hospital beds cleared. A local-support station established in the old school above the third wall. Battery units borrowed from surgical reserve without interrupting active care. Gate crews assembled from harbor workers and retired operators. The magistrate required a written chain of responsibility and put her own name at the top of the order.

Mara watched Venn issue instructions into her terminal. The director was efficient, specific, and listened when Havel corrected her. The same competence that had maintained the prison was now moving buses toward the basin. Mara did not know what to do with that fact except refuse to let one use cancel the other.

At the end, Venn approached her beside the empty observer chairs.

"You may get your brother out," she said. "You may also learn why I kept delaying."

"I already understand why."

"Then perhaps you should be less certain."

Mara looked at the signed order in her hands.

"I'm not certain this will work. I'm certain you couldn't keep taking his answer away."

Outside, the first evacuation buses were arriving. People carried blankets, bags, pets, framed photographs. Above the square, the public speakers clicked, and Sella's recorded voice began announcing a precautionary relocation.

For the first time Mara heard the announcement as labor performed by a particular woman, in a particular room, who had asked to go home.


Chapter 13: The Long Way Uphill

The buses could not enter the basin yard because freight trailers blocked the access road. For twenty minutes, three municipal departments tried to determine whose authority covered moving them. Yusef solved the problem by finding the man who owned the keys.

The man had been at supper. He arrived with rice still caught in his beard and began shifting trailers without asking who would pay. Mara wrote down his name anyway. She had spent too much of her life treating unpaid useful work as something the city naturally produced.

The first bus took the oldest residents and those who could not climb the terrace stairs. Nera sat beside the driver with her household list. Every few minutes she leaned out to shout that evacuation was not an orderly queue if people kept sending their luggage ahead of their neighbors.

Mara helped the girl with the fish climb aboard. The jar was wrapped in a towel. Through its glass, a single orange shape moved slowly in cloudy water.

"Does it know?" the girl asked.

"Probably that something is changing."

"I told it we're coming back."

Mara looked at the narrow houses beneath the loading platform. "You can tell it where you are when you arrive. That might help more."

The girl considered this and nodded solemnly, as if Mara had offered sound fishkeeping advice.

By nine, the wind had begun lifting loose paper from the yard. The excavators arrived under municipal escort. Orla stood on the loading platform with a torch, showing the operators where to break the concrete without damaging the buried inlet. Havel had sent a survey crew. Their fresh measurements confirmed most of her plan and contradicted one important slope. Water would reach the northern houses sooner than expected.

The evacuation boundary moved another street uphill.

People already angry about leaving became angrier when they saw the boundary change. A man shouted that nobody knew what they were doing. Mara could not truthfully deny it in the absolute terms he wanted. She explained what had been measured, what remained uncertain, and why the new information meant moving farther.

"So we pay for your mistakes," he said.

"You're being asked to bear a cost you didn't create. Yes. We are recording that."

He stared at her, deprived of the easy argument that would have followed reassurance. Then he asked where his mother could sit while they waited. Mara found a chair.

At ten, the first gate crew reported a successful dry test. At ten-thirty, the second reported a seized bearing. Tamsin borrowed a portable heater from the print shop and took it down to the control chamber. Kit, with two fingers splinted, had been assigned to the upper school to label beds. He sent a message asking whether the heater needed its earth connection checked.

Tamsin replied that it did, then called a qualified electrician. Mara saw the effort it cost her to accept the question without allowing the boy back into danger.

The city changed shape around the work. Streets normally filled with shop signs and tram bells became routes marked by lanterns. People who had never met carried each other's cupboards up flights of stairs. Others locked their doors and refused to answer. An argument broke out over whether a shelter admitted dogs. Nera settled it by entering with the elderly bakery dog and inviting the objector to explain his policy to her personally.

None of this looked like the orderly movement of figures across Venn's model. It was slower, noisier, and more inventive. It also had limits. A woman collapsed on the third set of stairs. A wheelchair lift failed. A bus driver, seventeen hours into his shift, drove past a turning and had to reverse under shouted instructions. Mara began to understand how much of any evacuation was the management of fatigue.

She sat on a wall at eleven and ate a roll Lena pressed into her hand. The bread was still warm in its center. She had forgotten lunch and most of supper.

"How many left?" she asked.

"From the basin? Six households and a man who won't leave his pigeons. From the wider belt, too many to count from here."

"We need a count."

"Then eat while someone counts."

Lena sat beside her. Until that evening, Mara had known her only as the woman in Nera's photograph, holding a fish. Now her hair had escaped its tie and flour marked one sleeve beneath the raincoat.

"My grandmother likes you," she said.

"She has an unusual way of showing it."

"She spends her best arguments on people she hopes will listen."

Below them, an excavator broke through the last section of loading platform. Concrete fell with a deep hollow crash. The inlet emerged as a dark rectangular mouth framed by old stone. Orla climbed down to inspect it and waved one arm. The structure had held.

Mara wanted to call Ivo. There was no direct line to his room. Doss had promised to send updates through the hospital coordinator, but none had arrived in an hour. She imagined security shutting the ward, Venn reversing the order, her brother becoming unreachable again while Mara distributed tea above him.

She stood too quickly.

Lena caught her elbow. "Where are you going?"

"The clinic."

"Then tell someone before you disappear. We've had enough of people doing that."

Mara wrote her destination on the coordination board. She handed Nera the latest household list, confirmed which numbers were unverified, and found the hospital shuttle waiting near the square. It was carrying blankets, power units, and three independent patient advocates recruited by the magistrate's office. A fourth advocate, a retired mental-health nurse, was already at Saint Brigid's.

On the journey, one of the advocates asked Mara whether her brother had expressed a choice.

"He wants to leave."

"Does he want to leave immediately?"

Mara looked out at the wet street. "I didn't ask it that way."

"Then we will."

The answer should have reassured her. Instead it brought back Ivo's warning: know it when it becomes inconvenient.

At the clinic, the loading bay was bright with temporary lamps. Ambulance crews checked portable support packs against handwritten lists. The guard who had asked about pillows the previous day was helping unload them now. He recognized Mara and opened his mouth, then closed it. Whatever he had thought the lower ward contained, the activity had made concealment impossible.

Doss met the shuttle at the lift. He looked as if he had aged a year since breakfast.

"Some staff left," he said. "They were frightened of being arrested. Others stayed but won't sign anything. The hospital has sent replacements. We are short of people who know the equipment."

"What can I do?"

"Listen. Help people understand the choices. Don't promise recovery. Don't promise the city will forgive them for choosing themselves."

The lift doors closed around them. Mara felt the familiar vibration under her feet.

This time, when they reached the lower ward, the corridor was full of open doors. Patients stood beside their beds, sat with advocates, or watched the commotion without visible understanding. A man was shouting that he wanted his shoes, his own shoes, not the padded clinic slippers. Another was weeping into the shoulder of a woman who had brought him a clean shirt.

Anil Dev's wife looked up as Mara passed. Her hand rested on his white hair. On the table between them, buttons marked streets that no longer existed.

"He remembers our old kitchen," she said. "I'll start there."


Chapter 14: Sixty-Four Answers

The first person Mara helped was a man named Mikel Roan, who had once supervised the tram power network. He lay in bed with an oxygen tube under his nose and a folded newspaper on his chest. He had read the Ledger's report twice and disliked the headline.

"Makes it sound as if I need permission," he said.

The advocate beside him introduced herself as Sumi. She asked whether he understood the risks of moving. His heart had been weakened by an infection. The portable unit could support the neural transition, but it could not reproduce every function of the ward.

"I understand that this bed is in a hole below the water," Mikel said. "Your list of risks ought to start there."

Sumi wrote that down.

He wanted to leave. He wanted his newspaper, his own cardigan, and the little tin of tea in his locker. He did not want his sister called until he had reached the school because she would spend the journey worrying and then scold him for causing it.

"She may be angry we waited," Mara said.

"Then you can give her my name. I made the decision."

He signed slowly, pausing when his hand cramped. Sumi recorded his words as well as the signature. Mara put the tea tin into a cloth bag and tied it to the bed rail where it would not be lost during transfer.

The next patient said no.

She wanted to remain in the ward until morning. She had lived there eight years. Her family had moved inland and did not know she was alive. The school, the ambulance, the crowd of strangers felt to her like another disappearance arranged by officials.

Mara began explaining the flood risk. The woman turned her face toward the wall.

Sumi touched Mara's arm and asked her to wait outside.

For ten minutes Mara stood in the corridor watching other people's progress. She disliked being removed from a conversation she believed she could make better. When Sumi emerged, the woman had agreed to transfer with a nurse she knew, in a vehicle whose door would remain open until she said she was ready.

"She wasn't refusing safety," Sumi said. "She was refusing to be handled."

Mara wrote the distinction in her notebook because she suspected she would need it again.

Not every answer was clear. One patient could explain the flood plan but could not retain it for more than a few minutes. Another understood moving but became distressed whenever the advocate mentioned the outside city. For those cases, the advocates documented preferences expressed consistently over time, consulted trusted people where available, and arranged the least restrictive safe care. There was no single form that made the uncertainty disappear.

Jonas arrived shortly after midnight with the original book and a smaller blank ledger. He sat at the nursing station entering decisions as they came, each with a time, a witness, and the patient's own words when possible. His right hand stiffened after a dozen entries. Mara offered to take over.

"In a minute," he said.

He finished the page before passing her the pen. It was not a dramatic gesture. The work mattered enough that he wanted to do his share of it and no more than his hand could sustain.

By one, fifty-three patients had requested withdrawal from cognitive duty and transfer as soon as medically possible. Eleven had offered to continue limited assistance while the gates changed over, with an absolute stop at four in the morning and the right to stop earlier. The advocates checked those offers privately, without staff or relatives present. Two people changed their minds during that conversation. Their names moved to the transfer list. Two others, after asking more questions, volunteered to fill the limited roles.

The final eleven included Sella and Ivo.

Mara learned this from the ledger, not from her brother. For a moment she could not read the line underneath his name. She put down the pen and went to his room.

He was folding a shirt into a small bag.

"Four o'clock," she said.

"Or earlier if the gate crews finish."

"You told me you wanted to leave."

"I do."

"Then why—"

He looked up, and she stopped. She heard the sentence she was about to make: why are you making this harder for me? It had dressed itself as concern for him, but it was still her own fear speaking.

Ivo put the shirt down.

"The east sequence is the one I know. Tamsin will be at the counterweight. I can help her avoid a bad interval while Orla brings the spill route online. After four, I'm finished. I told the advocate to write that twice."

"What if they say they need another hour?"

"Then I say no. And you help them hear it."

Mara sat on the edge of the bed. She wanted him safe with an urgency that made everyone else's risk feel abstract. Recognizing that did not remove it.

"I don't trust myself to be reasonable about you," she said.

"You never were. You once threatened a boy with a compass because he pushed me into a ditch."

"He was twice your size."

"You were smaller than I was."

She remembered that afternoon perfectly: the hot grass, the mud on Ivo's trousers, the compass clutched absurdly in her fist. He had laughed at her until she began crying, then pretended it was a very dangerous compass after all.

They smiled at the same memory. For a few seconds, the intervening years did not vanish, but they stopped occupying every part of the room.

Sella appeared in the doorway carrying a pair of shoes and a bright green scarf.

"They've asked me to make a live announcement," she said. "Apparently the recordings are causing confusion because I sound too calm."

"Are you going to?" Ivo asked.

"I want people to know where the shelters are. I also want them to know I require a proper cup of coffee afterward. We'll see which part they broadcast."

Mara followed her to the communications station. A technician offered to smooth the tremor from her voice. Sella declined. She sat in front of the microphone, adjusted the green scarf, and waited for the signal.

"This is Sella Orr," she began. "You have heard my voice for many years. Tonight I am speaking from the eastern medical ward. Please move above the second terrace if your street is on the evacuation list. Take medicines, warm clothing, and the people who may need your help. There are volunteers at every marked stair. You do not need an identity card to enter a shelter."

She paused, breathing carefully.

"Some of us are leaving this ward tonight. We have served this city for a long time. We are afraid too. Help the people beside you, and listen to the gate crews when they give instructions. I hope to meet some of you tomorrow."

The technician looked toward an administrator, who looked at Mara. Nobody cut the line.

Sella took off the headphones. "No coffee," she said. "I lost my nerve."

At the far end of the ward, the first transfer team began moving beds toward the lift. The cables connecting those patients to the forecast were already dark. Their medical packs blinked green, each held by a nurse walking beside the stretcher. The distinction between support and service had become visible at last.

Mara went back to the ledger and entered the first departure time.

One-seventeen.

The page had room for sixty-four names. She intended to account for every one.


Chapter 15: The Weight Below the Floor

At two in the morning, Tamsin stood beneath the eastern counterweight and tried not to think about how much iron was being held above her by a pin with a crack in it.

The pin was larger than her forearm. A rust-dark line ran along its visible end and disappeared into the housing. Six years earlier, Ivo had noticed an irregular return in the gate sequence. Somewhere, a maintenance clerk had translated that warning into a low-priority inspection. Now the whole eastern control depended on moving the weight once without letting the damaged connection take a sideways load.

The chamber was cold enough to hurt her teeth. Water struck the outer wall with the rhythm of a heavy animal testing a fence. Beside her, two harbor mechanics unpacked a temporary steel collar. They had fabricated it that evening from a crane component, following Orla's drawing. The welds still smelled hot.

"It fits," said Jun, the older mechanic.

"That's one of the things it needs to do," Tamsin replied.

They lifted it around the housing using a chain hoist. Tamsin guided the lower edge while Jun's partner, Bea, watched the clearance above. Every movement had to be small. A careless knock could finish what nine years of deferred maintenance had begun.

The radio on Tamsin's belt clicked.

"East team, this is control. Confirm readiness for partial transfer."

It was Havel. Behind his voice, Tamsin could hear other calls and the thin repeated alarm of a weather feed demanding acknowledgment.

"Not ready. Collar going on. Ten minutes if we stay lucky."

"We need an exact estimate."

"Then send someone who can see the future. I hear you're retiring them."

She regretted the joke immediately. Havel said nothing, which was more generous than she deserved.

They tightened the collar in a cross pattern, checking the gap after each turn. Tamsin's gloves were wet. Her injured shoulder, an old souvenir of a winch accident, had begun to ache. She thought of Kit asleep or pretending to sleep in the school, his fingers swollen beneath their splint. His mother had called her an irresponsible fool, then asked whether the school had enough blankets. There would be a longer reckoning if they both lived to attend it.

At two-twenty, Orla reached them over the radio. The north inlet was clear. Evacuation teams had checked the basin houses twice. The pigeon keeper had finally agreed to leave after volunteers moved six cages into a municipal van. Three birds escaped; nobody had time to regret them properly.

"We can begin taking water when your east gate reaches the first mark," Orla said. "Not before. If we open first, the lower return pushes against your blade."

Tamsin looked at the painted travel scale on the wall. The first mark was a faded red line, almost covered by a later coat of grey.

"Have somebody verify your upstream level by hand," she said. "No green lights."

"Already done."

The collar was seated. Jun checked the bolts a final time and moved clear. Bea took the lever at the hydraulic isolator. Tamsin stood at the manual control wheel, a larger cousin of the museum exhibit. She had run engines in storms, cut trapped lines, entered flooded hulls with only a torch and a partner's grip on her ankle. This wheel frightened her because its consequences extended beyond what she could see.

Ivo came onto the channel.

"Tamsin?"

She pressed the transmitter. "Your directions still have terrible arrows."

"Mara said that. I think they're elegant."

He sounded thin, distant, but unmistakably himself. They had worked together for a summer before the flood, repairing small engines behind the harbor store. He had once spent a whole afternoon making a broken radio play only one station, then claimed that the limitation improved public taste.

"Glad you're here," she said.

"I'm leaving shortly."

"Good. Tell me how to avoid making that difficult."

He described the sequence. Isolate the powered drive. Let pressure equalize through the smaller bypass. Wait until the vibration changed from a continuous hum to distinct pulses. Turn the manual wheel slowly through the first interval. If the counterweight started to twist, stop immediately.

Bea pulled the isolator. The chamber's familiar mechanical undertone ceased. Into the space it left came the raw sound of water moving behind stone.

Tamsin waited. She could feel the hum through the wheel. It broke unevenly, returned, then settled into pulses.

"Now," Ivo said.

She turned.

The first revolution took nearly all her strength. Jun joined her at the opposite spoke. Together they moved the wheel one quarter turn at a time while Bea watched the pin. The great shaft rotated. Somewhere beyond the wall, the gate rose through its first inch.

Tamsin felt the resistance ease, then increase sharply.

"Hold," Bea said.

They stopped. The collar had shifted a fraction against its seating. A bright line appeared on one bolt where the paint cracked.

Ivo asked for the position. Bea read the scale. For several seconds the radio carried only his breathing.

"Reverse an eighth," he said. "The blade is catching on the old guide. Let it settle, then try again."

They reversed. The weight made a low groan that traveled through the floor into Tamsin's feet. The collar held.

On the second attempt, the gate moved past the resistance. The red mark approached the pointer slowly enough that Tamsin wanted to swear at it. Sweat ran inside her waterproof collar. Jun was breathing through his mouth.

"First mark," Bea said.

Tamsin locked the wheel. Her hands remained clamped to the spoke for a moment after she stopped needing them.

"North, begin," she told the radio.

Orla acknowledged. Thirty seconds later, the pressure gauge beside the east shaft began to fall. A new sound entered the chamber: water choosing a route the city had forgotten it possessed.

For the next twenty minutes, the reports were good. Water entered the north basin. The second gate transferred cleanly to local operation. Shelter counts improved. A ferry returned for its last group from the lower belt. Tamsin allowed herself one swallow of cold tea.

Then Ivo said, "Wait."

His voice had changed.

"The lower return isn't emptying. There's something connected to it."

Havel began checking the revised plans. Orla asked for two pressure readings. Tamsin watched the gauge that had been falling. It stopped, trembled, and began to rise.

"Medical cistern drain," Ivo said. "They share the old outlet. If the basin level passes the third sill, the drain can reverse."

"There should be a nonreturn valve," Havel replied.

"There should. I can't feel it closing."

Tamsin looked at the clock above the wheel. Three-oh-seven. The final eleven patients were still below the city, and the water route that would release them was beginning to threaten their rooms.

"Can we stop the basin fill?" she asked.

Orla answered at once. "For a short interval. After that the canal rises against the residential wall."

"Then give them the interval."

Havel issued the hold. Tamsin pictured a pencil drawing becoming water, the water finding a line nobody had drawn, and Ivo feeling that omission inside his body.

She called Mara's channel.

"You need to move faster," she said. "And I need you to understand that isn't an expression."


Chapter 16: The Last Departure Board

Mara received Tamsin's warning while helping a nurse wrap a patient's feet in dry socks. The woman's slippers had gone missing during transfer preparation, and she refused to leave barefoot. Around them, the ward had become a landscape of empty beds and coiled cables. Every departure changed its acoustics. Voices traveled farther now.

Forty-six patients had reached the upper school. Seven more were in transit or waiting at the lift. Eleven remained on limited duty. Mara could account for each by name, vehicle, attendant, and destination. She had checked the list so often that the numbers had become a rhythm in her head.

Then the hospital coordinator called about Mikel.

He had suffered a cardiac arrest in the ambulance. The team had resuscitated him once. At the school, his heart stopped again. They were still working when the first message came. The second message arrived six minutes later.

Mara stood beside the nursing station with the receiver against her ear. Doss watched her face and knew before she spoke.

"Who is with his sister?" she asked.

The coordinator said a nurse had called her. She was on her way to the school.

"His tea is in a cloth bag tied to the rail. His newspaper too. Please keep them together."

The coordinator promised.

Mara put down the receiver. For a moment she could see only the slow movement of Mikel's pen, the deliberate signature, his annoyance at a headline that made freedom sound conditional. She had wanted the ledger to become an uncomplicated list of arrivals. The first death made it an account of what had actually happened.

She wrote the time in a separate column. She did not mark him as transferred successfully. She did not mark the transfer as his cause of death. That would require a medical review. All she knew was that he had chosen to leave, that he had left, and that he had died before reaching the room prepared for him.

Doss gave her thirty seconds, then said her name.

Water was entering the lowest equipment gallery.

They went down one flight with a maintenance worker. The gallery floor shone under the lamps. A thin steady stream came through a drain grille, carrying flakes of rust. The worker opened an inspection cabinet and found the nonreturn valve's manual linkage. It moved freely in his hand, disconnected from whatever it was supposed to move below.

"Corroded through," he said.

"Can you close it locally?" Doss asked.

"Not without going into the outlet. Not in this water."

They returned upstairs. The hold at the north basin gave them perhaps forty minutes before the inlet had to reopen. Havel had dispatched a pumping crew, but its equipment was still crossing a congested terrace. The ward's emergency lift remained functional. The sensible route was to use it while they could.

The remaining seven transfer patients went first. Two needed portable units that had just returned from the school. One had to be carried because the wheels of her bed jammed at the lift threshold. Mara helped lift the frame, feeling the injured muscle in her shoulder protest. She refused to turn the pain into evidence of virtue. She was simply lifting too much, and when another orderly arrived she let him take her place.

At three-twenty, only the eleven volunteers, staff, and advocates remained.

Venn arrived through the upper service entrance with two engineers and a municipal guard. She had changed her garden cardigan for a waterproof coat. Mud marked its hem. She looked at the empty beds, the rising-water report, and the clock.

"How many support packs?"

"Twelve here," Doss said. "Enough for everyone who remains, with one reserve."

"Battery duration?"

"Three hours at current medical load. Less if anyone deteriorates."

Venn nodded and sent the guard to keep the loading route clear. Then she went to Ivo's room.

Mara followed.

Ivo sat at the table, one hand flat against it. He was speaking into the radio, giving Orla information about the delayed basin fill. Sella stood near the wall with her green scarf around her neck. Her lips moved soundlessly as she listened to machinery beyond the room.

"We need another controlled interval," Venn said when Ivo finished. "The inlet cannot stay closed. Can the eastern duty group sustain until four-thirty?"

Mara felt herself move before she chose to. She stepped between Venn and the table.

Ivo touched her wrist.

"Ask me," he said to the director.

Venn's face changed, not enough to name the expression.

"Can you continue until four-thirty?"

"No."

The word was quiet. Ivo looked exhausted after saying it, as if refusal used a muscle the ward had allowed to atrophy.

Venn glanced at Sella.

"No," Sella said. "I said four. I meant four."

The director took a breath. Mara watched for the familiar next step: an explanation of danger, an appeal to duty, the careful rearrangement of responsibility until the patient's answer no longer appeared to belong to them.

Instead Venn asked, "What can be handed over in the time left?"

Ivo pointed to the radio. "The raw pressure readings. Orla knows the sequence now. Stop trying to obtain the confidence the network gives you. You won't have it."

Venn stood very still, then went to the communications desk.

The next half hour was full of small tasks. Support packs were tested under load. Patients changed into outdoor clothes where they could. Sumi checked each volunteer's decision again. Jonas prepared a final departure sheet, leaving spaces for the people still present. The engineers isolated nonessential circuits in the lower gallery to reduce the risk of electrical failure.

At three-forty, water reached the bottom stair.

At three-forty-five, Havel authorized a restricted flow into the basin. The residential wall could bear no longer hold. He read the new settings aloud over the shared channel so the ward team would know exactly what was changing beneath them.

At three-forty-seven, the lift stopped between levels with no one inside. The indicator went dark. A technician tried the reset, listened to the machinery, and shook her head.

There was another route: a maintenance ramp rising toward the old tram power house above the second terrace. It was long, steep, and too narrow for beds. The engineers had checked it earlier and found the upper door sealed by a municipal security lock. Venn supplied the code. The lock accepted it, then reported a power fault.

Tamsin heard the exchange over the shared channel.

"My boat is beside the power house steps," she said. "There's a service hatch into the top landing. I can reach it."

"You're at the east gate," Mara replied.

"Jun can hold a wheel. I taught him the difficult part, which is listening when Bea says stop."

Mara looked at the clock. Three-fifty-two.

They began moving the volunteers toward the ramp in pairs. Some walked with help. Two needed carrying chairs. Ivo remained at the communications station for the last handover, a nurse beside him with his portable pack ready.

Mara went to the station and sat where he could see her.

"Eight minutes," she said.

He nodded. Beneath the floor, something heavy struck the drain grille from the other side.

Nobody mistook it for a request.


Chapter 17: Four O'Clock

The minute hand moved with an indecent lack of urgency.

At three-fifty-four, Ivo gave Orla the last sequence adjustment. The north inlet would have to open gradually, allowing the basin to take water without a sudden rise in the shared outlet. The east gate needed to remain at its first mark until the third pressure gauge stopped climbing. After that, the crews would use their own measurements.

"The third gauge has a lag," Ivo said. "Count thirty seconds after it settles. Not ten. The pipe is partly obstructed."

Orla repeated the instruction. Ivo asked her to repeat it once more. She did, without impatience.

At three-fifty-six, the last routine forecast appeared on the wall display. It showed the city in pale lines, the estuary pressing against the barriers, the basin slowly filling. Numbers moved beside each district. Mara had seen such projections for years in public briefings. Now she watched Ivo's fingers twitch with every update and understood the bodily cost behind the smooth movement.

The forecast labeled the upper terraces secure.

It labeled the cistern uncertain.

At three-fifty-seven, Venn received a call from Havel. A gate crew had lost its electronic level indicator. The manual scale was still readable, but spray made observation difficult. Havel asked whether the system could remain available as a fallback until the outer tide arrived.

Venn looked at Ivo, then at the departure sheet.

"No," she said into the receiver. "Use the manual scale. Rotate observers every five minutes."

She ended the call before Havel could offer another reason.

Mara did not thank her. This was what the order required. Still, she saw the effort of it and did not pretend otherwise.

Jonas came to the station with a registry terminal. Disabling the active cognitive sessions was not enough. The old emergency protocol would automatically request reconnection if predicted risk exceeded a threshold. Medical collars could receive that request even through portable units unless their duty authorizations were revoked at the registry.

"I can close each authorization," he said. "But the master continuation order is still active. It may issue new ones."

Venn supplied her director's credential. The terminal rejected it because the order required two officials and a clinical witness. Mara recognized the administrative design. It had been built to prevent one frightened person from switching off essential infrastructure. It had also made the collective decision nobody's individual responsibility.

"My temporary registrar appointment," she said. "Was it ever formally ended?"

Jonas checked. The old appointment had expired years ago. Mara felt a foolish brief disappointment. There would be no convenient resurrection of authority to match the revelation of her signature.

"The magistrate can issue a revocation," Jonas said. "We need her on the emergency line."

It took forty seconds to reach Sar. Forty seconds in which the water climbed another step below them, and Ivo gripped the table so hard that his knuckles whitened. Jonas read the order number. Sar dictated a revocation under the emergency ruling. Venn confirmed receipt. Doss witnessed the clinical transfer. The terminal accepted the three records.

The master order changed from active to closed.

It was three-fifty-nine.

Jonas printed two copies and placed one in the waterproof case with the original book. The other he handed to Ivo.

Ivo looked at the paper, then at him. "How many steps?"

Jonas swallowed. "Forty-eight from the trial room to the lift. I counted them every day after I got out."

"I remember. You said forty-eight was a number a person could finish."

"This is a different route."

"We'll count it anyway."

The clock reached four.

Doss asked Ivo for the last confirmation. Ivo said yes. The nurse connected the portable support pack, waited for its indicators to stabilize, and isolated the forecasting interface. A thin line of light along the wall cable went dark.

The city map stopped updating.

Nothing exploded. The ceiling did not fall. Water continued moving in the pipes, indifferent to the exact moment a human agreement changed. Somewhere above them, people were still walking uphill with bags and blankets. The ordinary continuation of the world made the silence around Ivo more frightening.

He looked at Mara.

"That's small," he said.

"What is?"

"The room."

His eyes moved to the corners, the ceiling, the table. He seemed to be seeing distances collapse. He reached toward the wall and missed it by an arm's length.

Doss lowered him into the chair. "Look at one thing. Not all of it. One thing you recognize."

Mara held up her father's wire recorder. She had carried it in her bag since the first visit to Jonas, a weight she had nearly stopped noticing.

"Do you remember this?"

Ivo frowned. "The survey recorder. He dropped it in the channel."

"You took it apart on the kitchen table. Mother said we'd never eat without screws in the food again."

For a moment his breathing slowed. Then his body jerked. His hand struck the table, and the cup beside it fell. The nurse caught his shoulder as he began to slide from the chair.

Mara moved forward. Doss told her to give them space.

They lowered Ivo to the floor and protected his head. The seizure was brief but seemed to remove the room from time. Mara heard the nurse counting, Doss calling a medication dose, the hollow crash of something falling in the lower gallery. She could not tell which sound belonged to the greater emergency.

When Ivo stopped moving, he did not immediately wake.

"Is the pack working?" she asked.

"Yes," Doss said. "This can happen during separation. We need to monitor his breathing and move him carefully."

"Could reconnecting—"

The question escaped before Mara could stop it.

Doss looked at her. He was not accusing her. That made it harder.

"The medical support is active. Returning him to forecast duty would add load, not treat the seizure."

Mara nodded, ashamed of how quickly fear had reached for the familiar prison. She had not wanted the city served. She had wanted her brother's eyes to open. But the impulse to reverse his decision had still been hers.

Sella knelt beside him. She had already been disconnected and wore her portable pack across her chest like an awkward satchel.

"Ivo," she said. "You're in the ward. The shift is over."

Her voice had guided him through years of shared attention. His eyelids moved. He breathed more deeply, then opened his eyes without seeming to focus.

"Too quiet," he whispered.

"I know. We'll make some noise."

She began humming. It was not a public melody, not an anthem or a reassurance chosen for crowds. It was a song Mara did not know, with an uneven phrase that returned before she expected it. Sella's voice shook. She kept going.

The ramp team called. The upper door was still closed, but Tamsin had reached the service hatch. The first nine patients were waiting on the landing with their attendants. Water had entered the lower end of the ramp.

They secured Ivo in a carrying chair. Doss checked every connection twice. Mara took the recorder and the bag of clothes. Jonas lifted the waterproof records case. Venn took one side of the chair despite the nurse's first objection. There were too few free hands to turn help away.

As they left, Mara looked back at the room. The broken cup lay beneath the table. Ivo's handprint remained in condensation on the metal surface. She wanted to collect both, to preserve every proof that he had existed here, then understood that she was already carrying the part that mattered most to her and that he was not an exhibit.

At the foot of the ramp, the water covered her boots.

Sella walked beside Ivo's chair, still humming. The first turn hid the ward behind them. Mara counted the steps aloud because Jonas had begun to lose his breath and because numbers, for once, were serving the people who made them.


Chapter 18: When the Water Arrived

There were eighty-three steps in the maintenance ramp, interrupted by three sloping sections and two landings. Mara counted the first forty-eight with unreasonable hope, as if the old number might have retained some protective property. Then she counted forty-nine and kept going.

The carrying chair was awkward at the turns. Venn and an orderly took the front; Doss and a second attendant took the rear. They changed positions at each landing. Ivo drifted in and out of awareness, sometimes asking where his hands were although they lay visible on the blanket. Doss answered each time without irritation.

Behind them, water slapped against the lowest steps. Ahead, somebody hammered metal.

Tamsin's voice came down the shaft. "Stand clear of the door!"

A heavy impact followed. Then another. The sound changed on the third blow, becoming broader, and cold outside air moved down the ramp.

Mara had not noticed how warm the cistern was until that air touched her face.

At the upper landing, the first group was gone. Tamsin had sent them with their attendants toward ambulances waiting on the higher road. One advocate remained to guide the last patients. The door hung crookedly from its frame. Its electronic lock was intact; Tamsin had broken the older hinges around it.

"Expensive lock," she said when Mara reached her. "Poor door."

Outside, the power house yard glittered with rain. Wind pushed hard enough to make Sella stagger. A sheet of roofing had fallen across the road used by the first ambulances, bringing electrical lines down with it. The crews had stopped traffic until the lines could be isolated. That route would reopen, but nobody could say how soon.

Mercy waited at the canal steps below the yard.

"We can get to the school landing before the outer tide," Tamsin said. "Nine aboard, including me. The advocate can walk by the upper path. Jonas, help me with the ropes once we're loaded."

They moved Ivo down the short flight to the boat, each step slippery underfoot. Mara boarded first to receive his bag. The engine was already running. Its familiar uneven knock seemed almost intimate after the vast machinery below.

Sella sat in the wheelhouse beside Ivo, refusing the sheltered bench for herself. Doss checked their packs. Venn stood on the aft deck holding the rail. Jonas stowed the records case beneath a seat and took the bow line from Tamsin.

Mara looked back at the power house. Through the open door, she could see the first turn of the ramp. There was no sign of the ward, no visible indication of the sixty-four lives that had occupied the darkness beneath it. The city might have passed over that place for another generation.

"All aboard," the orderly said.

Tamsin eased Mercy into the channel.

At four-twenty-three, the north inlet opened fully.

Orla reported it over the radio, her voice hoarse and precise. The basin took the first controlled overflow. Water crossed the freight yard, slid under doors, lifted empty crates and a child's abandoned plastic hoop. The houses were empty. Mara knew because Yusef and a fire crew had signed the final check together. She also knew that emptiness did not make them worthless.

The boat moved upstream through water thick with debris. Tamsin kept close to the sheltered wall. A floating pallet struck the hull and spun away. Above them, people watched from upper windows. One raised a hand. Sella raised hers in return, a small uncertain gesture that belonged to no announcement.

At the east gate, Jun and Bea held the manual setting. Their radio calls carried effort without drama: gauge reading, time, confirmation. Orla coordinated the next interval using three imperfect observations. Havel repeated each order to the crews. The network's smooth predictions had been replaced by people saying what they could actually see.

Then the third gauge stopped climbing.

Orla waited thirty seconds.

Mara counted with her, silently, beside the wheelhouse.

The east gate moved to its second mark. Pressure shifted through the canal. Mercy rolled sharply enough to throw Venn against the rail. The orderly caught her coat. Tamsin turned into the new current, jaw clenched, and increased power.

A branch struck the wheelhouse window. Glass cracked but held.

Ivo cried out.

Mara went to him. He was trying to stand, his eyes fixed on something beyond the window.

"The south return," he said. "It's wrong. I need—"

Doss kept a hand on his shoulder. "You aren't connected. You're on a boat."

"I can hear it."

"You can hear the engine."

Mara looked at the radio. Every part of her wanted to transmit his warning. Perhaps he retained some extraordinary perception. Perhaps it was a useful fragment of knowledge. The temptation to make him necessary again was almost irresistible.

She asked him what he was hearing.

He tried to explain, then stopped. His face crumpled with confusion.

"I don't know."

"That's all right," she said. "The crews are watching the water. You can sit here."

She did tell Orla that a disconnected patient had expressed concern about the south return, explicitly naming the uncertainty. Orla answered that its local gauge was normal and that the observer would continue checking. The information went into the work without turning Ivo back into an instrument.

By the time Mercy reached the school landing, the eastern sky had begun to pale behind the storm. Volunteers took the carrying chair. Someone put a blanket around Sella's shoulders. Kit appeared at the top of the steps, his splinted hand held against his chest, and shouted at Tamsin for not answering her messages.

"I was driving," she said.

He hugged her with one arm. She closed her eyes for a moment and held him hard.

Inside the school, the assembly hall had become a ward. Beds stood between faded murals of planets. Every station had a lamp, a written name, and a person responsible for it. Mara checked Ivo's arrival with the coordinator, then Sella's. The final eleven had reached the school. Sixty-three participants were alive. Mikel's place had a folded blanket and the cloth bag containing his tea.

His sister sat beside it.

Mara introduced herself and told her what Mikel had asked them to bring. The woman opened the tin, smelled the leaves, and made a sound Mara could not bear to interpret. She did not thank Mara. Mara had not expected her to.

At five-oh-six, the outer surge hit the barrier.

The school windows shook. Conversation stopped across the hall. Even people too exhausted to follow the situation lifted their heads. From the lower city came a roar that seemed to rise through the foundations rather than the air.

Venn stood beside the radio, listening to the crews. A section of the eastern service road had overtopped. The basin was filling faster than expected. The residential wall held. Gate Two needed another adjustment. Orla gave the interval and waited for confirmation.

Mara sat beside Ivo's bed. She could do nothing useful at the gates. Remaining with him felt at once necessary and intolerably small.

He opened his eyes.

"Is it morning?"

"Nearly."

"I don't know where the sea is."

She took his hand, careful of the monitoring line.

"Outside," she said. "Other people are watching it."

The hall lights flickered, recovered, and stayed on.


Chapter 19: The Record of What Happened

The barrier held through the first crest and the smaller one that followed. At seven-thirty, Orla authorized the east gate to begin its slow return. At eight, the water in the basin stopped rising. At nine, people in the school started asking when they could go home, and the answers divided the room in ways the night had briefly concealed.

Some homes were dry. Some had lost power. Some stood in waist-deep water that would take days to drain. The basin houses had absorbed the force intended for the residential wall. Their people were alive, but their mattresses, cupboards, photographs, and floors had paid for that survival.

Yusef arrived at the school carrying the mold folder in a plastic bag. He had added photographs of the new waterline. Mara offered him tea. He asked for the official who could confirm that emergency compensation covered people whose homes had never appeared on a residential register.

She found the solicitor from the Ledger office.

That became the shape of the morning: needs arriving in ordinary voices, no longer willing to wait behind the word emergency. Insulin. A missing dog. A woman who had left her mother's ashes on a shelf. A child whose school uniform was in a house nobody could enter. Mara wrote, directed, carried messages, and returned to Ivo every hour until Doss ordered her to sleep.

She lay on a classroom mat beneath a poster about the phases of the moon. She had not slept for more than an hour at a time in two days. Yet every time she closed her eyes, the ward returned: the water at the stairs, Ivo on the floor, the still map at four o'clock.

Nera sat down beside her with two cups.

"Mine is tea," she said. "Yours is hot water. You've had enough stimulation to alarm a horse."

Mara accepted the cup.

"Mikel died."

"I heard."

"He chose to leave. I keep thinking that sentence should help his sister, and it doesn't."

"It may help her understand what happened. That's different from helping it hurt less."

Nera settled against the wall, stretching her legs. The tremor was worse when she was tired. Water tapped the rim of her cup.

"I want to remember the conversation I had with your brother," she said. "The first one, before I carried his message. I must have liked him. I would like to know why."

"The recording was taken. We may recover it from clinical security."

"And if you don't, don't invent a noble version for me. I might have agreed because I was bored."

Mara smiled despite herself.

She slept for three hours. When she woke, the casualty report had changed twice and settled on four confirmed deaths. Mikel. A man found in a locked workshop below the evacuation line. Two residents whose vehicle had overturned on an access road after the driver attempted to cross moving water. There were injuries, missing-person checks still open, and an uncertainty the coordinator refused to hide behind the word final.

Four was fewer than the worst forecast. It was not a small number to the people counting one of its names.

That afternoon, the Ledger published the casualty list only after families had been notified. It also published the known damage and the unresolved questions about the manual defense plan. Mara appreciated that more than the article praising the rescue, which made several decisions sound cleaner than they had been.

Venn was suspended pending investigation at six that evening. She handed over her access credentials in the school office, beside a cupboard of sports equipment. There was no crowd and no camera. The magistrate read the restrictions; Venn asked three practical questions about preserving operational records; the city engineer took temporary command of flood defenses.

As she left, Venn stopped beside Ivo's bed.

Mara stood, but Ivo raised a hand. He was awake, tired, and capable of deciding whether he wanted this conversation.

"I won't ask you to forgive me," Venn said.

"Good."

She nodded once. "I will testify to the orders I gave."

"All of them?"

"All that I remember. The records should establish the others."

Ivo studied her. "Don't call it a difficult choice every time you say what you did. We know it was difficult. You still had choices we didn't."

Venn looked down. When she left, Ivo turned his face toward the window and asked Mara to close the curtain halfway. The sun was too bright. That was the next thing he needed, and she did it.

Three weeks later, the inquiry opened in the same municipal chamber where they had argued about the gates. This time there were enough public seats. The hearings were transmitted to neighborhood halls, with the medical testimony delayed and redacted where necessary. Patient advocates could challenge disclosure. Several survivors refused to testify publicly, and their refusal was entered without speculation about what it meant.

Mara sat at the witness table with a glass of water she did not drink.

She described the first deposit, the map, the authorization book, her visit to the ward. She distinguished what she had seen from what others told her. When counsel asked about the eleven hours she had surrendered, she said she could not recover them and would not claim to remember events because documents made them plausible.

"Do you consider yourself deceived?" counsel asked.

"Yes."

"Do you consider yourself responsible?"

Mara looked at the public seats. Jonas was near the aisle, his right hand resting on a folded coat. Tamsin sat beside him. Nera had brought a notebook and was writing with an expression that promised trouble for imprecise questions.

"For the decisions I made, yes," Mara said. "Some were made with false information. Some were made because correct procedure was easier for me than attending to the person in front of me. Those should not be treated as the same thing. Both should be examined."

Counsel asked about Rhea Dall.

Mara answered. She gave the name, the registration delay, the hospital's request, her refusal to authorize an exception. She did not turn the account into a speech about how much she had changed. Rhea's daughter sat in the back row. Whatever Mara had learned belonged to Mara; the death belonged first to a family who had not volunteered to become part of her education.

Afterward, in the corridor, the daughter approached her.

"You wrote that you were sorry," she said.

Mara had sent a letter through the inquiry's family liaison, making clear that no reply was expected.

"Yes."

"I'm not ready to answer it."

"You don't have to."

The woman nodded, not warmly, and left. Mara stood still until she had gone. Then she went back into the chamber to listen to the next witness.

The inquiry found that no single hidden order had created the nine-year detention. There had been a chain of renewals, budget decisions, medical accommodations, silenced objections, and convenient interpretations. That did not dissolve responsibility. It located it more precisely. Names were attached to signatures. Warnings were compared with responses. The designation temporary was read aloud often enough to become unbearable.

The investigators recovered Nera's deposit from a sealed cabinet in clinical security. Its label described it as an intercepted communication; the original privacy instruction had been crossed out. A preservation officer tested the filament without replaying its contents, then asked Nera what she wanted done with it. She allowed the inquiry to retain a protected evidentiary copy and asked for a private appointment to hear the conversation herself.

Mara waited outside that appointment. When Nera emerged, she was holding a paper tissue folded into a precise square.

"He asked whether I wanted more soup," she said. "Before the joke. Nobody had asked whether I wanted anything all morning. They'd asked whether I'd taken my tablets and whether I could stand still. He asked what I wanted."

"Is that why you helped him?"

"Partly. Also the soup was terrible. I resented the idea that anybody should have to eat it for another nine years."

She put the tissue in her pocket. Later she chose to keep the experience restored, though the practitioner warned that recollection would remain imperfect. Mara's own destroyed hours could not be returned. They learned to speak about both outcomes without making either woman an example the other had to follow.

Orla testified about the blocked basin. Havel testified about confidence ranges removed from public reports. Doss described patients asking to leave and his own participation in the system that refused them. He retained legal representation and accepted that helping during the rescue did not erase the preceding four years.

Jonas brought the red book to the table. He explained why he had kept it, whom he had tried to show it to, and the point at which he had stopped trying. His voice failed once. He waited, drank water, and continued.

Sella's testimony lasted twelve minutes. She refused to reproduce the tone used in the public announcements when counsel asked whether the voice had been hers.

"You have the recordings," she said. "Today you can listen to this voice."

Mikel's death received a separate review. The medical panel concluded that his underlying heart disease, the strain of emergency transfer, and the conditions of prolonged neural service had all contributed. It could not say with certainty whether remaining in the ward would have kept him alive, particularly after the lower galleries flooded. His sister rejected attempts from both sides to use him as proof that every transfer was either justified or reckless.

"He was my brother," she told the panel. "Do your work properly. Don't turn him into a vote."

By the sixth week, the first interim orders were issued. Involuntary cognitive duty was prohibited. Medical support was separated legally and technically from civic service. A funded program began restoring identities, housing, income, and treatment to the survivors. The basin residents received temporary rent support while their claims were assessed, after Yusef and forty neighbors occupied the compensation office until it accepted records other than property deeds.

None of it moved quickly enough.

Some survivors remained in hospital. Some relationships could not absorb the return of a person long mourned. One man chose to move inland without telling his former family where. Another asked to continue research work under a paid contract and strict limits; the advocates defended that choice against people who wanted every survivor to represent the same lesson.

Mara visited Ivo each evening. They talked about therapy, food, the weather as an external thing. Sometimes they argued. He disliked the way she watched him drink, counting successful ordinary acts as if they were scores on a recovery chart.

"You can just have supper with me," he said one night.

She put down the notebook she had brought to record his questions for the doctor.

They ate in silence for a while. Then he complained that the potatoes were undercooked, and she said they were fine. It was their first completely unimportant disagreement in nine years. Neither surrendered it easily.


Chapter 20: A Room Above the Tide

Seven months after the storm, Ivo rented an apartment on the fourth terrace. It had two rooms, a narrow balcony, and a kitchen tap that knocked whenever somebody upstairs took a shower. The landlord offered to repair the tap before he moved in. Ivo said he would prefer to hear it for a week before deciding.

Mara helped carry his boxes. There were not many. The things from her cupboard, some clothes chosen with the fiercely specific taste of someone tired of institutional blue, a secondhand radio, three books about subjects unrelated to water. He had bought a yellow chair because he liked it, then spent two days worrying that liking it was not a sufficient reason to spend money.

The compensation advance made the apartment possible. The paperwork restoring his legal identity had taken longer than the money. For several weeks, he had been alive for medical purposes, deceased for taxation, and absent for housing eligibility. Jonas helped untangle the records. He referred to the process as a vigorous test of whether the city had learned anything.

It had learned some things. Not enough to make the process pleasant.

Ivo's recovery had no single direction. He could cook, walk to the market, and repair small devices for an hour before fatigue made the work unreliable. He could not tolerate crowded trams. Some mornings the boundaries of the room felt wrong, and he had to touch the doorframe before crossing it. Heavy rain sometimes left him nauseated, searching for information his body no longer received.

He had forgotten the name of his first schoolteacher. He remembered the exact pitch of a failing pump in a district he had never visited. The doctor could explain some of this and was honest about the rest. Ivo liked her because she did not describe every loss as a stage on the way to improvement.

Mara had been offered her old job at the archive, with back pay and an apology carefully written by three departments. She accepted the back pay. She declined the job.

Instead she worked three days a week with the new independent records office, helping people inspect what had been kept, altered, or destroyed under their names. The office occupied two former classrooms. Its furniture was mismatched. Its first useful achievement had been a rule requiring staff to explain a refusal in ordinary language and tell the applicant how to challenge it.

Pei joined on loan from the archive. He brought the green desk lamp and, on his first morning, a paper parcel of buns. Mara's blue cup stood beside it. The objects looked different in a room where the people arriving could decide what questions mattered.

One afternoon, an old woman asked to destroy a deposit of her son's final illness. Mara explained the options: permanent destruction, sealed retention, access restricted to the woman herself. The woman listened and chose destruction. Mara processed the request.

Afterward she sat for a moment with the empty sleeve. Her own missing hours did not entitle her to tell another person which grief to keep. Preserving evidence of institutional acts was one obligation. Preserving every private pain against its owner's wishes would have created another institution she did not want to serve.

She thought about that again when Ivo asked her to accompany him to a clinic.

The clinic was new, independent of the flood department, and inconveniently far from his apartment. He had already attended two consultations. He wanted relief from a seventeen-minute episode during his third year below the city, when a failed test had made him believe he was drowning while lying in a dry bed. The memory returned several nights a week. It did not help him understand what had happened. It simply happened again.

Mara's first feeling was fear.

"Have you considered—" she began, then stopped.

Ivo looked at her across his kitchen table.

"Yes," he said. "I have considered things."

She apologized. He accepted it without making the moment more comfortable than it was.

At the clinic, the practitioner discussed risks, alternatives, and the limits of selective relief. Ivo asked questions. He changed his mind about one boundary and asked for a week before proceeding. Nobody reduced his housing support. Nobody suggested that cooperation would make him a better citizen. Mara sat in the waiting room reading an old gardening magazine and tried to recognize the enormous difference contained in that ordinary delay.

The following week, he proceeded with the treatment. He kept his testimony and the clinical account. He did not keep every sensation. For several nights afterward, he slept badly for different reasons. Recovery remained untidy. The choice remained his.

On the first clear Saturday of autumn, they walked down to the north basin.

The Trade Hall loading platform was gone. The basin had become a permanent part of the flood defenses again, with paths along its upper edge and a protected storage area for market equipment that could be moved quickly. It was less profitable than the old arrangement. The city argued about that at nearly every council meeting.

The former residents had been offered several kinds of compensation. Some took apartments nearby. Others moved inland. Yusef stayed in Bellwether and joined the housing committee, where his collection of dated photographs acquired a reputation that made officials unusually careful around him. He disliked being called an activist. He said he had begun as a tenant and was still trying to finish the original conversation.

Near the basin path, the girl with the fish was riding a bicycle. She recognized Mara and stopped hard enough to skid.

"It had babies," she announced.

Mara blinked. "The fish?"

"We got another fish. Then babies."

"That is a fuller explanation."

The girl considered Ivo, decided he was probably allowed to hear the news too, and described the aquarium in their new apartment. The conversation lasted six minutes and concerned almost nothing except fish. Mara found herself wishing she could remember it exactly, then let that wish pass. Some things could be kept imperfectly and still remain enough.

They continued toward the harbor.

Mercy was in dry dock with a new wheelhouse window. Tamsin was painting the starboard name while Kit, whose fingers had healed crooked but functional, read measurements from a clipboard. He had enrolled in a marine engineering course and now corrected her terminology with intolerable confidence.

"Your influence," Tamsin told Ivo.

"I haven't taught him anything."

"Exactly. No respect for tradition."

She climbed down from the scaffold and embraced Ivo carefully. They had seen each other several times since the rescue, but she still seemed mildly astonished to find him in sunlight. Mara suspected that astonishment would take years to fade, if it ever did.

Tamsin had testified about the gate operation and Rhea Dall. Afterward, she and Mara had eaten at the harbor stall without trying to settle the past between them. They were becoming friends in a way that allowed the old facts to remain present. Sometimes the process was awkward. Mara had begun to trust awkwardness more than sudden absolution.

"Sella's singing tonight," Tamsin said. "Community hall. No civic messages, apparently. She's specified that on the poster."

"We're going," Ivo said.

Mara looked at him. Crowded rooms still exhausted him.

"For as long as I want," he added.

"Of course."

At the hall that evening, Nera had saved seats near an exit. Lena sat beside her with the children. Jonas arrived wearing a jacket Mara had never seen, carrying no bag and no documents. He looked almost unburdened until someone asked about the latest compensation hearing, at which point Nera informed them that bureaucracy could have an evening off.

Venn was not there. Criminal proceedings against her and several senior officials were still underway. She had resigned from public office and testified over four days. The inquiry's report had rejected both the claim that she alone created the system and the claim that necessity removed her responsibility for maintaining it. Mara read the report, then put it away. She did not need to think about Venn every day to want the proceedings completed properly.

Sella came onto the stage in the green scarf. She looked at the audience for a long time before speaking.

"This first song is about a woman who makes a very poor romantic decision," she said. "I chose it because the sea is not mentioned once."

People laughed. Sella waited until they finished, then began.

Her voice was rougher than the recordings. It missed a note, recovered, and found a kind of force the public speakers had never carried. Mara watched Ivo listening. His hands rested loosely in his lap. He was not searching for pressure changes inside the melody. At least, she hoped he was not. She no longer needed to ask him to confirm every hope.

They stayed for four songs. Then Ivo touched her arm, and they left during the applause, quietly enough that Sella would not have to make their departure part of her evening.

Outside, the streets smelled of cooling stone. The tide bells sounded once from the barrier. Their new schedule was public, and the gauges could be read by anyone who wished to climb the observation steps. That did not make the city invulnerable. It made some of its uncertainties available to the people living with them.

At Ivo's door, Mara handed him the small parcel she had carried all evening. Inside were two black cakes from the south-loop bakery and the old birthday card he had drawn before the flood. She had asked whether he wanted the card back. He said yes, then accused her of taking nine years to authorize the drink.

He unlocked the door and went inside. She waited on the landing, giving him the space to decide whether the evening was over.

The apartment contained the yellow chair, an untidy shelf, and the sound of water moving through an imperfect tap. Nothing in it served the city. It was a place where a man could be tired, untidy, unnecessary, and at home.

Ivo turned on the kitchen light.

"Tea?" he asked.

Mara looked at the clock. There was work she had intended to finish. There would always be work she had intended to finish.

"Yes," she said, and went in.
