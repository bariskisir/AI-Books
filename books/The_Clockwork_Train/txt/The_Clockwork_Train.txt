The Clockwork Train
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Timetable

Ada Bell kept the timetable the way other children kept their favourite things — close, and every day.

It had come to her on her sixth birthday, from Gran, who had taken it from its brass case and folded it into Ada's small warm hand. The case was old brass, smooth from a hundred years of thumbs, with a little clasp that opened like a yawn. Inside, the timetable was a folded strip of paper, long as a ruler and thin as a promise, and on it, in Gran's blue ink, were the strange words of the route: Copperwell, the Station of Lost Buttons, the Bridge of Sighs, the Orchard of Wrong Turns, the Sea of Static, the House of Clocks, and, at the very end, the End of the Line.

Ada read it every night before bed. She did not have to — she knew it by heart now — but reading it was part of the keeping, like winding a clock. She would sit on the edge of her bed with the brass case in her lap, unfold the strip of paper, and run her thumb down the stops, one after another, feeling each name like a stone in a stream.

The timetable did not say what time the train ran. Ada had asked Gran about that, once, when she was small. Gran had looked at her with her bright eyes and said, "The train is never late. On time means when it comes — and it always comes when it is needed."

Ada had not quite understood then. She thought she understood now, a little. She was ten. She had been keeping the timetable for four years, and in all that time the train had come on time every year — on the longest night of the year, when the dark was deepest and the stars were closest, the midnight clockwork train ran through Copperwell, along the old track everyone said was gone.

And tonight was the Night.

Ada stood at her window with the brass case held to her chest, watching the ridge where the old track ran, its line of dark against the sky. The frost had written ferns across the glass. Somewhere below, the village was settling into sleep, doors closing, lamps going out one by one.

The timetable was warm in her hand. It seemed to tick.

"It runs tonight," Ada whispered. "The timetable says the train runs."

She felt it in her chest, the way you feel a bell before it is rung — something about to happen, something golden.

Chapter 2: Copperwell

The village of Copperwell was a village of clockmakers, and it kept itself on time.

Stone houses stood along the one street, their roofs dark with frost in winter and bright with moss in summer. In the middle of the village rose the great clock tower, and it was Ada's dad who wound it, every Sunday, with a key as long as his forearm, turning the great brass gears until the tower's hands told the truth again. The tower had four faces, one for each direction, and at night they glowed like four round moons.

Up the hill stood the dead station. That was what everyone called it, though no train had stopped there in living memory. The line was gone — the track pulled up, the sleepers gone to fences, the rails to scrap. All that remained was the little stone station house, and Old Mr. Pendle, who kept the lamps lit out of habit. No one knew why he did it. The lamps burned every night, one in each window, warm and yellow, and when Ada asked him once he had only smiled and said, "A lamp that stays lit is a lamp that's ready."

Ada's home was the clockmaker's shop, where Dad mended clocks and Mum kept the books, and where a hundred clocks ticked at a hundred different times. Ada had grown up among that sound — a soft, uneven rain of ticks, and on the hour, a small storm of chimes as every clock tried to tell its own version of the time at once. Dad said each clock told the time that was true for it, and that was what mattered.

The grown-ups of Copperwell had half-forgotten the train. They remembered it the way you remember a dream you had and didn't have — a brightness at the edge of the picture, gone when you look straight at it. Mum and Dad never spoke of it. Old Mr. Pendle never said he saw it. But both of them kept time, Ada had noticed, and Dad kept the tower wound, and Mr. Pendle kept the lamps lit, and she had come to think that was its own kind of belief, kept in a smaller way.

Bedtime came with the dark. Ada lay under her quilt with the brass case on the nightstand beside her, and Jem, in the little bed across the room, had already made a tent of his blanket. Through the window she could see the ridge, dark against the frosty sky, and the line of the old track running along it like a held breath.

Frost grew on the glass, a white garden in the night. Ada watched it, and thought about the longest night, and about what the timetable said was coming.

Chapter 3: The Night

Ada could not sleep.

She had tried — she had closed her eyes and counted the ticks of the hundred clocks downstairs, and she had counted them twice, and still the sleep would not come. Something in her chest was keeping watch, the way a clock keeps watch between its chimes.

In the bed across the room, Jem rustled. "Ada?" came his small voice out of the dark. "Are you awake?"

"Yes," she whispered. "How did you know?"

"I heard you listening."

Jem was six, and he said things like that, and Ada loved him for them. He scrambled out of bed and climbed in beside her, and they sat together on the windowsill, knees to their chins, watching the frost creep across the glass in ferns and feathers.

The village was asleep below them. The streetlamps stood in their pools of gold. The great clock tower had struck eleven, and now the town waited, tucked under its blanket of dark, for the longest night to reach its deepest point.

"It's the longest night," Ada said, and even saying it made the hairs on her arms stand up, but not from cold.

"I know," said Jem. "That's why I'm awake too. In case anything happens."

"Nothing happens," Ada said, because that was what you said. But her hand went to her chest, to the shape of the brass case under her nightgown, where she had hidden the timetable from bedtime.

And then she heard it.

A whistle. A train whistle, far away over the ridge, so far it might have been the wind in the chimney — but Ada knew the wind, and this was not the wind. This was a note that meant business. This was a note that meant coming.

"Did you hear that?" she whispered.

Jem was very still. "Hear what?"

But Ada was already pulling her boots on, her heart going like a hammer. She looked at her timetable — it was warm in her hand, and it seemed to her that it had grown warmer, and that it was, impossibly, ticking. Not the tick of a clock, but something underneath the paper, like the paper itself remembering its purpose.

"There," she said, and listened. "There it is again. Closer."

And it was closer. Over the ridge, faint as a star, came a long, kind note of a whistle, trailing behind it the softest possible hiss of steam, and Ada thought: it runs. The timetable says the train runs, and it runs.

She looked out at the dark, at the ridge, at the place where the old track everyone said was gone ran like a held breath — and she began to count the minutes until morning, the way a clock counts, faithfully, without hope and without doubt.

Chapter 4: The Train

The first thing Ada saw was the light.

It came over the ridge like a slow sunrise arriving early and in the wrong place — a golden glow, moving, growing, and behind it the darker shape of something huge and patient and full of purpose. Her breath caught and made a cloud on the glass.

"Jem," she said, and her voice came out small and full of wonder. "Jem, come here. Come here now."

He was already there, crowding at the window, and together they watched the Clockwork Train come over the ridge.

It ran along the old track — the track everyone said was gone, the track that was not a track at all, only a line of dark earth and tumbled stone — and yet there the train was, running on it as easily as a needle in a groove. It was brass and steam and lit windows, a whole string of carriages joined like paper dolls, each window a small golden square of warmth, each carriage breathing steam into the cold night air.

And at the front, the great Engine.

Ada had seen her before, every year, from this very window — had seen her from so far away that she was only a shape and a glow. But tonight the Engine seemed closer, or the night seemed clearer, and Ada could see her properly for the first time: the tall brass boiler, warm and shining as a summer kettle, the great wheels taller than a man, the lamps burning at her front like eyes, patient and bright. As Ada watched, the Engine breathed — a long slow out-breath of steam that rose and hung above the ridge, and the sound of it came across the night, huge and soft at once, like a cat purring in the dark.

"Can you see it?" Ada whispered. She did not dare to take her eyes off the train, not even to look at Jem. "Jem — can you really see it?"

"I see it," said Jem, and his voice was round with amazement. "Ada, it's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

And it was. It was a dream made solid, brass and steam and golden windows, and it was coming along the ridge toward the crossing below the hill, and Ada understood with a queer lift of her heart that it was coming for her.

"The track," she whispered. "Everyone says the track is gone."

"It's not gone," said Jem. "It's just — waiting."

The train ran on. The frost on the window glittered gold in its passing light. Ada stood with her hand flat against the glass, and the timetable in its brass case against her heart, and she thought: it has never stopped before. In all the years, in all the nights, the train has run over the ridge and past the village and never once stopped.

But tonight — tonight the Engine was slowing. Ada could see it, could feel it in the way the steam came, softer now, patient, like a visitor taking off her coat.

And the train stopped.

Chapter 5: The Stop

It had never stopped before.

That was the thought that went around and around in Ada's head as she stood at the window, watching the Clockwork Train stand still in the dark below the hill, at the crossing where the old road met the gone track. Steam hung around it in a patient cloud. The golden windows glowed. The great Engine stood quiet, breathing softly, like a horse that has come to the end of its ride.

"It stopped," Ada said. "It's never stopped before."

Jem pressed his face to the glass. "Is it — is it supposed to stop?"

"I don't know. It never has."

They watched. The train waited. And then, at the middle of the train, a door opened. Ada saw a square of warm light appear in the dark side of a carriage, and beyond it the dark shape of the crossing, and beyond that, nothing. No one stepped out.

The door stood open, waiting.

Ada felt the timetable warm against her chest, and she understood — she could not have said how — that the door was for her. That the train, which had run past her window every year of her life, had stopped tonight, on the longest night, because it needed something, and that something had her name on it.

"We have to go down," she said.

Jem looked at her, and she saw the same understanding in his face, only not afraid of it. "Yes," he said. "We do."

Getting out of the house was the kind of adventure Ada had only ever read about. She pulled on her coat over her nightgown, and her boots, and her woolly mittens with the button missing off the left one, and she wrapped the brass case up in her scarf and tucked it inside her coat where it would be safe and warm. Jem put on his coat and boots and, at the last moment, dragged his sack out from under his bed — the old sack he carried everywhere, though Ada had never understood why.

"In case we get lost," Jem said, when he saw her looking.

They went down the stairs on their toes, past the hundred clocks, which ticked on without betraying them. At the door, Ada paused, and looked back at the dark house where Mum and Dad slept, and thought of waking them — and did not. Some things, she was beginning to understand, were for children.

The door opened onto cold and stars. The frost crunched under their boots. Below the hill, at the crossing, the train waited in its cloud of steam, its door standing open, its windows full of gold.

The village stayed asleep. No lamp went on, no window opened, and Ada was glad of it — the night and the train and the crossing belonged to them now.

Ada took Jem's hand. "Come on," she said. "Before it changes its mind."

And together they went down the hill toward the stopped train, toward the open door, toward whatever it was the longest night had been keeping for them.

Chapter 6: The Conductor

The man who stepped off the train wore a dark wool uniform with brass buttons, each one polished to a small warm sun. His face was brass — not a mask, but a face made of warm polished brass, with eyes of amber glass that caught the lamplight and held it, like lanterns. He was the tallest, kindest-looking person Ada had ever seen, and when he spoke, his voice was like steam through a warm kettle: soft, and full of comfort, and carrying a small bright whistle at the back of it.

"Ada Bell," he said. It was not a question.

Ada's mouth had gone dry. She nodded. She was standing on the road at the bottom of the hill with Jem's hand in hers, the frost crackling under her boots, the open door of the carriage glowing behind the conductor, and she had never felt so small or so sure.

"And this is Jem," the conductor said, and his amber eyes went soft. "The one who brings the sack."

Jem beamed. "How did you know about my sack?"

The conductor smiled — Ada could tell, because the lines at the corners of his amber-glass eyes deepened. "I know every child who sees this train," he said. "I know them by the thing they keep. You keep a sack. Your sister keeps a timetable."

Ada's hand went to her chest, to the brass case under her coat. "You know about the timetable?"

"I know it by name," said the conductor. "I have been watching for it for a long time. It was given to you — I know — by a woman who wrote it out in blue ink, in a hand that believed."

Ada felt the words land somewhere in her chest and settle there, warm and heavy as a good stone. "Gran," she whispered.

"Gran," the conductor agreed. He took a step closer, and his brass face bent toward her, and his voice dropped to something softer, graver. "Ada Bell. I have stopped the train tonight. It has never stopped before. And I have stopped it because I need your help."

Ada's heart gave a great, startled knock against her ribs. "My help?"

"There is a clockmaker's daughter in Copperwell who keeps a timetable," said the conductor. "And I have a clock that has stopped. I believe they are the same kind of clock."

He turned, and held out one brass-gloved hand toward the open door, and the light from the carriage fell across him like a tide.

"Come in," he said. "There is something in the Engine I want to show you."

Chapter 7: The Heart

The carriage was warm, and smelled of oil and coal smoke and something like clean frost. But the conductor did not stop in the carriage. He led them through it — past rows of empty seats, past a coat hanging alone on a hook, past a teddy bear with one eye sitting on a bench as if it had been waiting a long time — and down to the front of the train, through a door of brass and glass, and into the cab of the Engine.

It was huge in here, and hot, and alive with gentle sound. Pipes sang. Levers stood at attention. A great wheel of brass turned slowly, turning something unseen. And in the middle of it all stood the firebox, tall as a cupboard, dark.

The conductor laid his brass-gloved hand on the firebox door. "Look," he said, and opened it.

Ada expected heat — the roaring gold of a fire, the brightness the Engine had carried all night over the ridge. Instead the firebox was dark, and cold, and in its heart, where a fire should have been, lay something she had never seen before.

It was a heart made of brass.

It was as big as both of Ada's hands cupped together, and it was beautiful — gears and springs and little wheels, all tangled and nested like the inside of a watch, built to beat. It was shaped, unmistakably, like a heart. And it was still.

Not sleeping. Not resting. Still. The way a clock is still when it has run down. Ada could feel the cold coming off it, the way you can feel the cold off a window in winter.

"Your Gran's clock," said the conductor softly, "wound every Sunday. This heart is wound the same way. Once a year, on the longest night, by a hand that believes. And when a year passes and no hand comes, the heart runs down, a little. It has been running down a long time now, Ada Bell. And tonight —" His voice caught, the way steam catches in a kettle. "Tonight it stopped."

Ada stared at the still brass heart, and something in her own chest ached, the way you ache for a clock you love that has stopped.

"Why is it still?" she whispered. "Why doesn't it wind itself?"

The conductor looked at her with his amber-glass eyes, and in the dim firebox light they were full of something old and patient.

"A clock can only be wound by a hand," he said. "And this heart can only be wound by a hand that believes. The grown-ups of Copperwell have forgotten the timetable. But you, Ada Bell — you kept it. You read it every night. You believed."

He stepped back, and the firebox door swung gently, and the cold stillness of the brass heart seemed to fill the cab.

"Only a child who still believes in the timetable can wind it," said the conductor. "Only a child can put the heart back together."

Ada looked at her timetable — she had taken it out, and it lay open across her mittens, Gran's blue ink true as ever under the warm cab light.

She believed. She had always believed.

She looked up at the conductor. "What do I have to do?"

Chapter 8: The Winding

The conductor sat them down on the low brass seat at the back of the cab, where the Engine's faint warmth came up through the floor, and told them about the winding.

"Every heart has a winding," he said, "and the winding is its own little story. This heart was made whole, once. But a journey like the one this train makes takes a toll. It takes the heart apart, piece by piece, and scatters the pieces along the route. Not to lose them." He smiled. "To keep them safe. To wait."

Jem had his sack open and was arranging his things on the floor of the cab — the map, the biscuits, the marble, the thimble. "Like the things in my sack," he said, delighted. "Kept safe, waiting."

"Exactly like that," said the conductor. He counted on his brass fingers, and Ada counted with him, and this is what he told them.

There were six parts to the heart. Five of them had been scattered along the route, on the night the heart ran down — placed gently, waiting to be gathered. And the sixth part was not lost at all.

"First, the mainspring — the coil of keeping, the part that holds the turning. It waits at the Station of Lost Buttons."

"Second, the escapement — the little wheel that turns sighs into tick-tocks. It waits at the Bridge of Sighs."

"Third, the balance wheel — the wheel that weighs choosing. It waits at the Orchard of Wrong Turns."

"Fourth, the bell — the part that rings. It waits at the Sea of Static."

"Fifth, the chime — the part that sings. It waits at the House of Clocks."

"And sixth," said the conductor, and his amber eyes were very warm, "is the key. The key is never lost. It is the thing a child already has — carried all along without knowing it was a key."

Ada's hand went, by itself, to the brass case at her chest. "I don't understand," she whispered.

"You will," said the conductor. "When we reach the End of the Line."

The End of the Line. The last name on the timetable, the last stop of the route. Ada had read it a hundred times, a thousand — a platform in a field of stars — and never imagined standing on it.

"To wind the heart," said the conductor, "a child must ride the whole route. All the stops. All the parts. And at the End of the Line, put the heart back together, and turn the key."

He looked at her, and his brass face was very kind.

"It will not be a hard journey. There is nothing on this route that is not gentle. But it is a long one, and it asks for believing, all the way through. Only a child can make it."

Ada was quiet. The Engine breathed softly above them, one long slow sigh.

"A whole route," she said at last. "The whole route, tonight?"

"The train runs only on the longest night," said the conductor. "It must return to Copperwell by morning, or the heart will sleep another year."

Ada looked down at the timetable, and the stops on the paper seemed to look back, each one waiting, each one keeping something of hers.

Chapter 9: Ada's Yes

Ada was afraid.

She would have been ashamed of it, except that it was true, and she had promised herself long ago — the year she turned nine, the year Gran's clock ran down for the last time — that she would not pretend things that were true were not true.

"I'm afraid," she said, and her voice came out in a little breath of frost in the cold cab. "A whole route. Six stops. And I've never been anywhere — not even the next village. I'm only ten."

The conductor did not laugh, or hurry her, or tell her not to be silly. He simply waited, patient as a lamp, his amber-glass eyes holding her steady in the dim light.

"That is a very good reason to be afraid," he said. "Being afraid of a journey is how you know the journey is real. Would you like to hear something I know about you, Ada Bell?"

She nodded, not sure she could speak.

"You are afraid," said the conductor, "and you said so. That is the bravest thing a person can do with fear — not to hide it, but to carry it, out into the dark, and bring it home again. I have waited many longest nights for a child who would do that."

The words settled into Ada, warm as the fire the Engine could not make. She thought of Gran.

Gran, who had believed enough to write the timetable down in blue ink, line after line, in a hand that believed. Gran, who had wound the kitchen clock every Sunday, faithful as the week. Gran, who had folded the timetable into Ada's small warm hand on her sixth birthday and said, "Keep this. It runs."

If Gran could believe enough to write it down, Ada could believe enough to ride it.

"Yes," said Ada.

The conductor's amber eyes went very bright. "Yes?"

"Yes," said Ada, and she was surprised by how steady her own voice was. "I'll ride the route. I'll gather the parts. I'll wind the heart at the End of the Line."

The conductor put one brass-gloved hand on her shoulder, and it was warm through her coat. "Then you are the keeper of the route," he said, "and the train will be glad of you."

"And me!" said Jem.

Ada looked down at her brother, who had been so quiet during the telling that she had half-forgotten him, and found him standing with his sack in both arms, his face bright and determined.

"I'm coming too," said Jem. "I can see the train. I've always been able to see the train. And if you're going to ride a whole route in the middle of the longest night, you're going to need somebody who brought biscuits."

"Jem —" Ada began.

"I'm coming," said Jem again, and his voice had a small, absolute weight to it that Ada knew well. It was the same voice he used for I'm not tired and I saw it first.

The conductor laughed — a soft, warm, steam-kettle sound. "Children who can see the train are welcome," he said. "Both of you. Always."

Ada looked from the conductor to her brother, and something in her chest let go, like a spring uncoiling.

"All right," she said. "Both of us."

Chapter 10: Jem

On the platform between the Engine and the Carriage of Lost Things, with the steam hanging like a held breath over the crossing and the frost of the longest night in the air, Jem Bell stood with his sack and looked up at the train he was about to ride.

"Ada," he said. "When I'm grown, I'm going to drive it."

Ada laughed, a surprised, happy sound that surprised even her. "Drive it?"

"Drive it," Jem confirmed. "That's why I'm on the map." He dropped his sack on the platform and knelt, untying its mouth with the serious concentration of a boy doing important work. "I brought everything, in case we get lost."

Out came the map — a page of paper covered in Jem's own marks, a wobbly line for the track, little squares for the stops, a scribble of circles that Ada thought was meant to be the Sea of Static. He spread it flat on the platform, holding down the corners with his boots.

"This is Copperwell," he said, tapping a mark. "And this is the End of the Line." He looked up at Ada, bright-eyed. "See? We can't get lost. It's on the map."

"Jem, you drew that map before we knew about any of the stops."

"Which is why it's a good map," said Jem, rolling it up again. "Maps are for finding out. If you already knew everything, you wouldn't need to go."

Ada had no answer to that. He was six, and he was right.

The biscuits came next, three of them, wrapped in a cloth — the ones Mum had made that morning, smelling of butter and something golden. "Two for now," said Jem, tucking one in his pocket, "and one for emergencies. There's always an emergency."

He laid out his marble — a blue one, with a twist of white inside like a frozen storm — and then, last of all, Gran's thimble.

Ada felt her heart give a small turn. The thimble had been Gran's, silver and worn, and after Gran died Mum had put it in a drawer, and Jem must have found it and kept it, the way Ada kept the timetable. He held it out on his palm like a small silver bell.

"That's for winding," said Jem, solemnly. "In case you need a smaller key."

Ada knelt down on the frosty platform and looked at her brother, and all of a sudden she could not speak, because of the size of the thing in her chest that he had made.

"You," she said, at last, "are the strangest and best little brother in the world."

"I know," said Jem, and put the thimble back in his sack.

The boarding bell rang — a small, bright, brass note that went up into the night like a star rising. Ada stood. Jem stood. The door of the Carriage of Lost Things stood open before them, golden and warm.

Ada held out her hand. Jem took it, sack swinging on his shoulder.

"Together," she said.

"Together," he said.

And they went up the steps into the light.

Chapter 11: The Boarding

The carriage smelled of oil and coal smoke and something older — dust, and the faint sweet smell of forgotten things. It was called the Carriage of Lost Things, and it had the look of a place where everything had come to wait politely for its owner.

A coat sat on the bench opposite, its sleeves folded neatly, one button missing from its front. Beside it a teddy bear leaned, soft brown fur and a single button eye; the other eye was a little hole where something had been loved right off. A kite rested against the window, its paper bright as a poppy, folded as if it had been put away carefully after a long good day. An umbrella stood in the corner, dry as a bone. A hat lay on the seat, waiting to be picked up. And somewhere above them, so faint Ada almost thought she imagined it, a song hummed — a tune she could not quite catch, as if it had been half-forgotten on purpose.

"Lost things," Jem whispered. "All of them. Found things, once."

The conductor had not followed them in. Through the window Ada watched him walk the length of the platform, checking each door, a small lamp in his hand. When he came to the Engine, he touched her boiler the way Dad touched a finished clock — with a hand that already knew it.

Then the bell rang. Not a loud bell, but a warm one, deep, as if the whole train had taken a breath.

"The world is about to begin moving," said the conductor's voice, from somewhere. "Hold on, children. The route is long, and it does not start by stopping."

Jem gripped her hand. The floor did not jerk. The lamps did not flicker. But the frost on the window began to slide upward, and the station began to draw away — slowly at first, like a boat leaving a dock, and then with a sound like a held sigh.

The whistle sounded, long and kind.

Copperwell slid past below the window: the clock tower, dark and gold-lit; Dad's workshop, its windows yellow; the roof under which Mum was asleep; the hill with the old track everyone said was gone, and which the train was, at that very moment, riding.

"Goodbye, Copperwell," Ada said, very quietly, so as not to wake it.

"Goodbye," said the Coat, in a voice like wool. "We'll come back. We always do."

And Ada held her timetable against her chest — warm, real, ticking with her own heart — and the train carried them on into the night, along the route that only children could see.

Chapter 12: The First Stop

The train had been running a long time — or perhaps a short time; in the Carriage of Lost Things, time kept itself the way the passengers did, politely, without fuss. Ada had dozed against the window with Jem asleep on her shoulder, and when she woke, the light outside had changed. The night had gone grey and small, and ahead of them, where no station had a right to be, a platform stood in the dark, lit by a hundred tiny lamps.

The lamps were buttons.

"Jem," Ada said. "Jem, wake up. It's the first stop."

Jem opened his eyes and pressed his nose to the glass. "It's all buttons," he said, and he was right. The platform glimmered like a snowfall at dusk, scattered from one end to the other with buttons — mother-of-pearl and brass, brown bone and blue glass, round and square and one that was shaped like a star. Some lay singly; some lay in drifts where the wind had pushed them into heaps. It looked as if the whole world had buttoned itself up too fast, and everything that had popped off had landed here.

The train slowed. The button-lamps swung gently, each on its own little thread, and as the wheels came to rest, a sound ran through the station like a breath — the soft small clatter of ten thousand buttons shifting, as if the platform itself were waking.

"Never a button lost in Copperwell?" said the Coat, stirring on its bench. "Oh, there have. There have been many." It said this with great dignity, and its one empty buttonhole seemed to point at the platform like a finger.

The conductor was at the door. He did not hurry; he never hurried. He stood with his brass hand on the frame and looked out at the glimmering platform, and his amber eyes were warm.

"The Station of Lost Buttons," he said. "First stop on the route of the heart. Come and see what the world has kept for you."

Jem hopped down first, because Jem was always first, and the buttons crunched under his boots like frost. "Ada," he said, bending down. "Ada, come look. There's a button here that's been waiting."

And Ada stepped down onto the platform, into the small lamps and the button-snow, and the door of the train closed softly behind them, like a book shut on a bookmark.

Chapter 13: The Lost Buttons

There were buttons from coats, and buttons from cuffs. There were small pearly buttons from pyjamas, the kind that go soft after a hundred washes, and round brown buttons from cardigans, and tiny bright ones that had fallen off dolls. There were buttons from the ends of sleeves and the tops of trousers and the backs of dresses where the wearer never even saw them go. The Coat had stepped down from the train to look, and it walked slowly among the drifts, its empty buttonhole forward, like a nose.

"Every one," it murmured. "Ever lost. Even mine. Somewhere."

"Find it," said Ada. "Your button. It's here."

The Coat searched. It shuffled through the mother-of-pearl, the brass, the blue glass. It looked under the heaps and along the edges of the platform. It lifted drift after drift, and the buttons ran through its wool like water. Jem helped, then Ada, and then the three of them stood in the middle of the platform, and the Coat was quiet.

"It's not here," it said, and its voice had gone small, the way voices do when something lost has been lost for a very long time.

"Wait," said Ada.

She had been noticing something, the way she noticed things — Gran's old habit, to look at what was right there. In the middle of the platform, where the drifts were deepest, the buttons lay in a low hill. But at the very centre of the hill, one button was not lying in the drift. It was standing on its edge, upright, as if it had been placed there on purpose, and had been waiting all this time.

It was a brass button, plain and warm, with four holes and a little wear around the rim — the kind of button that has been held between a thumb and a forefinger a thousand times. It was not lost. It was waiting.

"Ada," Jem whispered.

The Coat came and looked. It did not say anything, but it reached out one wool sleeve, and its buttonhole and the button met as if they had always known each other — and the button slipped in and settled, and the Coat stood up straight, a different Coat, complete.

And in the moment it settled, the button-hill opened. Gently, like a hand unclosing, the buttons parted in a ring around the standing button, and at the heart of the hill lay a coil of bright brass — a spring, wound tight and shining, the size of Ada's fist.

"The mainspring," said the conductor softly. "The coil of keeping. It has been kept here — in the one place the world forgets — by the one button that was never lost, only waiting."

Ada knelt. The mainspring was warm, and when she closed her hand on it, she felt something in her own chest give a little turn, the way a clock feels when the key has found it.

"Part one," said the conductor. "Four more, and the key. Well done, keeper."

As the train pulled away, the station rose up behind them and rang — ten thousand small bright sounds, like a thousand tiny bells, each one saying goodbye to a thing that had been found.

Chapter 14: The Second Stop

Back in the Carriage of Lost Things, Ada sat with the mainspring in her lap, wrapped in her handkerchief, and the train ran on through the dark. Outside the windows the world had changed again. The land had gone pale and soft, as if it were made of breath, and the mist lay over everything in low grey folds.

The Coat sat very straight, its new button catching the lamplight, and it hummed a little — the same half-tune that lived somewhere above the carriage, the Song, as if the two of them had always travelled together. The Teddy had woken up. It watched the windows with its one eye, the way an old sailor watches the sea.

"Jem," said Ada. "What are you doing?"

Jem had taken something from his pocket and was examining it closely, one eye squinted shut.

"Found a button," he said. "When we got off. It's a good one. Brass." He held it up. "Do you think it's a biscuit?"

"No," said Ada.

"Are you sure?"

"Jem. It is a button."

"Right," said Jem, and put it away, disappointed. "But it's a nice one," he added, to the button. "Don't tell anyone you were almost eaten."

The Coat laughed a woolly laugh. The Umbrella, in its corner, creaked once, which was its way of laughing too — it had never opened, and so it laughed the way a folded thing does, all at once and then gone.

The mist deepened. The train slowed, the way it had slowed at the button station, and through the window Ada saw the shape of it ahead: a bridge, rising out of the fog, its iron arches dark and enormous, hanging over a valley so full of grey that its bottom could not be seen at all.

And the bridge was sighing.

Not creaking. Not groaning. Sighing — a long, soft, human sound, as if the whole valley were letting out its breath, over and over. It rose and fell against the carriage windows, and Jem stopped smiling, and Ada felt a small cold place open in her chest. Not fear — she was not afraid — but something else. Something like listening.

"The Bridge of Sighs," said the conductor, and his voice was gentle. "Second stop on the route of the heart. Every sigh the world has ever let out collects in this valley, and the bridge has carried them all. It is a heavy cargo. Come and help it breathe."

The train stopped. The bridge went on sighing, patient and enormous, all around them.

Chapter 15: The Bridge of Sighs

The bridge was not spooky. That was the strange thing. It was huge and old, its ironwork laced with frost like lace, and the fog lay thick beneath it — but when Ada stood on it, with Jem's hand in hers, she felt no shiver. She felt something more like pity. The bridge had been sighing for so long that it had forgotten it was a bridge. It thought it was a place where sighing happened.

Every sigh the world had ever let out had come here, the conductor said. Sighs over lost buttons and lost letters. Sighs for friends who moved away. Sighs of grown-ups at the end of long days, and of children pretending to be asleep. Sighs that meant I wish I had said something, and sighs that meant I am so happy I do not know what else to do. They had drifted down into the valley and settled, and the fog was them — the world's collected breath, waiting to be let go.

"I wish," said Jem, "we could do something about it."

"Sometimes you can," said the conductor. He led them to the middle of the bridge, where a small stone house stood — the toll-house, where once a keeper had taken a coin from every traveller. The door was open. Inside, on a shelf, a plain wooden clock ticked.

"Look," said Ada.

She had noticed, the way she noticed things. The clock was ticking. It had never stopped — in a valley of sighs, one small clock kept time. Its face was worn, its hands were brass, and it ticked as steadily as a heart.

"The escapement," said the conductor, "is the little wheel inside that turns a clock's motion into a tick and a tock — one step, then the next. This clock's escapement is the strongest in the world. It has to be. It ticks against a valley full of sighs."

"But it's slowing," said Ada.

The tick had begun to drag, soft and tired, like a walker at the end of a long road. The clock had kept time for so long, against so much weight, that it was running down.

"It has never been wound by a hand that believed," said the conductor. "Only by duty. And it is tired of duty."

There was a small brass key on a hook beside the clock. Ada took it down. It fitted the clock's face as if it had been made for it, and she turned it — once, twice, three times — the way Gran had wound the kitchen clock every Sunday, slow and sure, in time.

And the clock's tick straightened. It grew firm and bright, and as it did, the fog began to change. The sighs stopped sighing. They began, very softly, to tick — the valley full of grey breath found the clock's rhythm, and the whole bridge began to keep time.

"Part two," said the conductor. "The escapement — the wheel that turns sighs into tick-tocks." And the Bridge of Sighs, for the first time in its long history, sighed once — a sigh of relief — and then held its breath, and listened.

Chapter 16: The Third Stop

Beyond the bridge, the fog thinned and the train climbed. The track ran up into woods, and the woods were the kind of woods that have a hum in them — not a sound so much as a feeling, as if the trees were thinking very slowly about very old things. Through the windows the trunks stood dark and patient, and the train's lamps threw long gold paths ahead.

Jem sat with his face nearly flat against the glass, the way he sat when he was looking for something. The Coat was quiet, but its new button shone, and the Teddy had moved to the other window, as if it, too, wanted a better view.

Then the track forked.

Ada felt it before she saw it — the slight hesitation in the rails, the way a path pauses when it must choose. Ahead, the single track divided in two, each branch curving away into the dark. The branches were exactly alike. Both were mossy, both were lined with the same patient trees, both led out of sight.

"The train always takes the wrong turn here," said the conductor. He had come into the carriage without a sound, the way he did everything. "Every time. It is part of the route."

"Why?" asked Jem.

"Because if you never take the wrong turn, you never learn what the right turn feels like. And because the wrong turn is where something of childhood is kept." He nodded at the window. "Look."

The train took the left-hand branch — the wrong one, the conductor said — and the woods opened, and the Orchard of Wrong Turns appeared.

It was hung with light. The trees stood in rows, heavy with fruit that glowed from inside — soft gold, and green, and a warm pink like the inside of a shell. Each fruit hung on its branch like a little lantern, swaying as if it were remembering something. The orchard smelled of autumn and apples and the last day of summer, all mixed together, and of something else that Ada could not name.

"What are they?" she whispered.

"Every road not taken," said the conductor. "Every left turn when you went right. Every 'almost' and every 'next time.' Each fruit holds one. Choose carefully, if you choose at all — a wrong-turn fruit, once picked, tastes exactly like what you almost did."

Jem was already at the door.

Chapter 17: The Orchard of Wrong Turns

The orchard was quiet in the way of places where a great many things have almost happened. The trees whispered as Ada and Jem walked among them — soft, sifting whispers, like leaves deciding something they would never quite decide. Would-have-beens. Almost-sos. If-onlys. The words did not quite form, but Ada understood them anyway, the way you understand a tune before you know the words.

Jem stopped before a tree whose fruit hung lowest, a fruit of warm gold the size of his fist, glowing like a small held candle.

"Can I pick one?" he asked. "I came prepared." He swung his sack off his shoulder. "I have a map. I have biscuits. If the fruit is a road not taken, I want to see where I didn't go."

The conductor made no move to stop him. "Every child picks one," he said. "It is part of the route. The fruit will taste exactly like the road you did not take — whatever you most nearly did, and turned away from. Are you sure you want to taste it?"

Jem was sure. Jem was always sure. He reached up, and the fruit came off in his hand as gently as a held breath, and he bit into it.

For a moment nothing happened. Then Jem's eyes went very wide, and he stood quite still, and the gold light of the fruit ran over his face.

"What does it taste like?" Ada asked.

"Almost," said Jem, around the bite. His voice was strange and soft, the way it got when he was very far away inside himself. "It tastes like almost." He swallowed, and something flickered across his face — not sadness, exactly. Wonder, and a little loss. "I almost didn't come tonight," he said. "I almost let you go by yourself. It tastes like that." He looked down at the fruit, then at Ada, fiercely. "I'm glad I didn't."

"And there," said the conductor softly, "is the orchard's gift. You can always turn back. The wrong turn is never wasted — it is the thing that shows you the right road, by being the road you leave."

The trees had gone still, listening. And out of the First Tree — the oldest, the one whose branches bent lowest over the track — a branch lowered itself, and at its end, instead of fruit, hung a small brass wheel, fine as a watch part, with a little axle through its middle. It swung slowly, weighing, choosing.

"The balance wheel," said the conductor. "The wheel that weighs choosing. The pendulum of the heart." He took it down and held it out, and Ada closed her hand on the cool brass. "Part three. Two more, and the key."

Behind them, the orchard rustled — but softly, like a whole forest of almosts saying you may leave, and we will keep your road warm for you.

Chapter 18: The Passengers

The night had grown long and the train had grown warm, and somewhere between the orchard and the next stop, the Carriage of Lost Things came alive with talk. It was the kind of talk that starts small, the way fires start, and ends with everyone remembering.

The Coat went first. It had been waiting all night to tell someone, now that it had its button back.

"It was a windy day," it said, in its woolly voice. "The windiest day of that whole winter. I was hung on a hook in the hall, minding my own business, when the door was flung open — the little girl's father, in a great hurry — and the wind came through and took my button like a thief takes a penny. I saw it go. It went down the path and over the wall, and I could not chase it, being a coat. I have thought of it every day since. The same four holes. The same warm brass. The way it fit." It touched its new button with a sleeve, and was quiet, and everyone was quiet with it, politely, until the Teddy spoke.

"I saw the train first," said the Teddy. "One eye is enough, if it's the right eye. I was on a windowsill, and I saw it come over the ridge like a brass comet, and I told the little girl, and she said — the little girl said —" The Teddy stopped. "She grew up," it said. "She grew up and she stopped seeing trains. But I never did. That's why I'm here."

The Kite unfolded a little on its bench. "I flew once," it said. "One perfect afternoon. The wind was exactly right, and the sky was exactly blue, and I went up so high I could see the whole town and the river and the two hills and the cloud I almost touched. That was enough. Some things are enough the first time. I'm not sorry. I'm here because I'm glad."

The Umbrella creaked. "I was for rain," it said, "that never came. I have never been opened. I am not sad about it — a dry umbrella is a happy umbrella — but I like to be where the weather is interesting."

"Quite," said the Hat, who had blown off somebody's head and had been chasing that somebody ever since, gently, across the world. "Quite."

And the Letter, folded and sealed and never sent, sat very straight and said: "I am still kind. That is all a letter can be, if it never goes. I hold my kindness for someone who will one day need it."

The Song hummed above them all, half-remembered, half-whole.

And through the window, as if it had heard them, the Engine blew a great plume of steam — and the steam held, and formed, and hung over the carriages in the shape of a heart.

Chapter 19: The Fourth Stop

The talk had not quite finished when the hissing began.

It was faint at first, like rain heard from inside a house — so soft that Ada thought it was the wind in the trees. But the train was leaving the trees behind, and the sound grew with the dark, and it was not a wind sound at all. It was a hiss. A grey, patient, endless hiss, as if the whole night had been tuned to a station that played nothing.

The passengers went quiet. The Coat drew its new button close. The Teddy pressed its one eye to the window and said, in a small voice, "Oh."

Ahead of them, where the land should have been, the world had gone grey. It was a sea — Ada could see it now — a sea of grey, heaving slowly, and its waves did not splash. They hissed. Each wave broke and fell back in a long static breath, and where water should have been silver, it was the grey of a radio that has found nothing.

"It's like the air between stations," whispered Jem. "When you turn the dial and there's nothing but noise." He had an old radio at home that Mum kept on the shelf; he knew its sound better than he knew most songs.

The conductor had come to stand at the front of the carriage. For the first time, his face was not patient. It was serious — the brass set in lines that Ada had not seen before. He watched the grey sea, and the hiss rose, and for a moment she thought he was afraid, and she felt the fear start in her own chest, small and cold, and she did not look away from it.

Then his face softened. The lines eased, the amber eyes warmed, and he said, gently, to the sea: "You are only the half-remembered. You are only the songs that were drowned out. You have been carrying them a long time. Tonight we are going to cross you, and you are going to give us back one of them."

He turned to the children. "The Sea of Static," he said. "Fourth stop on the route of the heart. It holds every half-forgotten tune, every song that was never finished, every note that got lost in the noise of the world. And somewhere in it — kept, not lost — is the bell."

"The train can't cross water," said Jem.

"The train doesn't cross it," said the conductor. "The ferry does. Look."

Ahead, out of the hiss, a shape was coming — long and low and made of metal, glittering with a hundred thin prongs. It was a ferry made of tuning forks.

Chapter 20: The Sea of Static

The ferry of tuning forks was the strangest vessel Ada had ever seen. It was long and flat, its deck a forest of silver prongs standing upright, each quivering in the grey air, humming a note too small to hear unless you listened with the whole of yourself. The train rolled gently off the track onto the ferry, as easily as stepping from one room to another, and the ferry took them up.

"Every half-forgotten song lives here," said the conductor. "Every tune that was drowned out — by noise, by time, by other songs. The ones you almost remember. The ones your mother hummed when you were small, that you know but cannot quite call up. They are all here, in the static, kept."

The hiss rose around them. It was not unkind, but it was heavy — the weight of a thousand songs that could not quite be sung. Jem sat very still, and the Coat pulled its sleeves over its hands, and the Teddy's one eye grew dark.

The ferry slowed. The hiss thickened, grey as wool, pressing the windows. The prongs began to lose their shine, one by one, as the static found them and the crossing faltered.

"We're stuck," whispered Jem. "It's too heavy. It's all the songs, and none of them can finish."

Ada sat forward. She had been noticing something, the way she noticed things. In all the grey, in all the half-songs and drowned tunes, there was one tune she knew completely. Not half anything. Whole, and hers.

Gran had hummed it every Sunday, winding the kitchen clock — the plain wooden clock that never stopped, the one still at home, ticking, waiting for a hand. She had hummed it so often that it had gone in like a key, and it had never, not once, been lost.

"The song," Ada said. She stood up. "It's not in the static. It's in me."

She put her hands on her knees, the way she did at the window, and she whistled. It was Gran's song — the Sunday one, the kitchen-clock one, the one that meant the world was keeping time and all was well. She whistled it through the grey, one clear line of sound, and the static heard it, and the static knew it, and the static began to part.

Not violently. Gently, the way fog parts for a path. The hiss fell away in a widening lane of clear air, and the ferry's prongs woke, all a-hum together, and the whole sea began to sing — not the half-songs, but the song, whole and sure.

And out of the sea, in the middle of the parting, the bell rose.

It came up slow and golden, huge and quiet, a bell that had been kept at the bottom of every half-forgotten song, and as it broke the surface it rang once — one clear, pure note — and the note held.

"Part four," said the conductor, and his voice was the softest Ada had ever heard it. "The bell. It was never lost, child. It was waiting for a tune that knew itself."

The bell, warm and ringing, settled on the deck of the ferry beside her. One stop more, and the chime. And then the End of the Line.

Chapter 21: The Fifth Stop

Beyond the Sea of Static the land rose up again, and the grey hiss fell away behind the train like a sound being folded shut. Ada sat at the window of the Carriage of Lost Things with the bell's single clear note still warm behind her teeth, and watched the light change. It was not night and it was not morning; it was the colour between, the grey of a shell's inside, and somewhere ahead a real dawn was trying to be born.

The valley opened below them, wide and gentle. Rooftops with crooked chimneys, and from every chimney a ribbon of smoke going up straight as a needle, and under the smoke, in the half-dark of the streets, the small fires of people waking. Ada pressed her face to the cold glass. It smelled of frost and coal smoke and, very faintly, of bread.

"The Sea of Static is behind us," the conductor said softly. "What you are seeing now is the land of clocks." His amber-glass eyes were warm in the dawn-grey. "Every clock in the world that ever stopped is kept here."

Ada looked where his brass hand pointed, and there it stood on a hill above the rooftops, alone and patient: a house. It was not large, but it was the tallest thing in the valley, and it seemed to lean a little toward the train, the way a person leans when they have been waiting a long time and mean to be ready. Its windows were dark, and its door was shut, and yet —

"Listen," said the conductor.

Ada listened. Jem, beside her, stopped mid-bite of biscuit and listened too. And out of the house, across all that dawn-grey quiet, came the sound of ticking. Not one clock. Not many. A hill of it, a houseful, a whole valley breathing in tick and out tock, soft as snow falling on snow.

"The House of Clocks," said the conductor. "Where everything that stopped is kept, until a child comes to start it again."

The train slowed. The wheels changed their song. And the house, on its hill of its own ticking, opened its door.

Chapter 22: The House of Clocks

Inside, the House of Clocks was full of time the way a hive is full of bees — a sweetness of it, a hum of it. Stopped clocks stood on every shelf and sill: a grandfather clock whose hands were frozen at a wedding, a tin clock shaped like a moon, a carriage clock no bigger than a walnut, a clock with no hands at all, only the small round hole where a hand had once turned. Each one held its moment the way a jar holds a catch of fireflies. Ada walked slowly down the middle of the room, and as she passed each clock she knew, without being told, what it was holding.

There was one stopped at a first step — a tiny wooden clock, and the moment it held was so bright and wobbly that Ada could almost see the small foot coming down onto the floor, could almost hear the triumphant cry. Jem saw it too. He reached out one careful finger and touched the glass.

The hands of the little clock moved. One tick. One tock.

Jem stared at his finger as if it had done something marvellous. "I did that."

"You woke it," Ada said. "It was only waiting for somebody to come near enough."

The conductor stood in the doorway with his hands behind his back, saying nothing, letting them look. Ada understood, suddenly, that this was the point of the house. Not to keep the stopped moments locked away. To keep them safe — the way Gran had kept the timetable safe — until the right hand came.

At the far end of the room, on a plain pine shelf, stood one clock that was not stopped. It was ticking. It was a plain wooden clock, the plainest thing in the whole house, with a little glass door and a face of white paper and hands painted Gran's blue — and Ada knew it before the conductor said a word. It was Gran's clock. The one from Gran's kitchen. The one Gran wound every Sunday, humming her song, while the kettle breathed and the light came in.

"This is the clock that never stopped," said the conductor. "It is the fifth part of the heart. Its name is the Chime."

Ada took it down. It was warm, as if Gran had wound it that very morning. The blue hands said a time Ada could not read, and she did not need to.

Behind her, the stopped clocks were beginning to tick. One by one, all down the room, like rain finding a roof. The grandfather clock struck a single low note, then a carriage clock, then the tiny wooden one — and the whole house, for the first time in years, was keeping time.

Jem laughed out loud. "They're all waking up!"

Ada carried Gran's clock to the door, the Chime safe and warm in her hands, and the ticking followed her down the steps like a flock of small good birds.

Chapter 23: The Sixth Stop

The train climbed out of the valley of clocks, and the morning climbed with it. The track straightened itself like a long breath let out; the dawn-grey brightened into real light, pale and kind. And the conductor, who had been quiet since the House of Clocks, grew quieter still, and kinder, until his brass face seemed to hold a light of its own.

Ada sat with the Chime in her lap, wrapped in a cloth from Jem's sack, and felt the other parts of the heart riding safe in the conductor's buttoned pocket — the mainspring, the escapement, the balance wheel, the bell. Five parts. She counted them on her fingers the way she counted the stops on her timetable, and the number felt right, like the last stitch in a hem.

"The track is straight now," Jem observed, his nose pressed to the glass. "Are we nearly at the end?"

"Nearly," said the conductor. "The last stop is not far."

"Does it have a name?" Ada asked.

The conductor considered. "It has many names," he said at last. "Children call it the End of the Line. The train calls it Home. And the timetable calls it the place where everything waits."

Ada drew out the timetable. It was warm, and the paper ticked under her thumb. She unfolded it to the last line of the route, where the ink was freshest and the words were fewest, and read them to herself. The End of the Line. The place where everything waits.

The train rounded a shoulder of the ridge, and the sky ahead went strange and wonderful. The morning had not reached it. The stars were still out, hanging so low that they seemed to be standing in the grass like lanterns on sticks — hundreds of them, a whole field of them, quiet and lit, waiting. And in the middle of the field, on a platform of pale stone, stood the smallest station house Ada had ever seen, with one round window burning gold.

"Everything waits here," said the conductor. "The stars. The station. And inside, the heart's case — open, and empty, and ready."

The train slowed. The wheels sang their gentlest song. And the field of stars turned, slowly, to watch them come in.

Chapter 24: The End of the Line

The platform was pale stone, swept clean, and the stars stood in the grass on either side like a guard of small lamps. Ada stepped off the train first, with Jem's hand in hers and the Chime wrapped in the cloth, and the ground was so quiet under her boots that she felt the whole night's journey catch its breath behind her.

The station house was no bigger than Gran's kitchen had been, but its round window burned gold, and its door stood open, and everything about it said come in, come in, we have been waiting.

Inside there was one room. A lamp. A chair. A table. And on the table, the heart's case.

Ada had seen the heart in the Engine's firebox, still and cold, and she had not forgotten it. But she had never seen it whole. It lay open now, a brass heart the size of a loaf, its two halves swung back on their hinges like doors, and inside it the empty places where the parts belonged — a hollow for the mainspring, a cradle for the escapement, a cup for the balance wheel, a nook for the bell, a slot for the chime. Five empty places, shaped as precisely as her own palm.

"Put them home," said the conductor softly. "The journey gave them to you. The heart has been waiting for them."

Ada set the Chime down on the chair, unwrapped the cloth, and took out the mainspring — the coil of keeping, still faintly warm from the button-hill. It settled into its hollow with a small, satisfied click. The escapement, the little wheel that turned sighs into tick-tocks, fitted its cradle and was still. The balance wheel from the First Tree rocked once, as if weighing something, and came to rest. The bell from the Sea of Static rang a single soft note and was quiet. And last, the Chime, Gran's clock, plain and blue-handed, slipped into its slot as if it had been there always.

The heart was whole.

Ada stood looking at it, and the whole was so much more than the parts that she could not find words. It was beautiful. It was still. It was so still that the stillness felt like a held breath, like the moment before the first note of a song.

The conductor reached into his pocket and brought out the key. It was a small brass key, unremarkable, warm as if it had been held a long time. He held it out, and Ada took it, and it fitted her palm as if it had been measured for her.

"It was never lost," the conductor said. His voice was very gentle. "It has been waiting for a hand that believes."

Ada closed her fingers around the key. The heart lay on the table, whole and still, and somewhere out in the field, one star leaned down a little closer to watch.

Chapter 25: The Heart

The heart was whole now, and still, and beautiful, and Ada stood before it with the key in her hand and did not know what to do. She had pictured winding it a hundred times — turning and turning, the way Dad turned the key in the great clock tower — and now, with the key actually warm against her palm, all her pictures felt wrong. The heart was not a clock tower. It was smaller, and quieter, and it seemed to be waiting for something other than strength.

"The truth of winding," said the conductor, "is not strength."

Ada looked up. He had come to stand beside her, and his amber-glass eyes were soft.

"Turning a key hard is not winding," he said. "Anyone can turn a key hard. But the heart has a rhythm of its own. It has kept that rhythm since before the first train ran, and it will keep it after the last. Your job is not to turn the key to the heart. Your job is to turn the key in time with the heart."

Ada looked down at the brass heart. Its stillness felt like a held breath, and now she understood what the breath was. It was the heart holding its own beat, waiting for somebody to fall into step with it.

"Find the rhythm," said the conductor. "It is not in your hands. It is in your chest."

Ada closed her eyes. The room was very quiet. The lamp burned, the field of stars waited outside, and Jem stood very still by the door, holding his breath for her. And Ada felt, under all the quiet, something like a pulse — not in the room, not in the table, but in her own chest, where the beat of her small heart had been going all along, keeping time without once being asked.

She thought of Gran's kitchen clock, wound every Sunday. She thought of Dad's workshop, where all the clocks ticked together and somehow agreed. And she thought of the timetable, warm under her thumb, ticking like a paper heart.

Tock, whispered the heart. Or maybe it was her own heart, saying the same thing. Tock. Tock. Tock.

Ada opened her eyes. She fitted the key into the small brass hole in the side of the brass heart, and it went in as easily as a needle into cloth, as a key into the door of a home.

"Whenever you're ready," said the conductor.

Ada was ready.

Chapter 26: The Rewinding

The key turned with a sound like a small bell — once, and the mainspring, the coil of keeping, took hold somewhere deep in the brass and began, very gently, to uncoil. Ada did not turn it hard. She turned it in time, in the rhythm she had found, in step with the tick of her own heart. Twice, and the escapement, the little wheel from the Bridge of Sighs, gave one small click and began its endless tick-and-tock, turning sighs into time. Three times, and the balance wheel from the First Tree swung once, twice, weighing something invisible, and settled into its swing.

The bell in its nook rang a single soft note. The chime in its slot hummed, once, like Gran humming at the kettle.

And then the heart beat.

It was not loud. It was deep and slow, a brass heartbeat, ba-dum, ba-dum, from somewhere inside the beautiful joined machinery, and Ada felt it in her hands, up her arms, in her chest, where her own heart answered it — ba-dum, ba-dum — keeping time with the heart of the train.

"Ah," said the conductor, very softly. "There it is."

Ada kept turning the key until it turned no more, gentle and sure, the way you finish winding a clock by feel. And when she took her hand away, the heart was beating on its own, deep and slow and golden, and she knew she had done it. She had wound the heart of the train.

The Engine's firebox, out on the track, glowed gold through the station window. The train shivered — Ada felt it through the floorboards, a shiver of pure joy, the way a cat stretches when it is happy. And up over the field of stars, the Engine blew a great plume of steam, and it held its shape in the cold air, and the shape it held was a heart.

Jem came running out of the station house. "It's alive!" he shouted, and his voice cracked with delight. "The train's alive, Ada — everyone can hear it, the stars can hear it!"

And it was true. The heart was beating under the rails, under the platform, under the field of stars, and every star in the grass was trembling, just a little, in time.

Ada stood in the doorway with the key in her hand and the whistle of her own pulse in her ears, and watched the steam heart hang over the world, and knew that this was the most beautiful thing she would ever see.

Chapter 27: The Return

The return journey was the same route, and it was not the same at all.

The train left the End of the Line like a bright idea leaving a dark room. The field of stars slid past the windows, each star winking as it went, and the Engine blew a small steam heart at the last of them, a goodbye, a thank-you. Then the valley of clocks, and the House of Clocks on its hill — and the house did not just tick now. It chimed them past, every clock in it ringing out, a whole hill of music, and Ada leaned out of the window and waved.

The Sea of Static met them singing. The grey hiss had turned to melody — half-forgotten tunes, every one of them found again — and the tune it sang, over and over, soft as a lullaby, was Gran's song. Ada did not have to whistle it this time. The sea remembered it for her.

The Orchard of Wrong Turns glowed as they passed, every fruit alight, and Jem pressed his face to the glass and laughed at his own reflection. "The right turn," he said, "was the wrong one all along," and the trees seemed to nod.

The Bridge of Sighs did not sigh. It hummed, low and contented, and the fog under it ticked like a thousand soft clocks, and the toll-house clock called after them as they crossed, keeping time.

And the Station of Lost Buttons rang like sleigh bells as the train flew through — every button on the snowy platform singing out, a thousand tiny bells, a Christmas of sound. The Coat stood at the window of the Carriage of Lost Things with its found button shining, and it was not the only passenger who seemed brighter. The Teddy's one eye was wet. The Kite flew at the end of its string inside the carriage, once, twice, a perfect little afternoon.

The passengers sang. Ada had not known the half-remembered Song had words, but now she heard them, and they were about trains, and children, and the longest night.

Jem fell asleep against Ada's arm somewhere past the orchard, his biscuit-crumbed cheek warm, Gran's thimble safe in his sack. Ada sat very still so as not to wake him, felt the heart beating under the floorboards beneath her feet — ba-dum, ba-dum, on time, on time — and watched the dark outside begin, very far ahead, to turn the colour of morning.

Chapter 28: The On-Time Arrival

Morning came slowly, the way it comes to children who have stayed up all night — shy at first, then all at once. The dark at the windows went grey, the grey went gold, and Ada, who had not slept, watched the gold deepen and climb until it spilled over the last ridge.

And then there was Copperwell.

It lay below them in the new light, small and asleep: the crooked roofs, the chimneys, the great clock tower Dad wound, the huddle of stone houses around the market square. The frost was on everything, white and clean, and the smoke went up from the chimneys like prayer. Ada's heart, which had been keeping time with the train's all night, gave one extra beat. Home.

The train crossed the last ridge and ran down the far side toward the dead station. Ada could see it now, up the hill behind the village — the little platform, the shuttered ticket-window, the sign that read, in letters no one had repainted, COPPERWELL. And under the station's eaves, in the grey dawn, burned a lamp.

Mr. Pendle's lamp. It was still lit. It had burned all night, out of habit, believing for him.

The train slowed. The wheels changed their song. And the conductor came through the Carriage of Lost Things, straightening his dark wool uniform, and took the train's timetable from his breast pocket — older than anyone, older than memory — and read it with his amber-glass eyes.

"On time," he said, and his brass face was pleased. He looked at Ada. "Your timetable says the same."

Ada drew out her own timetable, warm from her pocket, and unfolded the strip of paper. The last line of the route, in Gran's blue ink, said what it had always said. And the clock tower in Copperwell, high over the sleeping roofs, struck the hour — six clear notes, falling like coins into the still air — and Ada counted them, and they were right. The train was exactly on time.

The train pulled into the dead station and stopped, gently, with no jerk at all, the way it had started. The hiss of steam went soft. The lamps of the Carriage of Lost Things dimmed, one by one, to let the morning in. And the village lay below, still asleep, not knowing — not yet — that the clockwork train had come home.

Ada stood at the window with her hand flat against the cold glass, felt the heart beating under her feet — ba-dum, ba-dum — and understood that she was not frightened, and not sad, and that she had never been so awake.

Chapter 29: Copperwell, Again

The platform of the dead station was cold under Ada's boots, and the frost crunched like sugar. She stepped off the train with Jem's hand in hers, and the air was the air of home — coal smoke and frost and, faintly, the smell of bread, because the baker's oven was already going somewhere below. The lamp under the eaves burned, gold against the grey, and she thought of Mr. Pendle, asleep in his cottage, keeping faith in his sleep.

The village was the same and different. The same roofs. The same tower. The same frost. But the train stood behind her, brass and steam and golden windows, and the whole world seemed to have been rinsed in something clear and kind, the way Gran's blue ink looked in the morning light.

Ada turned to look at the train one more time. The passengers were at the windows — the Coat with its found button, the Teddy, the Kite folded neatly on its seat, the Umbrella, the Hat, the Letter. They waved. Ada waved back, and Jem waved both his arms.

The conductor stood on the platform beside her, tall and brass-faced and kind.

"You must let her go," he said gently, and Ada knew he did not mean the train only.

She nodded. She knew. The train was not hers to keep. It was hers to watch for, to keep the timetable for, to be ready for. Letting it go was part of the keeping. She had not understood that last night. She understood it now, in the frost, in the morning, with home at her back.

"Will it come back?" Jem asked, though he knew the answer.

"On time," said Ada, and the word felt like a promise she had been given to keep. "It always comes back on time."

Below them, in the village, the first shutter opened. A lamp went on in a window. The day was beginning, ordinary and gold, and Ada Bell stood on the platform of the dead station with her little brother's hand in hers and her timetable warm in her pocket, and watched the clockwork train breathe steam into the morning, and did not need to say goodbye out loud — because goodbye was only the long way of saying until next year.

Chapter 30: The Honorary Conductor

"Ada Bell," said the conductor, and his voice was not the voice of steam through a warm kettle now. It was the voice of someone making a speech, and a speech was a serious thing. He stood very straight on the platform of the dead station, brass face turned to the morning, and in his hand he held something small and bright.

Jem poked Ada. "He's got a thing."

Ada was not sure what to do with her hands, so she put them behind her back, the way Dad did when he was being official. The conductor held out the thing in his hand, and it was a whistle — a small brass whistle on a chain, catching the gold of the morning.

"The honor of the route," said the conductor. "Every train has a conductor. But a train that carries the heart needs a child to keep the heart's belief, and that is a different office. It has been waiting for you, Ada Bell."

Ada took the whistle. It was warm, the way the key had been warm, and it fitted her hand as if it had been made for her hand. She had not known a thing could fit like that — like a word that had been waiting to be spoken.

"Your job," said the conductor, "is to keep the timetable, and to know the route, and to be on this platform next year. The whistle is your voice. The timetable is your memory. The route is yours now, all of it, from the first stop to the last."

Ada looked at the whistle in her hand. It was the plainest thing, and the most precious.

"And Jem," said the conductor, turning. "Every conductor needs a bell."

Jem's eyes went enormous.

The conductor led them to the side of the Engine, where the bell hung, polished and warm — the bell from the Sea of Static, returned to its place at the front of the train. The Engine purred, low and contented, as they came near.

"Ring it," said the conductor. "It is glad to be rung."

Jem pulled the rope. The bell rang once, a clear bright note that skipped across the frost and made the birds of Copperwell stir and wonder. And again — twice — and the second ring hung in the air like a question with a happy answer, and the Engine blew a small steam heart above them in the clear cold morning, and Ada, Honorary Conductor of the Clockwork Train, with the whistle warm in her hand and the bell still ringing in her chest, understood that the office had been waiting for her all along.

"Until next year," said the conductor. "On time."

"On time," said Ada.

Chapter 31: The Goodbye

The train pulled away with a sound like the whole night letting out a held breath. Its whistle called once, long and kind, and the steam rose off the ridge like smoke from a fire no one could see. Except that Ada could see it, and she watched until the last golden window was a coin of light, and then a spark, and then nothing but the cold bright morning.

They stood on the platform a long time. Jem held her hand, and she held the whistle under her collar with her other hand, so that the brass lay warm against her collarbone.

"Do you think it's gone for good?" Jem asked.

"No," Ada said. "It's never late. It always comes when it's needed." She was not sure she believed it yet, saying it out loud. But the whistle against her chest seemed to tick, very faintly, like it agreed.

The village woke while they walked down the hill. Frost on the rooftops, smoke from the chimneys, Mr. Pendle's lamp still burning at the dead station behind them, as if he meant to leave it lit for the whole of the day. Mum was at the kitchen table, and Dad was winding the kitchen clock the way he always had, every morning, the way Gran used to on Sundays.

"There you are," Mum said. "Up before the birds. I've made porridge."

Neither of them asked where the children had been. That was the strange thing. Nobody asked. The morning was ordinary: porridge, school to think about, the frost melting on the glass. But ordinary, Ada found, could be a little more golden. Everything she looked at seemed to have a warm edge to it, like the world had been given a new coat of light.

She touched the whistle under her collar. One small note, when no one was listening, and the steam rose off the kettle on the stove in a little plume that might have been a heart.

Jem caught her eye and grinned. He had been there. He had seen.

"Today," he said, around a mouthful of porridge, "I'm going to draw a bigger map."

The clock on the wall ticked. Ada counted its heart. It ticked, she was certain, in time with the one under the ridge, far away, going on.

Chapter 32: The Next Year

The year passed through Copperwell the way a train passes through a landscape, steadily, and if you watched closely you could see each small thing going by.

Ada polished the whistle with a soft cloth every Sunday, the way Gran had wound the kitchen clock. It was not a chore. It was a keeping. She read the timetable each night before bed, tracing the strange stops with her finger in the dark, and every time she came to the words at the top, the ones that were also at the bottom, her heart gave a small warm turn. The train runs. The heart keeps. The night is kind.

School was school. Arithmetic, and the letter A, and the map of the world with its oceans drawn in blue ink that was not quite Gran's blue. Ada did not mind. She carried her secret the way the Coat kept its found button now: close, warm, safe. Sometimes, at the window in the late afternoon, she thought she heard a far whistle in the wind, thin and kind.

"It's just the wind," Jem said once, and then looked at her. "Or maybe not."

"Maybe not," Ada agreed.

The seasons had their own winding. Apples in the orchard, frost on the pumpkins, the baker's oven smoking through the cold, and the clock tower up the hill going golden at dawn. Mr. Pendle still kept his lamp lit at the dead station, out of habit, or out of something larger than habit. Ada passed him sometimes. He never said he had seen the train. But one day he said, "You keep a good thing, child," and Ada knew he meant the lamp, and she knew he meant more than the lamp.

Spring came. Dad's workshop smelled of oil and shavings. The clocks all ticked together in their different times, and Ada thought of the heart under the ridge and knew it was ticking too, under the frost, under the ground, keeping time with the village.

On her window ledge, in its brass case, the timetable waited. Ada added a small tick for every night she had checked the window. The paper was growing a little diary of belief, written in her own hand, in Gran's blue ink, one tick at a time.

Chapter 33: The Timetable, Kept

The timetable had been Gran's, and then Ada's, and now it was kept.

One rainy afternoon Ada took it out of its brass case and spread it on the table where the light was best. The paper had torn along one fold, where a hundred fingers had traced the stops. She mended it with a strip of paper and a pot of paste, pressing it flat, careful as Dad pressing a gear into its place.

Then she found the blue ink. Gran's ink, in its small glass bottle, the same blue as the sky after the rain. Ada dipped the pen and went over Gran's faded lines, stroke by stroke, so that the names stood out again: the Station of Lost Buttons, the Bridge of Sighs, the Orchard of Wrong Turns, the Sea of Static, the House of Clocks, the End of the Line. Her hand shook a little, the first time, drawing over her grandmother's handwriting. But the lines came out true, and where Gran's writing had faded Ada's writing took its place, and they were, she thought, almost the same hand.

Dad came in and watched her over her shoulder. He said nothing for a long while.

Then he put a key on the table. A brass key, old and heavy, with a heart-shaped bow.

"That's the key to the dead station clock," he said. "It's been in our family since before the station was dead. It fits nothing now. Nothing works, except the lamp."

Ada picked it up. It was warm from his pocket.

"Why give it to me?" she asked.

"Because someone should keep it," Dad said. "A key is a promise, even if the lock is gone. You're the keeper in this family." He nodded at the timetable. "You keep the things that matter."

Ada put the key in the brass case, beside the timetable. It fit there like it had always lived there. That night she added a tick to the paper for every night of the year she had checked the window, and the row of ticks grew long, like a track of small dark sleepers under a train.

The timetable, she understood, was not a list. It was a promise, kept in blue ink. Every time she mended it, every tick she added, she was winding it, the way you wind a clock: not so it would go, but so it would keep going.

Chapter 34: The Train, Seen

One cold morning, on the path to school, Ada heard footsteps behind her and then a voice, small and sure.

"Is that steam?"

Ada stopped. It was the baker's daughter, Lily, six years old, wrapped in a coat that was a little too big for her. She had stopped on the path and she was pointing at the ridge, at the long pale shoulder of land where the track used to run.

Ada looked. The ridge was empty. Frost, and the bare arms of trees, and a sky the color of old pewter. There was no train there. There was nothing.

But Ada felt it. A warmth on the wind, like standing at the open door of Dad's workshop in winter, the heat of the forge reaching out. And she heard it, or thought she heard it, a single far note, so thin it could have been a bird.

"There's nothing there," Ada said, carefully.

Lily did not lower her arm. "But I saw steam," she said. "It went up like this." She made a plume with her mittened hands, straight and soft.

Ada stood very still. She knew that plume. She had watched it rise off the ridge on the longest night, under the light of the Engine, and she had watched it fade in the dawn. Nobody else had seen it. Grown-ups had half-forgotten the train, the way they half-forgot everything wonderful. But here was Lily, six years old, pointing at the ridge on a cold ordinary morning, and saying she had seen steam.

Ada felt the whistle under her collar, warm against her skin.

"Sometimes," she said, "the seeing takes time."

Lily considered this. Then she nodded, very seriously, like a small clockmaker agreeing about a gear.

"Then I'll keep looking," she said.

They walked the rest of the way together. At the corner Lily said goodbye, and Ada watched her go, and she thought about what the conductor had told her, the very first night, standing in the steam at the crossing. He had said the train's job was to come when it was needed. And Ada understood, in the cold and ordinary morning, that a train was needed here, in this small girl who had seen steam on a ridge where Ada could see nothing.

Or perhaps it was not that the train was needed. Perhaps it was that the train was coming, and Lily would be ready.

Ada touched the whistle. It felt warm, as if it had been blowing, though she had not blown it.

"Sometimes the seeing takes time," she repeated to herself, and she liked the way it sounded, like the first line of a timetable.

Chapter 35: Jem's Turn

The nights were drawing in. The longest night was a week away, then five days, then four, and the frost on Ada's window was thicker every morning, and the village curled tighter around its lamps.

One evening Ada came down to find Jem at the kitchen table with the timetable spread out in front of him. He had taken it from its brass case without asking. He was seven now, and he could read, and he was reading the timetable the way other children read adventure stories, his finger moving under the words.

"'The Station of Lost Buttons,'" he said. "'The Bridge of Sighs.' I remember the bridge. It stopped sighing." He looked up at her, and his eyes were very bright. "This year I'm coming with you."

"Jem," Ada said. "You were with me. You've always been with me."

"No," he said. "I mean to drive it. Or at least to wind it." He tapped the timetable. "It says here the heart is wound by a child who believes. I believe. I've always been able to see the train. You know that."

And Ada did know it. It was the truth. Jem had seen the train the first night, on the ridge, before it ever stopped. He had been six, and small, and he had been holding her hand, but he had seen it. She had never once asked herself what that meant.

"Besides," Jem went on, "you wound it last time. It's my turn." He said it the way he said everything, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world, like the sun coming up. "A heart needs winding by whoever believes. You said the conductor said that."

"He did," Ada said.

She looked at her brother. He was seven. He was still small, but there was something settled about him now, the way a clock that used to be carried everywhere finds its place on the shelf. He had been on the train. He had rung the bell. He had fallen asleep against her arm, and when he woke he had asked if the heart was still beating, and she had told him yes, and he had believed her, completely.

She thought of herself at six, the year Gran gave her the timetable, the year she first saw the train through her window and understood, all at once, that it was real.

"All right," Ada said. "This year, you come."

Jem folded the timetable with enormous care, the way Ada had taught him, along the old folds. He handed it to her, and she put it back in its brass case.

"We'll need the sack," Ada said.

"I already packed it," Jem said. "The map, the biscuits, the marble, Gran's thimble. And this year I added a pencil, in case we meet anyone who needs a map drawn."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere, very far off, Ada could almost feel the heart under the ridge, keeping time, waiting.

Chapter 36: The Night, Again

The second longest night arrived the way the first one had, quietly, on frost.

Ada and Jem went to bed early because the whole village did, lamps out, curtains drawn, the clock tower chiming nine over a sleeping Copperwell. But neither of them slept. Ada lay with her eyes open, listening, and beside her on the floor Jem lay in his sleeping bag, his sack hugged to his chest, wide awake and grinning in the dark.

"Not yet," Ada whispered, because she knew what he was waiting for.

"Can you hear it yet?" he asked.

"No. But I can feel it." She put her hand flat on the floorboards. There it was: the faintest pulse, far down, under the frozen ground, under the rails that everyone said were gone. The heart. It was coming.

An hour passed, or a small eternity. Frost grew on the window in ferns and rivers. Then Ada heard it, and her heart turned over: the first whistle, far off, thin as a needle of light.

It was closer than last year. Or perhaps it was not closer. Perhaps they were simply ready.

"Jem," she said. "Now."

They dressed in the dark, fast and quiet. Jem put on his coat and his boots and slung the sack over his shoulder, and Ada lifted the timetable in its brass case off the shelf and tucked the whistle into her pocket, and they crept down the stairs and out into the cold.

The village slept. Frost glittered on the rooftops, and their breath made small steam. They went down the lane and up the hill toward the dead station, and at the top of the hill they stopped.

The lamp was lit.

Mr. Pendle's lamp, in the window of the dead station, burning steady and warm. He kept it lit every night, out of habit, everyone said so. But tonight Ada knew, with a certainty that had nothing to do with seeing, that it was lit for the train. That he had left it burning for them, or for it, or for both, and that his lamp believed, the way some lamps do, even when the hands that trimmed them could not say so.

"Ada," Jem whispered. "It's lit."

"I know," Ada said. "It's lit for the train."

The whistle came again, closer, and the ridge beyond the station seemed to glow along its long edge, like a lit match drawn across the dark. Ada put her hand to her chest, where the whistle hung on its chain, and she heard, far away, the great brass voice of the Engine, purring, as it began its long last descent into Copperwell.

Chapter 37: The Whistle

The train came over the ridge like a thought arriving.

One moment there was only the dark and the frost and the glow of Mr. Pendle's lamp. The next, the ridge was full of it: brass and steam, golden windows, the great black body of the Engine breathing in the cold, and the sound of wheels on a track that everyone said was gone, going past the station and down to the crossing, where it stopped, gently, as if it had always been stopping there for this exact moment.

Ada stood at the crossing with Jem. Her heart was beating very fast. She lifted the whistle to her lips and blew.

One small bright note, clear as a drop of water in the frost.

The train answered. Its whistle called back, long and kind and low, the voice of the Engine herself, and the steam from her funnel rose in a single plume and curled, and there, in the lantern light, Ada saw it: the shape of a heart, blown in steam, hanging over the carriages.

Jem grabbed her arm. "She remembers us."

The door of the Carriage of Lost Things opened, and the conductor stepped out.

He was just the same: the brass face, warm and polished, the amber-glass eyes that saw everything, the dark wool uniform with its brass buttons catching the lamp. He looked at Ada, and the steam from his mouth rose soft and warm.

"Ada Bell," he said. "The keeper of the timetable. Welcome back."

"You know my name," Ada said.

"I have always known it," said the conductor. "It has been on the timetable since you were six. And this," he said, turning to Jem, "must be the young man who rang the bell."

Jem stood up very straight. "I'm coming this time," he said. "I can see the train. I've always been able to."

"I know," said the conductor. "And I know you have read the timetable." He put his hand in his pocket and drew out a key, and for a moment the lamplight caught it, and Ada felt the whole night hold its breath. "There is a heart that needs winding," he said, "and this year, I think, it has two hands that believe."

Above them, the Engine blew another steam picture, small and perfect: a child at a window, watching.

Jem laughed out loud. "That's Ada," he said. "That's Ada every night."

Ada put her hand on the warm brass rail of the train. It was warm. It was waiting. And somewhere, far down under her feet, she could feel the heart, ticking, ready.

Chapter 38: The Brass

Jem put his hand on the brass rail and went very still.

For a moment he did not move. He just stood there, his palm flat on the warm rail, his face turned up to the great dark body of the train, and Ada watched him understand, all at once, that it was real, and that he was allowed to touch it.

"It's warm," he said, at last, in a small voice.

"The Engine is always warm," said the conductor. "She keeps the heart. She keeps everyone who rides."

Jem walked the length of the carriage, touching everything: the brass rail, the couplings, the little ladder up to the cab. He pressed his cheek to the boiler and closed his eyes. Then he found the conductor's face, the warm polished brass of it, and he leaned in close.

"Can I see myself?" he asked.

The conductor inclined his head. The amber-glass eyes went soft, and the brass face caught the lamp like a polished mirror, and there, small and crooked and entirely Jem, was Jem's own reflection, nose pressed almost to the metal.

"There I am," Jem said, delighted. "Hello, me."

"Hello," said the reflection, which was only Jem, but he nodded to it gravely all the same.

Near the head of the train hung the bell, and Jem stopped beneath it as if he had been called. It was brass, and it was huge, and it was polished to a shine like a harvest moon, and around its rim were little marks, one for every time it had been rung, going back a hundred years or more.

"May I?" Jem asked. He meant it with both hands.

The conductor looked at the Engine. The Engine, which had been quiet, blew a small approving puff of steam, and the conductor said, "The bell is always glad to be rung."

Jem reached up. He was not tall enough. He stood on his toes, and the conductor, very gently, with two warm brass hands, lifted him, and Jem took the rope of the bell and pulled.

The sound was enormous and round and golden, and it went out across the frost and the sleeping village, and the Engine blew a great plume of steam that rose and curled, and Ada was certain the plume was laughing.

Jem rang it again, and the first ring was still ringing in the air, and then the boarding bell rang, quick and bright, three times.

"Come aboard," said the conductor. "The timetable will not wait for us. It never does."

Jem took Ada's hand, and then he let go of it, because he wanted to walk onto the train by himself, like a conductor, which is to say like a person who has somewhere to be and means to be there. He climbed the step, and the warmth of the Carriage of Lost Things closed around him.

Ada followed, and behind her the door of the train swung shut with a sound like the last note of a song, and the world began, very gently, to move.

Chapter 39: The Steam

The train did not start with a jerk. It started the way it had the first time, which is to say the world began to move, and Copperwell slid away below the window, and the lamp at the dead station shrank to a spark and then to nothing, and the train was running.

Ada did not sit with Jem. For the first time, the conductor led her forward, along the corridor, past the Carriage of Lost Things where Jem was already settling in among the passengers, and through a small brass door, and into the cab.

The cab was warm. It was full of steam and lantern-light, and levers of brass and iron, and a great black dial with a single white hand that trembled, and at the front of it all, above the firebox, the light of the burning heart glowed gold and deep, like a sunrise kept in a stove.

And the Engine was there. Not in a body, but in everything: the warmth, the smell of oil and coal smoke and frost, the long low purr that ran through the floorboards like a cat that had swallowed a bell. Ada felt it through her shoes, through her bones.

"Hello," Ada said, to the air, to the levers, to the glow.

The Engine purred louder. It was a greeting.

The conductor stood at the window of the cab, looking out at the track. "You know the whistle-calls," he said. "One for starting."

"One for starting," Ada repeated.

"Two for the bridge."

"Two for the bridge."

"Three for home."

Ada said the words slowly. "Three for home." She had never thought about it before, what the three blasts meant, except that they were the ones she heard when the train left Copperwell in the dawn. Now the conductor put a whistle into her hand. Not her own whistle, but the train's, brass and worn, warm from a thousand hands.

"Every call is a promise," the conductor said. "One for starting, so the road knows we have begun. Two for the bridge, so the fog parts and the sighing stops. Three for home, so that whoever is listening knows that someone is on their way."

"Three for home," Ada said again, and she felt the words settle in her, the way Gran's blue ink settled into the paper.

Behind them, in the Carriage of Lost Things, she could hear Jem's voice, high and happy, talking to the Coat. The purr under her feet deepened. The track unwound ahead of them, silver in the moonlight, and the dial trembled, and Ada stood in the warm cab with the Engine and the conductor, holding the train's whistle, and she thought: this is what it is to be part of a thing that goes.

She blew one small note. One for starting.

And the whole train seemed to stretch itself, like a cat waking, and run on.

Chapter 40: The Journey

The route was the same, and it was not the same, and that was the wonder of it.

The Station of Lost Buttons rose out of the dark, its platform scattered with buttons like seeds in snow, and the Coat stepped off with them, and this time the buttons did not just ring underfoot. They sang, humming with the memory of being lost and found, and the Mainspring somewhere in the Engine's firebox seemed to turn a little brighter as they passed.

"Last time you found a mainspring in a button-hill," Jem said.

"It was never lost," Ada said. "It was waiting."

On the Bridge of Sighs, the valley of fog no longer sighed. It hummed, deep and content, like a kettle just taken off the boil, and the whole bridge ticked along with the toll-house clock, and the train crossed on a sound that was almost music.

At the Orchard of Wrong Turns, Jem said, "I'm not eating it this time. I'm keeping it." He picked a small golden fruit that tasted, he said, of the right turn, and put it in his sack beside Gran's thimble, and the Balance Wheel seemed to swing a little more evenly as the train climbed away.

At the Sea of Static, the grey hiss rose ahead, and the ferry of tuning forks hummed to meet them, and Ada lifted her whistle and blew Gran's song, the one she hummed winding the kitchen clock. The static parted, gentle as curtains, and the sea sang along, and the Bell itself hummed the song back in one long clear note.

"The bell remembers," Ada said.

"The bell keeps," said the conductor, at her shoulder. "That is what bells do."

The House of Clocks chimed them through. Every stopped clock in that great dark house began to tick as the train passed, one after another, a wave of time moving across the hill, and Jem pressed his face to the glass and said, "Gran's clock. I can see Gran's clock." It was ticking, and under it Ada could almost hear her grandmother winding it on a Sunday: the key turning, the click, the tick, the warmth.

The track straightened. The sky lightened. The conductor grew quieter, the way he had before, as if the End of the Line were a place where voices should be small.

And Ada understood, riding toward it, that the journey was the map of the heart, and that she had begun to know it by heart: every stop, every sound, every part. The mainspring of keeping. The escapement of sighs. The wheel that weighs choosing. The bell that keeps. The chime that never stopped. She could have drawn it with her eyes closed, the way she could draw the timetable.

"Ada," Jem said, from the doorway of the cab. "Are we there?"

"We're there," Ada said. "The last stop."

The train slowed, and the field of stars came up to meet them, and somewhere ahead, in the small station house at the End of the Line, the heart's case was open, waiting.

Chapter 41: The Believing

The End of the Line held its breath around them. The heart's case stood open in the little station house, and inside, the brass heart lay whole - mainspring, escapement, balance wheel, bell, chime - all in place, all still, like a sleeper waiting for morning.

The conductor did not hand Ada the key. He set it in Jem's palm instead, and watched her.

"Your sister taught you the route," he said to Jem. "But the route is only the outside of the journey."

Jem looked up. "I read the timetable," he said. "I can read it all now. The Station of Lost Buttons, the Bridge of Sighs, the Orchard of Wrong Turns, the Sea of Static, the House of Clocks. It says the train runs on the longest night, and it always comes when it is needed."

Ada felt something turn over in her chest, the way a page turns in a book you have almost finished. That was it. That was what belief looked like when it was not yours anymore - when it had grown a second shape in her brother.

"Hold the key," the conductor said, "not as a thing to force. Find the heart's rhythm first. It has one - everything made of brass keeps a rhythm somewhere, even when it has stopped. You cannot count it. You feel it."

Jem stood on his toes. He held the key the way he held his marble, gently, as if it were alive. His eyes went a little distant. Then he turned the key - once, slowly, in time.

The mainspring took hold with a sound like a small animal settling. The escapement began its tick - soft, unhurried, even. The balance wheel swung one way, then the other, and found its middle. The bell and the chime settled into their places like birds coming down onto a roof.

And the heart beat.

It was a deep, slow brass heartbeat, and it filled the little station house and then the field of stars and then, Ada was sure, the whole dark country under the wheels. The Engine outside gave a long, happy sigh of steam, and a plume of white rose over the roof in the shape of a heart.

The conductor looked at Ada, and his amber-glass eyes were warm, and he smiled his brass smile. It was a different smile than she had ever seen on him - smaller, and prouder.

"The believing is no longer yours alone," he said. "It is shared."

Ada looked from the beating heart to her brother, who was watching it with his whole face, and she understood that the heart had never been about the winding at all.

It had been about the turning over - the moment a belief passes from one hand to another and is still alive in both. And for the first time that night, Ada felt the warmth of it, the same warmth she had felt on her own first night, but doubled, because it was Jem's now too, and hers, and the train's, all beating together.

Chapter 42: The Winter

They were home at dawn, and the house smelled of bread and frost. Mum and Dad found them at the table, mittens still on, and did not ask where they had been. Mum only said that the kitchen clock had needed winding, which was odd, because she was sure she had wound it the night before. Dad looked up from his tea and smiled, and Ada had the strangest feeling that he knew something, and that he was keeping it for her.

Ada's whistle hung under her collar, hidden and warm. She felt it all winter long, a small brass weight against her chest, and it was the best secret she had ever carried.

The deep winter came down over Copperwell like a held breath. Snow covered the ridge and the dead track until nothing showed but white. The village clocks kept time - Dad's workshop full of ticking, the clock tower sounding its hours over the buried roofs - and Ada and Jem kept the secret between them, a warm stone each carried in a pocket.

Nights by the fire. Mum hummed while she mended, and once, without meaning to, she hummed Gran's song, and Ada and Jem looked at each other and did not say a word, though both of them heard it all the way down. Ada's timetable hung on the wall above her bed, marked with ticks, and the whistle sat on its hook, and sometimes at night Ada would take it down and hold it, remembering the weight of the key, the beat of the heart, the wink of brass.

When the house was very still, Ada lay with her hand flat on the floorboards. And far down, under the frozen rails, under the snow, she felt it - a deep, slow beat, steady as a clock, patient as the frost. The heart, winding its year.

She would fall asleep to it, her palm on the wood, sure as any child has ever been of anything, that somewhere under the white world the brass train was waiting, and that it was warm, and that it was on time.

Chapter 43: The Spring

Spring came to Copperwell like ink spreading in water - green at the edges, then everywhere. The clocks were set forward on the first warm Sunday, and Dad went round the workshop turning every key a little, and the whole house ticked one hour later, together, as if it had all agreed.

Ada was eleven. It was a strange size to be, smaller than a secret and larger than a year.

The baker's daughter, Lily, grew too. She grew tall enough to reach the counter and brave enough to walk alone to school, and she had a habit of stopping on the path to look at the ridge, the way Ada had once done, though Lily did not know yet what she was looking for.

One afternoon, warm and full of birds, Lily saw the timetable peeking from Ada's pocket - the brass case catching the light.

"What does it say?" Lily asked.

Ada could have said many things. She could have hidden it. But she was eleven, and she had kept the secret for nearly a year, and keeping, she had learned, meant something more than holding on.

"Here," she said, and she opened the case, and the folded paper whispered open like wings.

Lily leaned close. She traced the stops with her finger - the Station of Lost Buttons, the Bridge of Sighs, the Orchard of Wrong Turns, the Sea of Static, the House of Clocks - reading the strange names slowly, the way Ada had first read them, as if each word were a door.

And as Lily traced, Ada heard it. Very far off, very sure, riding the spring wind: a whistle. One long note, then the shape of a longer one.

She did not look up. She watched Lily's finger move down the list of stops, and she felt the heart's rhythm under her ribs, and she understood, in that moment, that the heart's rhythm was the rhythm of the seasons - turning, always turning, never lost. It was the same rhythm Gran had known, winding the kitchen clock every Sunday. It was the same rhythm Dad kept, ticking the workshop hours. And now it was the rhythm Ada kept too.

The night, she thought, is kind. And the train runs.

Chapter 44: The Next Stop

Ada understood the conductor's route now. She had not been taught it in words; it had simply grown in her the way a track grows across a country, one mile at a time. The train's next stop was Lily - a child who was ready, whether she knew it or not.

All through the long days of summer and the cool evenings of early autumn, Ada prepared. She polished the whistle until the brass shone like a small moon. She mended the brass case where the hinge had loosened, using a bit of cloth and her own careful fingers, the way Gran would have. And in the garden, out of sight, she practised the whistle-calls - one for starting, two for the bridge, three for home - until she could have sounded them in her sleep.

Jem found her at it one evening, sitting on the back step, the whistle bright in her hand.

"For Lily?" he said.

Ada nodded.

Jem thought about it, the way he thought about everything, with his whole face. Then he said, "She can have my window seat. It's the best one - you can see the whole ridge from it, and the buttons ring loudest on that side."

Ada laughed. It was the first time in months she had laughed at the secret, and it felt like thaw.

"Thank you," she said.

"Will she know what to do?" Jem asked.

"She won't know," Ada said. "That's the point. She'll find out, the way we did."

Jem nodded, satisfied, and went inside to bed.

Belief moved the way a train moved - one stop to the next, one child to the next, never arriving anywhere for good, only passing through, and being passed on. Ada looked at the ridge, gone dark against the early stars, and she was not afraid. She was the keeper now. The timetable was hers, the route was hers, the whistle-calls were hers, and the passing of it all was hers too.

She was ready. The whistle was ready. The timetable was ready.

And somewhere under the hills, patient as ever, the train was ready too.

Chapter 45: The Ticket

The conductor's art came to Ada not as a lesson but as a feeling, in the way the timetable felt when she held it. The paper was warm, as if it had been held before. The stops were written in Gran's blue ink and re-inked in Ada's own, and the ticks of a year of watching ran down the margin like a fence.

Every child's ticket, she understood, is written the night before their ride. Not by the conductor - he only carried them. They were written by the child who believed before, out of the same blue ink, with the same careful hand. The timetable was not one ticket. It was all the tickets that had ever been, folded together, waiting to be unfolded again.

So on the evening before the third longest night, Ada took out Gran's ink. She dipped the pen the way Gran had shown her - not too deep, let the ink breathe - and on a clean strip of paper, in the same blue, she wrote:

This ticket admits the bearer to wonder, and the return trip is always on time.

She read it back to herself. Then she read it to Jem, who nodded very seriously and said it sounded like a proper ticket. Ada folded it, unfolded it, and read it once more. She wanted the words to be exactly right, because they were the words that would carry Lily, the way the timetable's words had once carried Ada, the way Gran's words had carried her mother before anyone could remember.

It was the truest thing Ada had ever written. It was true about the train, which had never once been late; it was true about the night, which had always brought the train when it was needed; and it was true about Lily, who did not know yet that she was already on the schedule, because Ada had just written her into it.

Ada folded the ticket small and smooth. She placed it in the brass case with the timetable, where it would keep, warm as a held hand, until the night.

The longest night. Tomorrow.

She slept, and under the frozen rails the heart beat on, on time, on time.

Chapter 46: The Ride

The third longest night was colder than the first two, and quieter, and the frost lay on Copperwell like a silver blanket. Mr. Pendle's lamp was lit at the dead station, burning small and steady, and Ada took Lily's mittened hand and walked her down to the crossing.

"Is it far?" Lily asked.

"No," said Ada. "It comes to us."

They waited. The frost breathed. And then the whistle came - far, then nearer, then close, and the Clockwork Train came over the ridge, brass and steam, its golden windows blazing, and it stopped at the crossing below the hill, gently, as if it had been waiting for them all year.

Lily's face - Ada saw her own first night in it. The same wide eyes, the same breath caught, the same small step forward she could not help taking. It was like looking into a mirror that showed last year instead of this one.

The conductor stepped off. His amber-glass eyes found Ada, and he nodded, once, as if to say yes, you were right, she is ready.

Ada gave Lily the ticket. The blue words glowed a little in the lamplight, and Lily held it in both hands, reading it the way Ada had first read the timetable.

"This ticket admits the bearer to wonder," Lily whispered. "And the return trip is always on time."

"Always," said Ada.

Lily boarded. The Coat, remembering being found, swept forward to meet her, and the Teddy raised its one-eyed face, and the Kite rustled its old bright paper, and the Umbrella, for rain that had never come, unfolded itself like a wing and let her pass beneath it, dry and warm.

And Ada boarded too.

Jem looked at her, surprised. She had ridden the year before. But Ada shook her head gently, and he understood. Believing was not something you gave up. It was something you took with you.

The ride was quiet and golden. The buttons rang like sleigh bells. The bridge hummed in its mist. The orchard gave Lily a fruit that tasted of everywhere at once. And at the End of the Line, in the field of stars, Ada stood back and watched Lily take the key.

Lily's hand was small. The key was old. And the heart, which had kept Ada's year, turned over and beat for Lily's.

Chapter 47: The Conductor's Wink

On the platform at dawn, the conductor stepped down with them, the way he had with Ada on her first night, and he looked at her for a long moment. The sky above the field of stars was going pale. The Engine breathed softly, steam curling, patient.

He reached up and touched his brass face - just under his amber-glass eye, where a tear would go if a face of polished brass could cry. It might have been steam. He made it steam.

"The heart," he said, "has never needed winding more than once a year. And never needed it less than the year you kept it."

Ada did not understand, and then she did. All that year - the ticks on the timetable, the polish on the whistle, the mended case, the blue ticket written in the night - she had been winding something without knowing it. The heart had beaten all winter because she had kept it, and keeping, it turned out, was its own winding.

The conductor put his hand in his pocket. When he drew it out, the key lay across his palm - the key to the heart, the one that was never lost.

"It is yours," he said. "To keep, and to pass on."

He gave it to her. It was heavier than she remembered, and warmer, as if it had been held for a long, long time by hands that believed.

He boarded. He did not look back, but at the last step he paused, and he turned, and his amber-glass eyes met hers once more - and he winked.

A brass-lid wink. The first and only time.

The whistle sounded - one for starting - and the train pulled away into the steam, and Ada stood on the platform with the key in one hand and the whistle at her neck, and she was not sad.

She was full. The way the Engine was full of fire. The way the timetable was full of stops. The way the night was full of the train, and the train was full of the heart, and the heart was full of the children who had believed it and the children who would.

Chapter 48: The Heart, Beating

Home. Morning. Ada lay in her bed in the grey light and did not want to get up yet, because she wanted to feel it once more, awake.

She put her hand flat on the floorboards.

It was there. It was still there. Not a sound, not a vibration you could measure - a knowing, deep and sure as her own pulse. The heart was beating.

Under the rails, where the dead track ran under the snow and the grass. Under the village, in the stone and the cellars and the roots of the winter trees. In the clock tower, where Dad's great clock ticked on in its patient way. In Dad's workshop, where all the clocks ticked together, a whole room full of small hearts keeping time in chorus.

The heart of the train and the heart of Copperwell were one heart.

Ada lay still and listened to it with her palm. And slowly, in the warmth of the morning, she understood something that the conductor had never needed to say.

Belief was not a thing you had. It was not a lamp you could switch on, or a key you could own. It was a thing you kept - and keeping it meant giving it. The heart did not beat because Ada believed in it. It beat because she had given the belief to Lily, and to Jem, and because every time a child believed, the heart learned the rhythm again, fresh, as if for the first time.

The train would run for as long as there was a child to see it. And there would always be a child. Ada knew this the way she knew the stops of the route, the way she knew the three whistle-calls, the way she knew the first words of the timetable by heart.

She got up. The house was full of light. Somewhere far off - or maybe not far at all - the whistle sounded, one long note, then two for the bridge, then three for home.

Ada smiled, and went down to breakfast, and the whole village ticked on around her, one heart, keeping time.

Chapter 49: The Home

Ada went round the house that morning touching things, the way you touch a room you are leaving, to keep it.

The whistle sat on its hook by the door, bright from all her polishing, and she touched it, and it was warm, as if it had been used and loved. The key lay in the brass case with the timetable, where the ticket for Lily had lain the night before, and she touched the case, and the paper inside rustled, friendly as an old voice.

The window - her window, with its view of the ridge - held the morning light. She looked out at the track line, invisible now under green, and she did not need to see the train to know the train. Seeing, she had learned, sometimes took time.

Downstairs, Mum's kitchen smelled of bread and honey. Dad was in his workshop, and all the clocks ticked together in their soft crowded way, and Dad looked up and smiled at her through the glass without stopping his work, and Ada understood that Dad, who owned a brass key that fitted nothing, had believed all along, in the only way grown-ups could - by keeping time. The key fitted nothing, and that was exactly right, because it was not for the dead station clock. It was for the belief that kept the station lit, even when the lamp was out.

Jem was asleep still, one arm flung out, his sack on the floor beside him with its map and its marble and Gran's thimble. Ada stood in his doorway a moment. Her six-year-old self had looked like that, once, with a brass case under her pillow.

Lily was dreaming of bells, Ada was sure of it.

The ordinary rooms were the same rooms. Only more.

Ada knew the train would come every year, for as long as there was a child to see it - and there would always be a child. That was the whole of the timetable, in the end. That was the on-time that never failed.

She went to her room, and she slept, and under the village the heart beat on, patient and golden, keeping her safe, keeping the night kind.

Chapter 50: The Timetable, Forever

The train runs forever.

You cannot see it from the road, and the grown-ups say the old track is gone, but on the longest night, when the frost is thick and the village is asleep, it comes over the ridge - brass and steam, golden windows, the great Engine breathing - and it stops at the crossing below the hill, because there is always a child waiting, and the conductor always steps off to meet them.

The timetable is kept forever. Ada keeps it, mending and re-inking in Gran's blue ink, ticking the nights she watched. And after her, Jem will keep it, because he has read every stop and wound the heart with his own hand. And after him, Lily will keep it, because her ticket is written into it and will never be crossed out. And after her, children not yet born, whose names the conductor will know by their belief before they ever learn them themselves.

The heart beats forever because a child believes in it every year. That is the whole secret, and it is no secret at all: belief needs renewing, the way a clock needs winding, and a year is how long a childhood promise lasts, and every year there is a new child, and the promise is kept.

Ada stands at her window on the longest nights, and on the other nights too, because keeping is not one night's work. She holds the timetable, and the whistle hangs at her neck, and the key sleeps in the brass case, and she knows the route by heart now - one for starting, two for the bridge, three for home. Sometimes, on the spring wind, she hears a far whistle, very far, very sure, and she knows the heart is turning somewhere under the hills, keeping its year.

The last image is the train itself: the Clockwork Train, brass and steam, running through the dark on a track that only children can see, its windows lit, its bell ready, on time forever - and a child at every window of every year, watching for it, and being watched for in return. The Coat rides with them, one button found. The Teddy's one eye keeps watch. The Kite rides high in its corner, remembering one perfect afternoon. The Umbrella waits for rain that never came. The Song hums itself, half-remembered and entirely loved.

And the timetable's first words, which are also its last, written in blue ink and never faded:

The train runs. The heart keeps. The night is kind.
