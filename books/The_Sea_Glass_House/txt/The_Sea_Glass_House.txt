The Sea Glass House
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: Crab Lane

Saltcove sat where the road ran out, at the end of the land, with the sea on one side and the gray-green hills on the other. It was a town that smelled of brine and tar and bread, and in summer the light was northern, gray and gold, and the shingle beach glittered like a spill of coins.

At the town's edge, where the houses stopped and the gulls began, a private lane ran off toward the headland. It was called Crab Lane, and a sign stood at its mouth, its paint long worn to the color of driftwood. No children beyond this point, the sign said. The words were the town's, but the rule had been Pip's family's for three years, and before that for everyone, for twenty years before she was born.

From the seawall you could see the house at the lane's end. It was a cottage of gray stone whose walls glinted with sea glass, green and blue and amber set into the mortar like jewels in a cliff. Its windows were not windows at all but old bottles, set deep in the stone, and when the sun went down behind it, the whole house lit up from inside like a jar held to a lamp. It had stood locked for twenty years. Its garden had gone to grass.

Pip Marlow was twelve, and she had been telling herself the story of that house for nine of them. She told it in bed at night, in the gray hour before the light. She told it to the gulls. She never told it to anyone who could answer back, because the story was the only thing she had that the house had not taken — the house took everyone else's stories and kept them behind its locked door.

She knew the lane was forbidden. She knew the sign meant her, and had meant her since she was nine. But knowing a thing and stopping your wondering were two different things, and Pip had a wondering heart. On the first true morning of summer she climbed up on the seawall, the way she had climbed it as a child, and looked down the lane at the sea-glass house.

The windows were dark. The door was shut. And yet the house seemed to be looking back at her, patient as the tide.

"One day," she said to it, half to the gulls, half to the sea. "One day I'll know what you're keeping."

The house said nothing. But it glittered, and the sea below it went on with its slow, enormous breathing, and Pip went home to Saltcove with the wondering still bright inside her.

Chapter 2: The Locked House

Moe came the second week of summer, in a green car with salt on the windows and his good jacket thrown in the back like laundry. Morris Kane, twelve, Pip's cousin, who stayed every summer and had done since they were small.

"Show me something," he said the first afternoon, standing in her kitchen doorway. "Last summer I saw a lighthouse. This summer better not be a puddle."

Pip took him down to the seawall. "Look."

Moe looked. He squinted at the lane, at the sign, at the far cottage with its glittering walls. "It's a house," he said. "Made of bottles. So what."

"Twenty years," Pip said. "Nobody's opened the door in twenty years."

"Twenty years and nobody's thought to break a window," said Moe. "Windows made of bottles. That's a weakness. That's a design flaw."

"It's not a flaw," said Pip. "It's the point. Come on."

They went along the wall until the lane was between them and the town, and the sea-glass house stood full at the lane's end. The tide was low and the cove lay bare below it, stones and weed and the long hush of the water. The house did not look ruined. That was the strange thing. It looked watched-over. The mortar was whole. The garden was grass, but the grass was green, as if someone watered it, though no one came down the lane. Moe stopped being unimpressed. Pip watched it happen — the way his jokes went quiet and his eyes went wide and the house went from a curiosity to a question.

"Who lives there?" he said.

"No one," said Pip.

"Who keeps it, then?"

That was the question nobody in Saltcove could answer, and Pip had asked it everywhere she was allowed to ask. Her mother's face closed. Her father shrugged. The postmistress, Mrs. Pemberton, had said only: "The man who bought it bought it for a reason, and the reason is not our business." There was a man, then. A man nobody saw, in a house nobody entered.

Moe was quiet a long time, which for Moe was like a storm holding its breath.

"All right," he said at last. "That's something. That's a proper something."

"It's a locked house," said Pip. "And I'm twelve now."

"Twelve," said Moe. "Old enough to know a locked door when you see one. Young enough to want inside it."

He looked at her sideways. And Pip, who had carried the wondering alone for years, suddenly did not have to carry it alone anymore.

Chapter 3: The Ban

The ban had a story, and Pip knew it the way you know a scar — she had heard it told about herself enough times that it had stopped being hers.

She had been nine. It had been a day like this one, low tide and gold light, and she had wanted to see the house up close, the way you want a thing you have been told you may not have. The lane was the only way in. The sign had been there even then — No children beyond this point, worn but clear — and she had walked around it, not through it, as if the rule were a gate she could step around.

She had not even reached the house. She had climbed the seawall to look down the lane, because from the wall you could see the roof and the chimney and the bottle-glass glint, and she had slipped. She still remembered the scrape of the stone, the grainy cold of it, the shock of being suddenly, horribly not on her feet. A fisherman had carried her home, frightened and bleeding at the knee, and there had been a row of grown-up faces at the door, and her mother's hands shaking as they washed the cut.

The town had done the rest. The lane was private land, and the council had posted it fresh and larger: No children beyond this point, in black letters, under it a smaller line about the danger of the cliffs and the current. It was true that the headland was dangerous. It was true that the sea there took no prisoners. But the sign was not only about the sea. It was about a nine-year-old who had climbed a wall and slipped, and the fright it had given everyone who loved her.

After that, the rule had come into the house like weather. No Crab Lane, her mother said, and her father agreed, and Pip had agreed, because what child argues with the fear in her own mother's face. She had carried the rule for three years. She had carried it so long she had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing the salt on the air.

What she did not notice, what she could not name until she was older, was the shape of it: a small locked door at the back of her own life. Not the house's door. Hers. A rule that said the thing she wanted most was a thing she was not allowed even to look at.

Moe understood the rule the way he understood everything — by asking it sideways. "So the ban's for everyone," he said, "or just for you?"

"Everyone," said Pip.

"And you've kept it three years," said Moe. "Even now."

Pip looked down the lane to where the house glittered at the end of it.

"I've kept it," she said. "I haven't stopped wondering. Keeping and wondering aren't the same thing, Moe."

"No," said Moe, and something in his voice went soft. "No, they're not."

Chapter 4: The Storm

The first storm of summer came on a gray-green afternoon. Pip watched it build from the kitchen window while her mother made tea and the light went the color of an old bottle, the sea turning flat and wrong, the gulls flying inland in long, silent lines.

Saltcove knew its storms. The sea here was not a friend you kept; it was a neighbor you watched, one that changed its face without warning. Her mother closed the windows and said, low, "The headland will get it worst," and Pip thought of the sea-glass house, alone out there at the end of the lane, and hoped its bottle windows were as strong as they were beautiful.

The storm came in at dusk. The wind arrived first, then the rain, then the sea itself, rising over the shingle like something standing up from sleep. Pip and Moe watched from the kitchen window, their noses almost touching the glass. The beach disappeared. The tide came in past the line of wrack, past the stones where the boats were hauled, and the water threw itself at the headland and came back white.

"It's moving the beach," Moe said. "It's — look. It's digging."

And it was. The sea was not only taking; it was digging, dragging the shingle off the bottom of the headland in great gray handfuls and laying it somewhere else. Stones rolled like marbles in the dark. The boom of it went through the floorboards and up into Pip's bones, and she was afraid, and she was also — she realized it with a small, guilty thrill — glad. Something was happening. The sea was not just keeping its secret. It was working on it.

Her mother came and stood behind them, a hand on each of their shoulders. "It's a big one," she said. "First of the season. They always are." And then, as if to herself, in a voice Pip was not meant to hear: "Iris would have been down there with a notebook and a string."

Pip turned her head so fast her neck cracked. "Who?"

"Nobody," said her mother, but her face had gone closed, and Pip knew the name was not nobody, knew it the way you know a door has just shut.

The storm blew through the night and into the gray hours. Pip did not sleep. She lay awake and listened to the sea rearranging the world, and she had the feeling — she could not have said why — that the storm was not destroying anything.

It was bringing something.

In the morning the sea was calm and the light was clean, and the beach was new. The storm had laid the shingle bare in long clean slopes, and the town came down to look, and Pip, standing on the seawall, saw something glinting at the tideline, something that did not belong, something the sea had carried up and left, like a word left in a mouth.

It was an iron key.

Chapter 5: The Key

It was crusted with barnacles, storm-thrown, an iron key the size of Pip's hand, and it lay in the wrack at the tideline like a message the sea had left on purpose.

Pip picked it up. It was cold and heavy and rough, and the barnacles crunched under her thumb. She rinsed it in a rock pool until the water ran clear, and the shape came out of it: a long shaft, a worn barrel, a bow like a small rough heart. No name. No tag. Just a key, and the sea does not hand you a key without meaning something by it, or so Pip had always believed.

"Door Key," said Moe, taking it and turning it over. "That's its name now. Every key needs a name before you can trust it."

"Every key needs a lock," said Pip.

They walked the beach end to end that morning, both of them bent like little old people, looking. The storm had thrown up every kind of thing — rope, a lobster pot, a bottle of red glass, sea glass by the pocketful, a fisherman's boot with no fisherman. But only one key. They tried it, in a way, on everything that looked like a lock. The church gate: too small. The lifeboat-shed padlock: too big. Moe's suitcase lock, for a laugh: no. It fit nothing they knew.

"Everything we wonder," said Moe, very quietly, and Pip knew exactly what he meant.

Because there was one lock in Saltcove that nobody ever tried, because nobody was allowed near it. One door that had not turned for twenty years. And here, in her pocket, heavy as a heartbeat, was the thing the sea had just brought up.

"It can't be for that," Pip said.

"Why not?" said Moe. "You said the storm was bringing something. You said the sea keeps secrets and you said it works — what'd you say? — works on them. Maybe this is the sea working. Maybe it's the sea opening its own hand."

Pip held the key up to the light. The barnacle shells glittered, and the iron underneath was black and old, and she thought: twenty years. Twenty years the house has been shut. Twenty years is a long time for a door to stay locked.

"The sign," she said, but her voice came out thin.

"Yep," said Moe. "The sign's still there."

"The ban. My mum."

"Yep. All still there." He looked at her level. "We're not doing it now. We're not doing it stupid. We're going to check the tide tables and we're going to go at dusk, and we're going to try the key, and if it doesn't fit we'll come home and nothing happened."

"And if it does fit?" said Pip.

Moe turned the key in the air, once, and put it in her hand.

"Then," he said, "the sea gave it to us. And I don't think the sea gives you a key for nothing."

Pip closed her fingers around it. The barnacles were sharp. The iron was cold. And something in her, some locked door of her own, was beginning, very quietly, to turn.

Chapter 6: Pip and Moe

Every summer they made their rules, and the rules held. It had been like this since they were eight, when the sea first taught them that you do not go down alone and you do not go down after dark, and they had added their own on top, year by year, until the rules were a little ladder they climbed together.

Rule one: Moe was the lookout and the runner. He had the long legs and the quick eyes, and if something went wrong he could be back at the house before you could count ten. Pip was the one who went first. She had the steady hands and the still heart, and Moe, who made fun of everything, never once made fun of that.

Rule two: nobody went down alone. The sea was not something you argued with. It had been there longer than the town, and it would be there after, and it did not care how good you were at swimming. This was not fear, exactly. It was respect, which is fear with the edges worn off.

Rule three: they told each other the truth, even when the truth was bad. Especially when it was bad. Moe had told her the truth once, the summer she was ten, about a thing she had said that had hurt someone, and she had hated him for a whole afternoon and loved him for it ever since. The truth, between them, was a lifeboat. You did not poke holes in it.

Now they sat on the seawall at the end of the day, the sun going down behind the sea-glass house, and made the new rules for the new thing.

"We go at dusk," Moe said. "No earlier. The lane empties by then."

"We take the key and nothing else," said Pip. "No torch. A torch is a thing that gets seen."

"Lookout stays at the lane mouth. Runner stays ready."

"The one who goes first goes slow," said Pip. "And if it feels wrong, we come back. Nobody goes all the way in because they're brave. Bravery isn't a thing you prove."

Moe looked at her a long moment. The sun was on his face, turning it gold. "You really think it fits the sea-glass door," he said.

Pip did not have to think about it. She had known it since she picked the key up, the way she knew the tide was in and out. She had only been pretending not to, because pretending not to was the last thing keeping her on the seawall instead of already down the lane.

"I think the sea brought it to me," she said. "And I've been waiting nine years to be brought a thing."

"That's not a rule," said Moe.

"No," said Pip. "It's a feeling. Feelings aren't rules. But they're allowed on the ladder too."

Moe nodded slowly, and then he grinned, his whole face lighting up the way it did when something good and forbidden was about to happen.

"All right then," he said. "Dusk it is. Lookout, runner, and the one who goes first."

He held out his hand, and she took it, and they shook on it, the way they had shaken on every summer since they were eight.

Chapter 7: The Unlocking

Dusk came slow over Saltcove, the gold going gray, the gulls settling, the sea turning from white to slate. Pip and Moe stood at the top of Crab Lane and looked down it, and the sign stood at the lane's mouth between them and the house: No children beyond this point.

They did not go around it this time. They went past it, side by side, and the house at the lane's end watched them come. Pip could feel the ban on her skin like a pulled thread. Three years. Nine years, if you counted the wanting. And now her hand was around the iron key in her pocket, and the wanting had a shape to it.

The lane was not long. It was grass-grown and cobbled, and the sea air had worn it smooth. The seawall ran beside it, and Pip did not look at the place where she had fallen, and she did look, and she was surprised to find that the scrape was only a scrape, and the wall was only a wall, and the fear she had carried about this lane was bigger than the lane itself. Moe stopped at the garden wall. He did not say the house was waiting. He said, "Lookout stays here. Runner stays ready. I count to a hundred, and if you're not out, I'm coming in."

Pip put her hand on the door.

It was a door of gray wood, salt-bleached, with an old lock thick with rust. She took the key out of her pocket. It looked small now, in the dusk, in front of this. She had rehearsed this a hundred times and it had never occurred to her that the key might not fit, and she held her breath and slid it in.

It fit.

It fit like the lock had been waiting for it, like the lock had been keeping its shape for twenty years, and Pip turned it, and the sound it made was the sound of a held breath let out. The rust gave. The bolt gave. The door, which had not opened for twenty years, swung inward onto dark and dust and green light.

"Sixty-one," said Moe from the wall, his voice high. "Sixty-one seconds."

Pip stepped over the threshold. She went slow, the way she had promised. The dark was deep and the green light was coming from somewhere, and she could smell salt and old paper and something else, something clean, like water kept still a long time.

"Moe," she said, and her voice was small and strange. "Come see."

The runner came in off the wall. The two of them stood together in the doorway of the sea-glass house, and the door stood open behind them, and the green light stood before them, and the house held its breath with them, the way the sea holds its breath between waves.

It had not been empty. That was the first thing Pip understood. It had been full, full the whole time, and it had been waiting.

Chapter 8: The Jars

The green light came from the windows, but it came from the shelves too. The front room of the sea-glass house was lined with them, floor to ceiling, shelf after shelf, and on every shelf the light was caught and held — hundreds of jars. Green jars, blue jars, amber jars, some round and fat, some tall and thin, some no bigger than Pip's fist. Sea glass, every one of them, worn smooth by the sea's patience, glowing from the inside in the last of the dusk.

Pip and Moe stood in the middle of the room and did not speak.

The dust was thick on the floor, and their footprints had come in behind them like a question. The air was old and still. But the room was not dead. Every jar held a note. Pip could see them rolled inside, tight as cigarettes, pale as old bone, and some of the jars were dark with age and some were nearly new, and all of them, all of them, had something inside, something put there on purpose.

Moe went to a shelf and stood very close to it, reading the glass like a man reading a foreign language he almost knew. "There's hundreds," he said at last, and his voice was not making fun of anything. "There's hundreds and hundreds."

"Somebody's been keeping these," said Pip. "Somebody's been collecting them. For years."

"Look," said Moe, and lifted a jar down, very carefully, the way you lift a bird's egg.

The jar was green and old, and the note inside it was water-stained, and through the glass Pip could see the handwriting — slow, patient, a stranger's. She did not touch it. Neither of them touched the note. It seemed too private, too held, like a letter the sea had carried and was still carrying.

"Do we read them?" Moe said.

Pip thought about the key, about the sea, about the nine years of wanting. She thought about the door opening, the held breath let out. And she thought about the way the house felt — not haunted, not spooky, not even sad. Full. Like a room where a hundred people had left their voices, and the voices were still in the jars.

"Yes," she said. "I think we're meant to. I think that's what the key was for."

Moe nodded slowly. Then he carried the green jar over to the window, where the last light was, and set it on the sill, and the two of them sat down on the dusty floor with the jar between them, the sea outside the window going quiet with the evening, the house standing around them like a great warm hand.

And Pip reached out and took the lid off the first jar.

The note inside was small and soft and old. She unrolled it the way you unroll a thing that has waited a long time to be read. And the sea, outside, went on keeping time.

Chapter 9: The Notes

The first note was from a sailor. The writing was small and crowded, the paper the color of tea. It said: For luck. Tossed overboard off the Head on a clear day. If you find this, throw it back in. The sea has more luck than I do. Pip read it twice, and she had the feeling of a door opening in a wall she had not known was there.

The second note was a girl's wish. The writing was careful and round, the way a child writes when a thing matters. It said: I wish my dad comes home from the oil rig by my birthday. If you read this, please wish it too. Somebody else's wish makes it stronger, my mum says. Moe read it and set it down very gently, the way you set down a thing that is breakable.

The third note was a wedding ring. A man wrote: I lost her ring over the side of a fishing boat in the cove, the day I meant to put it on. I have looked for it twenty years. I am not asking for it back. I am asking the sea to keep it safe, since the sea has it now. Keep this note, and think of us well.

There was an apology. It said only: I am sorry I never said it. The sea was the only one who would listen. And there was a we made it, in handwriting that shook a little, from a person who had crossed an ocean in a small boat and thrown a note in at the far end of the voyage, so that someone would know.

They read on into the dusk. A man who lost his dog at sea. A child who wrote I am ten and I am not afraid of the sea, and then, underneath, in the same hand: mostly. A note that said only: The sea knows. A note with a drawing of a boat. A note that had been read before — Pip could tell, because the paper was creased along different lines — and put back, the way you put a book back on a shelf.

Moe sat back at last, in the dust, and looked around at the hundred jars, the hundred strangers.

"Every one of these is a person," he said. "A real person. And they all threw something into the sea, and the sea kept it, and somebody here kept them."

"The sea's a postman," said Pip. "This house is the sorting office, and every jar is a letter that got delivered."

They were quiet a moment, and the quiet was full, the way the room was full.

"Do you think they ever got answered?" said Moe.

Pip thought about the girl's wish, and the man's ring, and the apology.

"I think getting read is getting answered," she said. "I think the sea delivers to whoever needs it most. Even if it takes twenty years."

Outside, the last of the light was going. Inside, the jars went on glowing, green and blue and amber, a hundred small lives handed to the sea, a hundred letters that had finally been opened.

Chapter 10: The Strangers

The last shelf was different.

All the other jars held the worn, water-stained notes of strangers — the washed-in letters of the sea. But the last shelf held newer jars, in careful rows, the notes inside clean and white, and each jar was labeled. Not with names. With dates. A jar for every year, going back and back along the shelf, and Pip understood it all at once, and her breath went out of her.

Someone had been collecting these bottles for decades. Someone had walked this beach after every storm, year after year, and picked up every message that washed into this cove, and brought them here, and rolled them into jars, and dated them, and shelved them. Not as treasure. As letters. As a person keeps letters.

"Not the sea's postman," said Moe, reading over her shoulder. "The house's. Somebody's been the postman for the sea."

The dates went back twenty years. Twenty years of jars, one after another, and Pip thought of the locked door, the locked lane, the man nobody saw. And the newest jars held recent notes, barely dried, the ink fresh. Whoever kept this house had been here this week. The jars were not an old thing kept. They were a living thing, being kept, right now, by somebody who came and went through a door that never seemed to open.

"It's not a monster," Pip said. "The town calls him a monster. The recluse. But he's the person who answers the sea."

"Pip," said Moe, very quietly, and his voice had gone strange. "We should go. This is somebody's letters. Somebody's whole life, and we broke in."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to read every note on the last shelf, to know the man who kept the sea's mail. But Moe was right, and the knowing came in like a tide.

A sound came from outside. A scuff on the gravel of the lane, the small sound a person makes when they are not trying to be quiet — or when they are, and the gravel gives them away.

Pip put the jar back, exactly where it had been. Moe was already moving. They went out and latched the door behind them — the key turned again, the held breath taken back in — and they ran down the lane, past the sign, and did not stop until the seawall was between them and the sea-glass house.

"Was that him?" Moe whispered.

"I don't know," said Pip. Her heart was a drum. "But he's real. Whoever keeps that house is real, and we've been in his house, and we have to know who he is."

"Or," said Moe, "we never go back."

They looked at each other in the dusk, and the sea-glass house stood behind them, and both of them knew, the way you know the tide, that they would go back. The house had been the most wonderful thing either of them had ever been inside.

And in Pip's pocket, where she had meant to put nothing, something heavy knocked against her thigh. She had taken a jar after all. One jar. The oldest-looking one, the one her hand had closed around without telling her.

She did not tell Moe about it yet.

Not yet.

Chapter 11: The Grandmother's Jar

The seawall was warm from the day, and the harbor below it breathed in and out. Pip and Moe sat with their backs to the sea-glass house, and Pip emptied her pockets onto the flat stone between them.

Two glass pebbles. A twist of salt-sweet tar. The key, still cold from the door.

And the jar.

It was small, the size of a lemon, and the glass was the deep green of sea water in shade. Not like the others. Every jar in that room had held a stranger's words, but this one Pip had lifted without thinking, the way you lift a shell because it is different.

"The rest are newish," Moe said. "This one's been in the sea a long time. Look at the barnacle marks. Like a scar on a knee."

Pip unrolled the note. The paper was soft as cloth, and the writing was small and firm, and her hands began to shake before she read a word, because she had seen that hand before.

It was on the back of the photograph. The one in her mother's drawer, of a woman at the tide pools, a woman with her sleeves rolled up and a glass jar on a string around her neck. And on the back, in ink gone faint: Saltcove, the year of the pool.

"I know this writing," Pip said.

She read the note aloud. The words were old and careful, the way you write when you know a stranger will read you:

To whoever finds this. I am looking for something I lost in this cove. The glass pool. If it shines again, tell Corwin. I will come back on the anniversary. — I.M.

"I.M.," Moe said. "That's initials."

"Gran's name was Iris Marlow." Pip's voice came out small. "Dr. Iris Marlow."

The sea said nothing. It was low water, and the gulls had gone quiet, and the whole cove felt like a held breath, the same held breath as the lock turning.

"My gran," Pip said, and the word felt strange in her mouth, like a word she had only ever read. "She wrote this. She was here. In this cove. And she said she would come back."

Moe was not joking. That was the measure of how much it mattered. He took the note from her and read it again, slowly, the way you read a thing you want to be true.

"The glass pool," he said. "You've never heard of it. Nobody's heard of it. So either the whole town forgot a pool, or the whole town was told not to see it."

Pip looked past him, up the lane, to the house with its hundred jars and its one green one.

"She didn't leave," Pip said. "She was looking. And she said she would come back. And I've been nine years old, being told she chose the sea over us."

She folded the note small and put it in the pocket over her heart.

"I want my gran," she said.

"Then let's find her," said Moe.

Chapter 12: The Missing

Mum was shelling peas at the kitchen table, and Pip sat across from her and said it all in one breath before she could stop herself.

"Gran didn't leave, did she? She was looking for something. She wrote it in a note and put it in a jar and the note is in my pocket."

The shelling stopped.

For a long moment Mum's hands hung over the bowl. Then she set the pea pod down, very carefully, the way you set down a thing you are afraid of breaking.

"Where did you get that note, Philippa?"

"Off the beach," Pip said. It was true. The sea had brought it.

"You found a bottle." Mum's voice was flat. "From some stranger. And you decided it was your grandmother's because you want it to be."

Pip pulled the note out and held it, unopened. "It's her writing. The same as the photograph."

Mum did not look at it. She looked at the window, and what she was looking at was not the window.

"She went missing," Pip said, "or she left, or she chose the sea over us. Which one is true, Mum?"

"The last one." Mum's voice broke in the middle like a wave. "She chose the sea over us. She had a daughter, Pip. She had a home. And she left the both of them to go looking in tide pools at the other end of the world, and she never came back for the one she had here."

"She says in the note she's coming back. On the anniversary."

"Every year she said that." Mum turned and for a moment she was not guarded at all; she was just a daughter, the way Pip was a granddaughter. "Every year I waited. I was your age when she went, Pip. I counted the days. I counted the summers. And the sea never gave her back."

The bowl of peas sat between them, full and green and ordinary, and neither of them touched it.

"In the attic," Mum said at last, "there's a box. Her things. I could never throw them away, and I could never look at them either. Go and see. Then you'll understand what she left behind."

Pip wanted to say that a box was not the truth, that the note in her pocket was the truth, that a person who writes I will come back has not chosen to leave. But her mother's face was closed again, and Pip knew there was a door in that face and she was standing outside it.

She went up to the attic.

The box was small, and it had been opened and shut so many times that the cardboard had gone soft at the corners. Inside: a few salt-stiffened books on tide pools, a jar of sea glass, a compass with the needle loose.

And the photograph. The woman at the pools, sleeves rolled up, glass jar on a string.

Pip took the photograph and the books and went back down the stairs, and she told herself that missing was not the same as gone.

Chapter 13: The Left

They spread everything on Pip's bedroom floor: the note, the photograph, the salt-stiffened books, the jar of sea glass, the compass with the loose needle.

Two stories lay side by side, and they did not fit.

"The family says she left," Pip said. "She chose the sea over us. She went missing. She's gone."

"The note says she was looking," Moe said. "For a thing she lost. The glass pool. And it says she will come back on the anniversary."

"A person who leaves doesn't write to a stranger," Pip said. "A person who leaves closes the door. A person who is looking leaves a note so somebody knows where to find her."

Moe turned the compass over and let the needle wobble. "And a person who is coming back," he said slowly, "packs light."

The box was small. Pip thought about it: one box, and it was mostly books. A person leaving for good takes the good silver and the photographs. A person leaving to look takes a compass and a jar and a note that says I will come back.

"If she was looking," Moe said, "she wouldn't have gone far. She'd have gone to the next cove, then the next. She'd have gone to the edge of everything, checking every pool, and come back here to check the one she lost."

"Every anniversary," Pip said. "For twenty years."

The photograph lay face-up. Gran's sleeves were rolled, her hands were wet, and behind her the water of a tide pool was so clear you could see the bottom. There was a piece of sea glass on the rock beside her, green as a thumb, and Pip thought: she was happy here. You can't see it and not know it.

"The family has the story wrong," Pip said. "Gran didn't go. Gran is going. There's a difference, and the difference is coming back."

Moe said nothing for a moment. Moe made fun of everything, Pip knew, but he was not making fun now. He was squinting at the photograph, at the woman's rolled sleeves, at the jar on its string, at the piece of green glass.

"I believe you," Moe said. "And I've got a tide-table calendar on my wall at home that says glass pool is real."

"You said you'd never heard of it."

"Because I'd never looked." Moe grinned. "Mum bought it from Mrs. Pemberton's shop two winters ago. It's all moons and fish pictures. I never read the pencil marks. But there's a date circled in June, and beside it, in somebody's hand: glass pool."

Pip sat very still. The note said the anniversary. The calendar said June.

"What month is it?" she asked.

"June," said Moe.

They looked at each other, and the house was full of the sound of the sea through the open window, patient, keeping time.

Chapter 14: The Seeking

The tide-table calendar hung on the kitchen wall beside the bread bin. Pip had walked past it ten thousand times and never once seen it, the way you never see the thing that has always been there.

Moe climbed onto a stool and lifted it down. It was two winters old, its pages gone soft, the fish illustrations faded, and along the top of the June page somebody had written a word in pencil.

glass pool.

Pip ran her finger over it. The letters were small and firm. They were the same letters as the note in her pocket. The same as the back of the photograph.

"Gran's writing," she said. "In our kitchen. She was in our kitchen, Moe. She stood where I stand."

"Or whoever lived here before you," Moe said gently.

But the pencil was old, and the writing was a scientist's writing, the kind that labels things. Pip could feel it the way you feel a room that has just been entered. She had lived her whole life with a map on the wall and never known to look.

She found the date. June, the lowest spring tide of the year, circled, and beside it in the margin: anniversary.

"When did Gran go missing?" she asked.

"Before you were born. That's all anybody says."

"Twenty years." Pip counted on the calendar back from the circled date, year over year, until the pencil marks simply stopped. "There. That's the year she left. It's on this page. The day she left was the day of the lowest spring tide."

Moe leaned in. "So the anniversary is a tide," he said. "Not a birthday. A tide."

"The note says I will come back on the anniversary. What if the anniversary is the day the pool comes back? The day the sea goes out farthest and shows what it's been hiding?"

Moe looked at her for a long moment. Then he went to the window and counted on his fingers.

"The lowest spring tide this year is in three days," he said.

Pip felt the bottom drop out of her stomach, the way the harbor does when the sea pulls away. Three days. She had found her grandmother's note, and her grandmother's map was on a kitchen wall, and the day she had promised to come home was three days away.

"We have to tell somebody," Pip said. "We have to find the pool. We have to be there."

"We have to go down Crab Lane in daylight," Moe said, "and knock on the door of the man the whole town calls a monster. Because the note says tell Corwin."

Pip looked out the window at the sea, gray and gold under the summer sky, and thought of her grandmother out there somewhere, checking pools on other coasts, coming home to check the one she lost.

"The pool is real," Pip said. "She wrote it in a jar and threw it to the sea, and the sea brought it to me. If that's not a message, nothing is."

"It's a message," Moe said. "It's been a message for twenty years. We're just the ones who finally read it."

Chapter 15: Mr. Corwin

They watched the sea-glass house for two days, and the town watched them watch it, and nobody said a word.

It was low tide on the first day, and they lay on the grass above the lane with the taste of salt on their lips, and then the door opened.

He was small. That was the first thing. Pip had imagined a monster, and the town had given her a monster, and what came out of the house was a small old man in a patched coat, walking with a stick across his own garden.

He had a jar in his hand.

He crossed the garden slowly, the way you cross a room at the end of a long day. And when he reached the garden wall, he set the jar down on it — green glass, catching the light — and stood there a moment, looking at it.

"Look," Moe whispered. "He's setting a place."

It was true. It was exactly that. A man setting a place at a table for somebody who isn't there. He touched the jar once, gently, the way you touch a shoulder, and then he walked back to the house and shut the door, and the lane was empty again.

They came back at high tide and watched the house from the seawall. He came out again. This time he went down the beach to the tideline, slowly, with his stick, and bent, and picked up something small. A jar. A fresh one, in the green glass that the sea had been leaving all summer. He looked at it a long time, the way you read a letter, and then he put it in his pocket and climbed back to the house.

"He collects them," Pip said. "The jars. All those years. He's the one who's been collecting them."

"The town says he's a hermit," Moe said. "The town says he never leaves. The town says he watches the beach with a telescope and frightens the children off the shingle."

"He watches the beach," Pip said. "Because the jars come in on the beach. He's not watching to scare people. He's watching for the sea to deliver."

They lay in the grass until the light went gold, and then gray, and the house lit one small lamp in a window of bottles, and the green light fell across the lane like water.

"He's keeping something," Pip said.

"Keeping it for somebody," said Moe.

And for the first time, the house at the end of the lane did not look locked to Pip. It looked held. It looked the way her mother looked at the photograph she never opened: like a hand closed around something too dear to let go.

The next morning they went back, and this time Pip went all the way to the gate, and stood a long time with her hand on the latch, afraid.

"He's just an old man," Moe said behind her. "He sets jars on walls."

"He's the man who knows Gran," Pip said. "That's the thing I'm afraid of. That he'll tell me the family was right, and she's not coming back."

"Then you'll know," Moe said. "And knowing is the whole adventure."

Pip lifted the latch.

Chapter 16: The Recluse

They did not knock that day. Pip's hand was on the latch, and then it wasn't, and they went back to the grass and lay there and watched.

The house was quiet. The jar on the garden wall still stood where he had set it, a small green light in the middle of the day. And presently the door opened, and he came out again, and Pip understood that this was the shape of his day: the jar, the beach, the wall, the house. Like the tide. Out, and in, and out again.

He walked the tideline at low water, his stick making small holes in the shingle. He picked up a shard of glass, held it up to the light, and put it in his pocket. He dusted something inside the jar room — they could see him through the bottle-glass window, a small moving shadow among the green and blue. And then he sat on the seawall, facing the sea, and did not move for a very long time.

"He's not hiding," Moe said. "He's just... keeping watch. The way the lighthouse keeper used to."

"Mr. Finn," Pip said. "The museum man. He's the old keeper."

"Then ask him," said Moe. "About the professor, and the woman who came every spring tide."

But Pip was watching Corwin on the seawall, and she was thinking about her mother's closed face, and about the green jar on the garden wall, set there like a place at a table.

"Everything the town says about him is a rumor," she said. "Nobody's ever talked to him. Nobody's ever knocked. Twenty years, and the town made up a monster and never once opened his gate to check."

"And we're afraid to knock," Moe said.

"We're not afraid of him." Pip said it, and knew it was true, and said the truer thing: "We're afraid of what he knows. If he says she's gone for good, the whole summer dies. If he says she's coming back, then we have to believe it, and believing it is the scariest thing of all."

The man on the seawall stood up. He looked at the sea a long moment, the way you look at a letter you're deciding whether to open. Then he turned, and he saw them — two children lying in the grass above his lane — and he did not wave, and he did not frown. He simply stood and looked, the way he had looked at the jars, patient, taking them in.

Then he nodded. Once. And went inside.

"Did he just nod at us?" Moe said.

"He's not a monster," Pip said. "Monsters don't nod. Monsters don't set places at tables."

She stood up. The fear was still there, cold in her stomach, but underneath it something else had started, small and warm: the feeling you get when a door you thought was locked turns out to have a gate in it.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We knock. We tell him everything. The key, the house, the jar, the note. And then we ask him the question."

"What question?"

"Who was she," Pip said. "And is she coming home."

The sea, low and patient, kept time below them. The green jar caught the light and shone.

Chapter 17: The Question

They knocked in the morning, and the door opened so fast they knew he had been standing behind it.

He filled the doorway — not with size, but with stillness. Small, careful, in his patched coat. His hands hung at his sides, and his eyes were the gray-green of the sea in shade.

"Children aren't allowed down this lane," he said. His voice was low and slow, like the sound of the tide turning.

"We know," Pip said. "We've been here before. Twice. We came in the back way and opened your house with a key the storm washed up."

She said it all in a rush, the way you pull off a bandage: the key, the house, the jars, the one green jar she had taken, the note, the writing, the photograph, her grandmother's name. And when she was done she held out the note, unfolded, in both hands.

The man looked at it a long time.

Then he went very still, the way the sea goes still before a wave breaks. He reached out, his hand shaking, and took the note and read it.

"It came in on the storm," Moe said quietly. "The key. She wanted us to find it. That's what we think."

Corwin looked up. His eyes were wet, and he did not hide them. "Show me," he said. "Show me where you found it."

They walked down to the house in silence, past the green jar on the garden wall, and into the jar room. Pip watched him look at the empty place on the shelf where her jar had been.

"Her jar," he said. "Twenty years. I kept it, and the sea took it back, and the sea gave it to you."

"Who was she?" Pip asked.

The question hung in the green light of the bottle-glass windows. Corwin turned the note over in his hands, once, twice, the way you hold a thing you can't believe is real.

"She was my teacher," he said. "Dr. Iris Marlow. She taught me the names of things in tide pools. She taught me that the sea keeps everything, and gives everything back, late." He looked at Pip, and his voice was steady. "And she is not dead."

Pip felt the house go quiet.

"Everyone says she's gone," she said. "My mum says she chose the sea over us."

"She chose to look," Corwin said. "That's all she ever did. She went looking for a thing she believed was real, and the looking never stopped, because the finding never came. And she is still looking. I would know." He pressed the note to his chest. "I am the one she told to keep the house. I am the one she comes home to."

"She said she'd come back on the anniversary," Moe said.

"She has come back," said Corwin. "Every year, to check the pool. She never knew I watched." For the first time something in his face loosened, like a knot tied for twenty years. "But this year the sea brought you. And the pool is real."

Pip's hands were shaking. "The pool shines again," she said.

"That's what she's been waiting for," Corwin said. "And it's three days away."

Chapter 18: The Beach

With Corwin's leave, the key was no longer stolen. It was given.

They went down to the cove below the house at low water, Corwin leading the way with his stick, Pip and Moe behind him, and the sea-glass house stood above them full of light. The shingle had shifted in the storm, and the beach looked like a beach Pip had never met.

"Every year I walk this cove," Corwin said. "Every jar I find, I take inside. But the storm moved everything. Old things are coming up that the sea has been holding."

They walked the tideline. Pip found bottle shards ground smooth as buttons, a fragment of rope, a crab's shell like a small red castle. Moe found a gull's feather and put it behind his ear like a captain's.

And then, wedged in the rocks at the headland's foot, where the water pulled at their ankles, Pip found a jar.

It was new. The glass was bright, unclouded, and the note inside was rolled tight and dry. Pip's hands shook as she unrolled it.

The writing was small and firm. The writing she knew.

Still looking. The pool is real. The anniversary will come. — I.M.

The date was last year.

"She was here," Pip said. "Last year. This cove. She threw this jar into this water, and it never went anywhere. It's been sitting in these rocks for a year."

"Waiting," Moe said. "The way everything in this story waits."

Corwin took the jar and held it up to the light. His hand was steady, but his eyes were bright. "She still comes," he said. "Every spring tide, she comes to check. And every spring tide, the pool is still buried, and she throws a jar, and she goes."

"She's not gone," Pip said. The words felt big in her mouth. "She's not missing. She's looking. And she was here last year. She was here, in this cove, where I've stood every summer of my life."

"Proof," Moe said quietly. "Somebody said there was no proof. There's proof. She's alive, and she has not stopped."

They walked the whole cove then, the three of them, and Corwin told them the names of things: the whelk eggs like dried sea sponges, the razor shells, the anemones closing in the retreating water. He spoke the way he did everything — slowly, meaning every word.

"When I was your age," he said, "I knocked on a door and asked a scientist to teach me the names of things. She said yes. That one word changed my whole life."

"Gran," Pip said.

"Gran," said Corwin. "I bought this house because she asked me to keep the jars and keep the sea coming. I have kept the sea coming." He smiled, and the smile changed his whole face. "It seems the sea has kept something for her, too."

Pip put the new note in her pocket, beside the old one, and the two papers lay against her heart like the two halves of one thing.

"Now we find the pool," she said.

Chapter 19: The Lighthouse

The lighthouse sat at the end of the harbor wall, short and white, its light long dead. It was a museum now, the size of a house, and Mr. Finn kept it.

Pip had been inside a hundred times on school trips. But she had never once asked Mr. Finn a real question. He was just the old man who dusted the museum. It had never occurred to her that a keeper is a person who watched the sea for forty years.

Mr. Finn was small and brown and weather-folded, and he was polishing the lens when they came in. He looked up, and his eyes were the pale blue of a winter sky.

"You're the Marlow girl," he said. "And this is the boy who stays summers."

"We need to know about the professor," Pip said. "The woman who came to the tide pools. With notebooks, and a glass jar on a string."

Mr. Finn set the cloth down. He did not ask why. He went to a cupboard at the back of the museum, and from the very back of the cupboard he took out a box of logbooks, soft as cloth, bound in oilcloth.

"They're not the museum's," he said. "They're mine. I kept them, the way a man keeps his own watch." He opened one, and the pages were covered in the careful handwriting of a man who wrote down the sea.

"He said you remember the professor," Moe said.

"I remember everybody," said Mr. Finn. "A keeper remembers the sea, and the sea brings everybody."

"Here. Spring tide, twenty-odd years ago. Professor Marlow, low water, tide pools at the headland. And here, the year after. Professor Marlow again. Took samples."

Pip leaned over the page. Year after year, the same small entry in his hand: Professor Marlow. Spring tide, spring tide, spring tide.

"The year she stopped coming," Mr. Finn said, "I wrote her in anyway. Habit. And then the habit broke." He turned to a page near the end and pointed. The last entry was different: Professor Marlow. Rockfall at the headland. Pool buried. She came anyway, and stood, and went.

"He knew," Pip said. "He wrote it down. The pool was buried the year she left."

"I thought the rockfall might have taken her," Mr. Finn said gently. "I went to the headland with a lantern. But the cove was empty. I found her notebook instead, dry as a bone."

"Her notebook," Pip whispered.

Mr. Finn went to the cupboard again and came back with a small oilcloth parcel, and he set it in her hands as if it were a bird.

"The cove's memory," he said. "On paper. I kept it for her, the way you keep a place setting for somebody who's coming back."

Pip opened the notebook. The pages were stiff with salt, and the first page read, in the small firm hand she knew: On the existence of the glass pool, Saltcove. Notes toward a paper. — I.M.

She held it the way Corwin had held her note, and she understood that the town was full of people keeping Gran's things, every one waiting, none of them knowing the others waited too.

Chapter 20: The Archive

The local-history room was a small green-painted room in the town hall, three shelves of box files, and Mr. Ogden the librarian, who misfiled things and remembered everything.

He remembered the Marlow girl, and he remembered the boy who stayed summers, and he did not ask what they wanted. He only looked at Pip's notebook and said, very quietly, "Ah. The professor."

There was a box file marked SUMMER, one marked FISH, and one marked, mysteriously, BOTH. The newspaper clippings were in BOTH, because Mr. Ogden had put them there and remembered exactly where.

The first clipping was yellow and soft as a petal. SCIENTIST SEARCHES FOR LOST TIDE POOL. A photograph of Gran — younger, sleeves rolled, holding a jar — and a story Pip read twice: a marine biologist had studied the tide pools of Saltcove for years, had believed there was a rare pool at the headland's foot, hidden and unproven, and was petitioning the council for leave to study the cove.

"She asked the council," Pip said. "She didn't just go. She asked."

"She was a scientist," Mr. Ogden said. "Scientists ask. That's what the asking is for."

The second clipping was a letter Gran had written to the council, copied small in the paper. I believe the pool exists, and I believe the creature in it is found nowhere else. I ask only for the cove, and for the patience to look.

"Patience," Moe said. "She said patience."

"The pool was real," Pip said. "She knew it was real, and the council said no, and she went anyway, and everybody turned it into leaving."

Mr. Ogden took down another file, marked MAPS, which contained a drawing of the headland, and a survey of the cove, and, at the very back, where nothing about it belonged, a hand-drawn map on stiff paper.

The map was small and precise. The headland, drawn in pencil. The cove. The house. The lane. And at the foot of the headland, marked with an X, a basin drawn in blue-green pencil, and above it, in the hand Pip knew better than her own:

GLASS POOL.

And in the margin, a date. June. The date from the calendar. The date from the note.

"The anniversary," Pip said.

"It's a map to a day," Moe said. "It's not just a map to a place. It's a map to when."

Mr. Ogden leaned over the map, and his voice was almost a whisper. "She drew the cove the way a keeper draws his watch. She knew where it was. She just needed the sea to show it." He turned the map over. On the back, in Gran's hand, small and firm: If the pool shines again, she will come.

"Who's she?" Moe asked.

"Gran," Pip said. "She's talking about herself. She knew the pool would shine again. She knew she'd come back. She wrote it down so whoever found the map would know the whole shape of the promise."

Mr. Ogden wrapped the map carefully in its tissue paper and handed it to her.

"The town's long memory," he said, "is in these rooms. But a memory you can hold in your hands is better. Take it, Marlow girl. Take it and find what she was looking for.

Chapter 21: The Marine Biologist

They brought the map to the sea-glass house that afternoon. Corwin spread it on the kitchen table while Pip held her breath. It was a map of the headland drawn in pencil, the shoreline inked in blue, and beside a small circle at the foot of the cliffs, in Gran's hand, the words GLASS POOL.

"She studied the water," Corwin said. He spoke slowly, the way he always did, as if each sentence were a jar he was setting down carefully. "Your grandmother was a marine biologist. She studied the little rooms the sea makes—the pools the tide leaves behind. Every one of them is a world. And in most of them, she said, you find the same small things. But she believed one pool in this cove held a creature that lived nowhere else on earth."

"The glass shrimp," Moe said.

Corwin nodded. "She wrote a paper about it. People in her field said it did not exist. A shrimp you could see through, pale as a bubble, living in a pool with walls of glass. She said it was here. She spent years trying to prove it."

Pip thought of the photograph she carried—the woman at the tide pools, her back to the camera, a jar on a string in her hand. She had looked at that photograph her whole life and seen only a woman who had gone away. Now she saw something else.

"What happened to the paper?" Pip asked.

"It was published. People read it. They went looking for the pool on other coasts, in other countries. They found nothing." Corwin's hands lay flat on the table. "But she knew it was here. She was right."

"How do you know she was right?" Moe asked.

"Because she was the best I ever knew." Corwin looked at the map. "I knocked on her door when I was nineteen, with a net and a bucket and more questions than sense. She took me to the pools and taught me the names of everything in them. That was a gift I never paid back. So when she asked me to keep the house, I kept it. When she asked me to keep the jars, I kept them. It is the only thing I was ever asked to do that mattered."

Pip put her hand on the map, careful not to smudge the pencil. "She drew this," she said. "She was standing right here, planning her way back."

"She was never gone," Corwin said. "Not to me. She was looking."

Chapter 22: The Glass Pool

The next day Corwin took them to the seawall and pointed at the headland, where the cliffs leaned over the water and the shingle ran thin and black.

"The pool is at the foot of it," he said. "You cannot see it from here. You cannot see it from anywhere, most of the year. The water covers it. But on the year's lowest spring tide, the sea draws back farther than it ever does, and the pool is left standing above the waterline like a glass bowl set down on the rocks."

"What makes it glass?" Moe asked.

"Old bottles," Corwin said. "Years of them. Sailors threw them overboard. The sea smashed them and wore them smooth and piled them in the basin until the walls were green and blue and amber, packed tight as tiles. The water in it is so clear it looks like glass itself. That is why she named it the glass pool."

Pip tried to picture it: a bowl of bottles at the bottom of the world, full of water clear as air. "And she found it?"

"She found it. She studied it for years. She drew it, mapped it, watched the creature in it through every season. And then, twenty years ago, there was a rockfall on the headland. The cliff let go in the winter storms and buried the pool under tons of stone. The sea rose, the shingle shifted, and the pool was gone. She was standing on this seawall the morning she found out. I remember her face."

"What did she do?" Pip asked.

"She left." Corwin's voice was quiet. "She said she would find the creature again if it existed anywhere else. She said the pool had to be somewhere, and if it was here, it would shine again when the sea moved the stone. She gave me her keys and her jars and her house, and she said she would be back on the anniversary. Then she walked down the beach and did not turn around."

Pip watched the sea, gray-green, heaving, keeping its secret. Twenty years the pool had been buried. Twenty years she had been gone.

"So it was the rockfall that did it," Moe said slowly. "The year she went missing. The pool disappeared the same year she did."

Corwin nodded. "She did not leave Saltcove. She left the broken pool. And every year she came back to check if it had risen again. Every year the sea kept it buried. And every year she walked away and kept looking."

"The pool and Gran," Pip said. "They disappeared the same day."

"The sea buried them both," Corwin said. "And the sea is the only thing that could bring them back."

Chapter 23: The Map

That evening they carried the map to the jar room and laid it flat on the floor, weighting the corners with two green jars and one blue one. Corwin brought the lamp and knelt, and Pip and Moe knelt with him, and the room went quiet around the four of them, hundreds of notes standing watch on the shelves.

"It is the same drawing," Corwin said. He traced the pencil line of the shoreline. "I have seen her maps a hundred times. She drew every pool in this cove. But I never saw this one."

"Why not?" Pip asked.

"Because she never showed it to me." He turned the map over. "This is a working map. You can see where she erased and redrew, where she argued with herself on paper. But look—the edge is doubled."

He picked up the map carefully, and the bottom margin lifted. A fold, tucked under itself, so clean it had looked like the edge of the paper.

"There's a second page," Moe said.

Corwin unfolded it. The lamp light caught a drawing Pip had never seen: the headland, seen from below, and at the foot of it a basin drawn in patient cross-hatching, ringed with a circle. Beside the circle, in pencil: GLASS POOL. And at the bottom of the folded page, in smaller letters, the careful words: if the pool shines again, she will come.

Underneath, a date. The date in June. The date on the tide-table calendar in the kitchen.

"The anniversary," Pip said. Her voice came out strange. "She drew the date."

"It has been under the map's edge for twenty years," Corwin said. He looked old and small in the lamp light, and his hands were shaking. "I have kept this house since she left. I have dusted every jar, I have read every note. And I did not know she left me a map."

"You weren't supposed to need it," Moe said. "You were supposed to keep the house. She was coming back."

Corwin looked at the date, then at Pip. "She is coming back," he said. "On the anniversary. This June. That is three days away."

Pip put her hand over the circle, the way she had put her hand over the map's edge that afternoon, as if she could keep the pool safe by keeping it near.

"She drew the creature too," Moe said. He pointed at the folded page, where a small shape floated in the basin, sketched in three lines—an arc of a back, a tail, a whisker of a leg.

"The glass shrimp," Corwin said.

"Three days," Pip said. "We have three days until Gran comes home."

Chapter 24: The Tide

The tide tables hung on the jar-room wall, a column of dates and times printed small, with the year's extremes marked in pencil by a hand Pip knew.

"See this," Moe said. He stood on a crate, reading. "Every day the tide comes in and goes out twice. But it is not the same every day. The moon pulls the water, and when the moon and the sun line up, the pull is strongest. That is a spring tide. It is not a season. It is a happening."

"How do you know that?" Pip asked.

"I read the note on the wall." Moe pointed. "Whoever kept these tables wrote it down. Someone taught them well."

Corwin came in with a kettle, and stood looking at the tables. "Your grandmother wrote those notes," he said. "The day she left, she pinned a paper beside the year's lowest tide and circled it. She said the pool would need the lowest of the low. A spring tide that falls farther than any other all year. That is the anniversary."

"So the anniversary is not a date Gran chose," Pip said slowly. "It is a date the sea chose."

"The sea keeps time," Corwin said. "Your grandmother read the sea the way other people read a clock."

Moe climbed down and fetched the weather glass from the kitchen windowsill, a small blue thing with a spout. He held it up to the light. "My dad had one of these on a boat," he said. "When the water in the spout rises, rain is coming. You have to keep it level."

"You are a captain now," Pip said.

"I am the lookout," Moe said, and he hung the weather glass on a nail beside the tide tables. "Somebody has to watch the weather for the biggest tide in a year. It would be just our luck to get rain."

For the rest of that day they did what Corwin called the sea's arithmetic. They counted the hours to the lowest water. They read the moon in the evening paper. They stood on the seawall and watched the tide go out a little farther each time, as if the sea were practicing, as if it knew the date as well as the calendar did.

"Three days," Pip said to Moe as they walked home in the dusk. "Then the sea draws back, the pool shines, and Gran comes home."

"Or," Moe said, "the pool stays buried and she walks past us and we never know her. We have to be there. We have to see it before it hides again."

"Then we will," Pip said. "We have the key. We have the map. We have the tide tables. What else could a person need?"

"The nerve," Moe said. "And you have plenty of that. So we are set."

Chapter 25: The Low Water

The night before the lowest tide, Pip could not sleep.

She lay in her attic room with the window open, listening to the sea. It sounded different. Farther away, as if the water had stepped back to get a running start. She counted the waves and lost count, and somewhere around the end of the dark the sea went very quiet.

She woke at first light to a silence.

She went to the window. The beach below the house was wider than she had ever seen it—a great pale shelf of shingle where water had always been. The tide was out, and out, and out, farther than the tide had been in her whole life. The sea lay like a line drawn far away, silver at the edge of the world.

She pulled on her boots and ran down Crab Lane, past the seawall, onto the wet sand. The air smelled of the bottom of the sea—cold and green and strange. Rocks she had never seen stood up out of the water like the backs of sleeping creatures, black and barnacled and furred with weed.

The headland's foot lay bare.

She stopped. Something in the rock had caught the light—a shine the color of a bottle, green and low and steady, where no shine had any business being. It did not look like a wet rock. It looked like glass.

Pip turned and ran, boots slipping on the weed, back up the lane to Moe's window.

"Get up," she shouted, throwing a handful of shingle at the glass. "Moe. Get up. The sea went away."

Moe's face appeared at the window, hair standing up, half asleep. "What time is it?"

"It is the lowest tide," Pip said. "It is lower than the tables said. And something is shining at the foot of the headland."

Moe was awake then. He leaned out of the window and looked at the sea, and his mouth opened, and he said, "Is it—"

"I think it is the pool," Pip said. "I think it is shining again."

They did not stop for shoes. They ran, and the lane was empty and bright, and the whole town was still asleep, and the sea kept its distance all the way down, waiting for them to see what it had been keeping.

Chapter 26: The Pool

They woke Corwin by knocking on his door, and he came out in his boots with his coat over his arm, and he looked at the sea and did not speak. Then he picked up his jar and his net—the same jar on a string Pip had seen in the photograph—and he said, "Come on, then. But stay where I step."

They picked their way across the uncovered rocks. The world had turned to stone and weed and pool-light. Barnacles clicked underfoot. Water dripped out of holes in the rock, and the sea was so far away that its voice had gone thin, like a radio turned low.

"Careful," Corwin said. "These rocks have not been walked in twenty years."

The headland's foot was a broken place, tumbled and dark, where the rockfall had come down. Pip climbed over a slab the size of a car and stood at the edge of the shine.

And there it was.

A basin in the rock, as big as a kitchen table, its walls packed with sea glass—green and blue and amber, worn smooth as pebbles, set so close together they looked like a mosaic someone had spent a hundred years making. The water inside was so clear it looked like glass itself, so clear the pool looked empty, a bowl of light with the sky in it.

Then something moved.

At the bottom, drifting, tiny and translucent as a splinter of ice, a creature no bigger than a child's little finger—a back arched like a comma, legs trailing like thread, body so pale it was almost not there. It drifted. It flicked. It was a shrimp made of water and light.

The glass shrimp. The creature that was always waiting.

"Look," Moe breathed. "Look, Pip. There are more."

He was right. As their eyes adjusted, the pool filled with them—a whole company of pale commas drifting over the green glass floor, catching the light, letting it through. The pool was alive. It had been alive the whole time, keeping its creature, waiting to be found.

Nobody spoke. Pip could hear her own heart, and the far thin voice of the sea, and the drip of water somewhere under the rocks.

Then Corwin sat down. Not slowly—his legs gave way, and he sat down on the wet rock as if he had been folded, and he looked at the pool, and he did not try to hide that he was crying. He had kept this house for twenty years for a woman and a pool he was not sure either would ever shine again.

"It's real," he said. His voice broke. "It's real. She was right."

Pip knelt beside him and put her hand on his shoulder, and for a long time the three of them just watched the little creatures drifting in their glass world, while the sea, far away, kept its distance.

Chapter 27: The Finding

It was Moe who saw the jar. He had climbed down to the pool's far edge, where the glass walls met the dark rock, and he crouched there, staring.

"Pip," he said. "There's a jar in the wall."

She came around the pool, careful where she stepped, and saw it: a jar wedged in the green glass, corked and sealed with wax, old enough that the wax had cracked and the glass had fogged. It had been pushed deep into the pool's wall as if someone had placed it there deliberately, the way a person leaves a stone on a grave, or a note under a door.

"Don't move it yet," Corwin said. He had come up behind them, quiet as a tide. "The wax is all that holds it. Let it be."

"Whose is it?" Pip asked.

Corwin looked at it a long moment. "She kept a jar for her field notes," he said. "The same shape. The same wax. I would know it anywhere. That is hers."

Moe fetched Corwin's net, and Corwin worked the jar free with the patience of a man who had spent years being careful with glass. It came out with a small pop, and he held it up, and the light went through the green walls of it and made the water inside look like emerald.

"Read it here," Pip said. "Open it here. She left it for someone to find."

Corwin worked the cork loose with his knife. The note inside was rolled tight, the paper yellowed, the ink faded but clear. He unrolled it slowly, holding it flat with both hands, and read it aloud because Pip asked him to:

"If the pool shines again, I will come home on the anniversary. — I.M."

The words lay on the morning air. The sea, far away, was still.

"Another promise," Moe said quietly.

Pip looked at the jar in Corwin's hands, then at the pool shining at their feet, then back at the folded map she had left in the house, with its date in June and its words—if the pool shines again, she will come.

The note in the house. The note in the jar. The tide tables. The map. The pool, uncovered at last. Every single thing she had touched since the storm pointed to the same date, like compass needles all finding the same north.

"Tomorrow is the anniversary," Pip said. "And the pool is shining."

She held out her hands, and Corwin gave her the jar, and she held it the way she had held the first jar on the seawall, as if it were the only real thing in the world.

The shrimp drifted below them in their clear water.

Pip looked at Moe. Moe looked at Corwin. Corwin looked at the sea, and the sea was coming back in, slow and silver, far away still but returning.

"She's coming back," Pip said. It was the first time she had said it out loud to the day, and her voice did not shake. "Tomorrow. She is coming home."

Chapter 28: The Anniversary

The anniversary came on a day like any other in Saltcove.

The town went about its morning. Bread came out of the ovens on High Street, and the smell of it drifted down to the beach. Mrs. Pemberton opened the post office and sorted the letters. Mr. Finn swept the doorstep of the lighthouse museum. Nobody knew that the sea had drawn itself back to the very edge of the world, or that a pool of glass at the foot of the headland was shining in the sun, or that a woman was walking along the far curve of the beach toward a house that had been kept ready for her for twenty years.

Pip knew. She had slept with the jar under her pillow, wrapped in her best sweater, and she woke before the alarm and lay still, listening.

The sea was quiet again. The lowest tide of the year was coming in the middle of the morning.

She dressed in her good boots. She put on the sweater with the little jar in her pocket, over her heart. She went down the stairs and out of the house before anyone was awake, and met Moe at the seawall, and they walked together down to the sea-glass house.

Corwin was waiting at the door. He had brushed his coat. He had combed his hair. He looked like a man going to a wedding, or a funeral, or something he was not sure how to name.

"The tide is at its lowest in an hour," he said. "I checked the tables again this morning. She will come at low water, if she comes."

"If," Pip said. She did not like the word.

"I have said 'if' for twenty years," Corwin said. "It is a habit. Forgive me."

They walked down to the headland, the three of them, the jar in Pip's hands. The shingle was wet and wide and empty. The sea was drawn back to a bright far line. The pool lay open to the sky, its glass walls glowing green and blue, its little creatures drifting, the water clear as the air around it.

They sat on the rocks above it. Pip held the jar. Moe sat on one side of her, Corwin on the other, and none of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the sea was not already saying.

The sun climbed. The shadow of the headland shrank. The water in the pool hardly moved. The town behind them went about its morning, knowing nothing.

And far down the beach, where the sea had stepped back to the edge of the world, a small figure appeared.

Chapter 29: The Return

The figure at the far end of the beach was small against the sky.

Pip's heart gave a lurch she had not expected. For a moment she could not breathe. She had imagined this meeting so many times, in so many versions—the woman from the photograph, the voice from the notes, the grandmother she had wanted since she was nine. And here she was, walking toward them along the wet shingle, a rolled towel under one arm and a glass jar on a string swinging from her hand.

Corwin went very still. Pip heard him let out a breath, long and slow, like a man who had been holding it for a year.

"She comes every year," he said, very quietly, as if the figure might hear. "To check. She walks the whole beach, looks at the headland, and walks back the way she came. She never knew I watched. I never knew how to tell her I was watching."

"Every year?" Moe said.

"Every year since she left. On the anniversary. On the lowest tide." Corwin's voice was barely a whisper. "I thought she had stopped coming. Last year I walked the beach all day and she did not come. I thought—" He stopped. "I thought she had given up. Or I had missed her. I did not know which was worse."

The figure came closer. Sun-browned, small, brisk. Gray hair tied back. Walking with a limp that Pip noticed and then did not notice, because there was something about the way she walked that was exactly like the way Pip's mother walked, that was exactly like the way Pip walked. The same hurry. The same head up.

"Look," Moe breathed.

The figure had reached the foot of the headland. She stopped. She was looking at the uncovered rocks, at the place where the pool should be buried, where it had been buried for twenty years.

And then she saw the shine. The green shine of glass, lit by the sun, glowing where no shine had been in her lifetime of looking.

The woman stopped walking. She stood very still. She raised one hand, slowly, and pressed it to her chest, as if she had forgotten how to breathe.

And then her eyes found them—the three small figures on the rocks—and they found the jar in Pip's hands. Her own jar. The jar she had sealed and wedged into a pool she had never expected to see again.

She did not move. She did not call out. She just stood, small and sun-browned, in the middle of the wide empty beach, and stared.

"She's seen it," Pip said. "She's seen the pool."

"She's seen you," Corwin said. "She has never seen anyone hold her jar before."

Chapter 30: Gran

For a long moment nobody moved. The sea was a silver line at the edge of the world. The pool shone. The little figure stood at the foot of the headland.

Then she started toward them across the rocks, older than the photograph, seventy at least, the lines of sun and wind deep on her face—fierce and gentle at once.

She stopped at the edge of the pool.

She looked at it a long time. At the glass walls, green and blue and amber. At the clear water. At the little pale creatures drifting at the bottom, the creatures she had spent her whole life looking for.

"Dr. Marlow?" Corwin said. His voice cracked. "Iris. It's Amos."

Gran looked up. "Amos," she said. "You kept the house." It was not a question. She had known he would. "You kept everything."

"I kept the jars," Corwin said. "I read them all. Every one. I kept the sea coming, the way you asked."

Gran's eyes moved to Moe, and then to Pip, and then to the jar in Pip's hands. She stopped mid-stride.

"You found my jar," she said.

Pip held it out, both hands, the way you would hold something precious. "It was in the pool," she said. "Wedged in the wall. It said you would come home on the anniversary."

Gran took it and turned it in her hands, and her face did something complicated—joy, and grief, and twenty years of searching. Then she looked past them at the shining water, at the creatures drifting in it, and her voice came out strange and low.

"You found my pool," she said. "After all this time."

Pip did not know what to say. She had practiced nothing, planned nothing, and here was her grandmother, real and sandy and fierce, and all Pip could think was that she wanted her to stay.

"I'm Pip," she said. "I'm Philippa. Your granddaughter."

Gran's eyes filled with tears. She did not try to hide them.

"Philippa," she said. "Your mother named you after my mother. Did you know that?"

"No," Pip said.

"She was brave," Gran said. "She held on to things." She looked down at the jar, then at the pool, then back at Pip. "I have been looking so long that I forgot how to arrive. I walked this beach every year to look. I never once walked it to stay."

"Is that why you never came home?" Pip asked.

Gran was quiet a long moment. "I thought if I could see the pool shine again," she said, "I could come home and explain. And it would not shine. And I could not explain." She looked at the jar. "You did what I could not do. You found it without ever seeing it. You held it open for me."

She knelt on the rock in front of Pip, a small sun-brown woman, fierce and gentle. "Thank you, Philippa. For finding my pool. For keeping my jar. For believing I was coming back."

And Pip, who had wanted a grandmother since she was nine years old, stepped forward and put her arms around her.

The sea, far away, had begun to turn. The tide was coming back in.

Gran held her, and held her, and did not let go.

Chapter 31: The Reunion

For a long moment nobody moved. The tide was still out, and the only sound was the pool's slow shine and the sea far off, holding its breath. Gran stood where the water had drawn back, looking at the jar in Pip's hands. Her hair was the color of old rope. Her face was brown as driftwood, and at her eyes were lines like the map of a coast Pip had never seen.

"You found my jar," she said again, softer this time. And then, because Moe was grinning fit to burst and Corwin was not moving at all, she laughed. It was a rusty laugh, a door that had not been opened in years and had forgotten it could.

Moe said, "We found a lot of jars."

The walk back up the lane was slow. Gran looked at everything as if she had never seen it — the shingle, the seawall, the house at the top with its walls of green and blue sea glass. She stopped at the gate. Corwin opened it without a word and stood aside, and Gran walked into her own house as if she had been gone a day instead of twenty years.

The jar room did the rest. Pip watched her grandmother go shelf to shelf, green to blue to amber, her hands on the glass the way other people touch photographs. She did not even take the notes out. She knew the jars the way Pip knew her own bedroom. "June," she said once, softly. "October. This one's the winter storm of ninety-eight." She turned, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling. "He kept them. Every one."

Corwin stood in the doorway, small and careful and strange. "You said keep the sea coming," he said. "I kept it coming."

Pip understood then, standing in the green light of the bottles, that the house had never been empty. It had been waiting — the way a harbor waits, the way a jar waits for a note. All those years the door locked, and the house had simply been holding her grandmother's place at the table.

"Welcome home," Pip said.

Gran looked at her then, really looked, and Pip saw the woman from the photograph, the one at the tide pools, young and fierce and glad. "Thank you," she said. "For unlocking it."

Chapter 32: The Story

They told the story twice that evening — first in the jar room, standing, and then at the kitchen table, with tea. Corwin had tea. He had kept tea for twenty years in case someone came, and now someone had, and he made it the way a person makes a speech he has been practicing.

Gran talked for a long time. The pool was real, she said — a basin at the foot of the headland, its walls made of old sea glass, water so clear it looked like nothing at all. It held one creature found nowhere else on earth: the glass shrimp, small and translucent, drifting like a piece of the water that had decided to move.

She had found it the year Pip's mother was born. She had spent a decade studying it, writing it down, loving it in the careful way of scientists. And then, one winter, the rockfall came. The headland shifted and buried the pool, and the shrimp with it, and she could not dig it out alone.

"I thought I'd be gone a year," she said. "There were other coasts. Other pools with strange creatures. I thought if I found enough of them I could understand the one I'd lost. And every summer I came back to check. And every summer it was still buried."

So she kept looking. She sent her bottles out from the cove because she believed — had always believed — that if you give the sea a message, the sea will carry it, late, to whoever needs it. And she wrote the letters to Corwin, the jars, the map, the promise.

"I never stopped being your family's scientist," she said, to Pip, and then to Mum, who had come because Pip made her come and stood in the doorway with her arms crossed and her face doing nothing at all. "I only couldn't explain the seeking. So I said nothing. And the silence —" Gran's voice went thin. "The silence became the story."

Mum did not move for a long time. Then she said, "You could have told me."

"I know," said Gran. "I know I could have."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the sea was coming in, filling the cove again, the way it had always filled it, the way it had always meant to.

Chapter 33: The Student

After the table was cleared, Corwin told his half.

He had been nineteen, he said, a boy from the cove who had never left, who worked the boats and thought the sea was only weather. Then one spring tide he had seen the professor on the rocks below the headland, a small woman in waders with a glass jar on a string, and she had looked up and said, "You're standing on a beadlet anemone. Watch."

He watched. He watched all spring. She taught him the names of things — the beadlet anemone, the porcelain crab, the lugworm's cast like a pile of little ropes. She taught him that a tide pool was a country with its own laws, that the sea kept its own calendar, that a notebook was an adventure.

She did not leave suddenly. That was the thing the town never knew. She left like a season. In her last spring she stood on the seawall with the jars and said, "Keep the jars, Amos. Keep the sea coming. I'll be back." And she gave him the key.

He bought the house because nobody else bid at the estate sale, and because the jars would not fit anywhere else, and because he believed her. He had kept her believing ever since. Every bottle that washed into the cove he pulled from the sea and read, and if it was a message — a wish, a name, a ring that fell overboard — he put it in a jar, dated it, and set it on a shelf, so the sea would have somewhere to put its mail.

"The town thinks you're a monster," Moe said, and then looked horrified at himself.

Corwin considered this. "I know," he said. "It helped. Nobody came down the lane to ask questions, and there were years I couldn't have answered them."

"You answered ours," said Pip.

Corwin looked at her, and his eyes were bright. "You showed me the map," he said. "You found what I'd been keeping for twenty years and didn't know I had. I kept the promise. You kept the proof."

Chapter 34: The House, Open

The next morning, the door stood open.

Pip noticed it first from the seawall, at a distance, like noticing a face you know looking up at you. All her life the door had been shut; it was the first thing you knew about the house, the way you know a lighthouse is a lighthouse. Now it was open, and the green light of the bottles spilled out onto the garden path like something poured.

The neighbors were shy at first. An old woman from the cottage row stopped on the lane and stood a long time, and then walked up and stood in the doorway with her hands clasped, not quite inside. Mr. Grant, who fished the end of the cove, came because someone told him the professor's house was open and he wanted to see with his own eyes.

Corwin lit the stove. Pip watched him move through his own house like a host who has forgotten how, offering tea in chipped cups to people he had not spoken to in years. They came slowly, in twos and threes, and the jar room filled with the town's quiet wonder. Nobody talked much. There is a sound people make in a place that has been closed and is open again — a soft ah, over and over, like the sea.

A girl Pip's age touched a green jar and whispered, "Is this real?" and Gran, who was sitting on the window seat with her jar on her lap, said, "It's real. It's all real."

The house as a kept promise, kept at last. Pip stood in the middle of it and thought of all the locked doors in the world, and how one of them had come unlocked, and how it had taken a storm and a key and a cousin and a grandmother to do it.

"You did this," Mum said from the doorway, and Pip turned, startled. Mum was looking at the house, not at her. "You opened it."

"No," Pip said. "The key did."

Mum shook her head. "The key just fits a lock." She came in a step. "It takes somebody brave to turn it."

Chapter 35: The Lane, Unbanned

The town council met in the church hall on Tuesday, and Mrs. Pemberton took the old sign down herself.

She did it that morning, at first light, so that the lane was bare and strange all day, the nails left in the post like a question mark. The sign was warped and salt-worn — "No children beyond this point" — and she carried it into the meeting under her arm like a loaf of bread, and set it on the table in front of the town councilor.

"Crab Lane," she said, "is not a crime. It's a road. And the house at the end of it is open."

There was a debate. Somebody said signs had kept children safe for twenty years. Mrs. Pemberton said the seawall had kept them safe, and the sea had kept them safe, and a lock had kept them out, and all of it had worked, and now none of it was needed. The councilor hemmed. Corwin, who had been summoned and had come, stood up and said, in his slow way, "There was never anything down that lane to hurt a child. Only a man, and a house, and a promise."

They voted. The sign stayed down.

Pip heard about it from Moe, who ran all the way up the lane to tell her, and then she walked down it in daylight, alone, past the seawall she had fallen from at nine years old. She put her hand on the stones. She remembered the scrape, the fright, the family rule that had grown around her like a garden wall. And now here was the lane, just a lane, with grass in the middle where the carts had stopped rolling, and a house at the end of it with its door open and its windows full of green light.

She walked the whole length of it slowly, because she could. Because she was allowed. Because it was a lane again.

At the top, Gran was standing in the garden, waiting for her, and Pip thought: this is what the sign was for all along. Not to keep her out. To make the coming home matter.

Chapter 36: The Jars, Shared

On Saturday, the jars came out of the house.

Tables went up along the lane — trestles and doors on barrels and one ironing board from Mrs. Pemberton's — and the town carried the jars out in crates, shelf by shelf, the way you'd carry a library into the sun. By mid-morning the lane was lined with them, green and blue and amber, a hundred and more, each with a note rolled inside like a seed.

Pip was afraid at first. The notes were strangers' secrets; she had kept them in the dark for weeks, and now the light was on them. But Gran said, "That's what they were made for. Handing things on." So Pip pulled the sailor's good-luck note out of its jar and read it aloud to the first knot of people, and the note was lucky, and the people clapped, and the fear went out of her like a breath.

The girl's wish was read by the postmistress, who wept a little and said she hoped the wish came true, wherever the girl was now. The wedding-ring note — from a man who had lost his ring overboard and written to the sea to say it was all right, the ring had done its job, the marriage was the thing that mattered — was read by Mr. Finn, the lighthouse keeper, who had to stop twice. The apology. The "we made it." Every stranger walked into the lane and stood among the living, and the town said, one by one, "That was brave," and "I hope they're all right," and "The sea brought it to us for a reason."

Then Gran read her own note.

She read it the way you read a letter from a friend — quickly, familiarly, as if she had read it a hundred times, which she had. "To whoever finds this. I am looking for something I lost in this cove. The glass pool. If it shines again, tell Corwin. I will come back on the anniversary. — I.M."

The lane went quiet. Pip watched her grandmother fold the note and put it back in the jar, and she understood that the note was not a message anymore. It was a receipt. The sea had delivered it, late, to the right hands.

And then the crowd cheered. They cheered until the gulls lifted off the roof, and Gran laughed and covered her face, and Pip stood in the middle of it, holding the jar, feeling the whole town come home.

Chapter 37: The Summer

Gran stayed.

That was the whole of it, the small miracle of the season. She stayed, and the days lengthened around her, and the house at the end of Crab Lane went from being a mystery to being a kitchen with the door open and the smell of bread in it.

Mum and Gran began the slow work of being mother and daughter again, and it was not fast, and it was not easy. Pip watched them circle each other like two boats learning the same current. They made jam together, one Saturday, in Gran's kitchen — gooseberry, the old recipe from the book with the floury spine — and Mum kept her face turned away while she stirred, and Gran said nothing about the years, and by the end the jam had set, and something small and real had been made. Another day they sat in the garden and went through the photograph album, page by page, and Mum asked questions, and Gran answered them plainly, and once, late, Pip heard Mum say, "I was so angry," and Gran say, "You had a right."

Pip and Moe had a summer with a grandmother. Tide-pool lessons at low water, where Gran showed them the beadlet anemone and the porcelain crab and the tiny lives that shut themselves up against the air. Stories of other coasts — red pools and green ones, a cave full of anemones like a garden turned inside out. Breakfasts that lasted an hour because nobody wanted to be the one to leave the table.

Corwin came down most evenings and played chess with Gran by the jar room's light, and the house filled with the click of pieces and the two of them, quiet, twenty years of kept promise settling between them like dust in sun.

Pip kept the green sea glass in her pocket. Some days she took it out and held it, worn smooth as a thumb, and thought about the bottle it had been and the sea that had made it beautiful.

"You're doing it again," Moe said once, catching her.

"Doing what?"

"Being glad," he said, and he was smiling, and so was she.

Chapter 38: The Pool, Shown

The next low water was a big one, and Gran went down to the pool alone.

Pip watched her from the headland — a small figure in waders, walking the uncovered rocks the way a person walks a room they once lived in. She stood a long time at the pool's edge, not touching anything, just looking. Twenty years of looking, Pip thought. And here it was, shining.

They gave her a while. Then Pip and Moe went down, picking their way across the wet rocks, and Pip said, "Gran. There's something you should see."

Gran turned. Her face was careful. "I've seen it," she said. "I've seen it a hundred times, in my head."

"No," said Pip. "You haven't seen this."

She knelt at the pool's edge and waited. The water was clear as air, clear as glass, and for a moment there was nothing in it but the sky and their three faces leaning over. And then a drift — a small, translucent thing, no bigger than Pip's little finger, moving the way water moves, as if it were a piece of the pool that had decided to live.

And then another. And another.

Gran made a sound Pip had never heard — a small, broken ah, like a knot coming loose. She went down on her knees in the wet and leaned over the pool, and the shrimp drifted in their clear thousands, more than she had ever counted, more than she had ever dared hope for, the whole lost nation of them, waiting in the glass water like the answer to a question she had asked every day for twenty years.

"The pool is hers again," Moe said quietly, and Pip nodded, because it was — not found, but shown. The seeker had been given her own treasure back by children, the way the sea gives things back, late, when you have almost stopped expecting them.

Gran did not speak for a long time. When she did, her voice was rough. "They waited," she said. "All this time. They waited for the pool to come back."

"The pool never left," Pip said. "It was just buried."

Gran looked at her, and her eyes were bright, and she laughed that rusty laugh again. "You sound like me," she said. And Pip took it as the highest praise she had ever been given.

Chapter 39: The Library

Mr. Ogden made a display.

It was his idea, and it was a good one, which surprised everyone including Mr. Ogden, who spent most of his days in the local-history room of the town hall misfiling things and remembering everything. He cleared the long glass case in the entrance hall and filled it with the life of the cove, and he did it properly.

The notebooks went in first — Gran's, four of them, with the tide tables and the small careful drawings of beadlet anemones and porcelain crabs and the long columns of dates. The map went in beside them, its edges unfolded flat to show the headland and the word GLASS POOL in Gran's hand, and the date, and the drawing of the creature. And the jars' stories went in on little cards, each one a stranger's name-or-not, the sailor's good-luck, the girl's wish, the wedding ring, the "we made it."

Pip and Moe helped build it. They wrote the labels out in their best handwriting, arguing about the spelling of "anemone" and getting it wrong twice and fixing it. They laid the glass shrimp's drawing in the center of the case — a small translucent shape on white paper, with a line beneath it in Mr. Ogden's careful capitals: FOUND AGAIN IN THE GLASS POOL, SALTCOVE.

Mr. Ogden stood back and looked at his work, and his face did something rare, which was to let the rest of him show. "History," he said, "is mostly what got lost. This is the part that got found." And he looked at Pip and Moe as if they were part of the display, which they were, in a way they had not understood until then.

When the doors opened, the first visitors were the children of Saltcove, who pressed their noses to the glass and asked whether the shrimp was really real, and Pip said yes, and Moe said it was probably looking at them right now, and the children went quiet and then asked to be shown the pool.

"The pool's only out at the lowest tide," Pip said. "But Gran's giving a talk about it. Saturday. You can ask her."

Saturday. The word went around the hall like a ripple, and Pip watched her summer become public, and found, to her surprise, that she did not mind at all.

Chapter 40: The Talk

The library talk was on Saturday, and the town came.

They came the way they had come to the jars — slowly at first, then all at once, until the meeting room in the town hall was full and people stood at the back and children sat on the floor in front. Mr. Ogden had set up the display case so it faced the chairs, so the talk would happen in front of Gran's whole life, like a person standing in their own history.

Gran was nervous. Pip could tell — the way she kept touching her jar, the way she looked at the door. Pip sat in the second row with Moe. Mum sat in the front row, alone, her hands folded on her knees.

Gran began with the tide. She told them how the moon pulls the water, how a spring tide is not a season but a happening, how twice a year the sea goes far out and shows its floor. She told them about the pool — the basin of sea glass at the foot of the headland, hidden under the water, shining one day a year. And then she told them about the shrimp.

The room went quiet when she described it — a creature found nowhere else on earth, small and translucent, drifting like a piece of the water that had decided to move. A creature she had found, and lost, and looked for for twenty years. Pip watched the town's faces change as she spoke, watched them understand that the professor had not been a strange woman who left; she had been a woman who kept coming back, to look.

Gran stopped. She looked down at her hands for a moment, and then she looked at Mum, and her voice went thin and true.

"I am sorry I was away," she said.

The room held its breath. And then Mum stood up — nobody had expected that — and walked the two steps to the table, and sat down beside her mother, and put her hand on Gran's hand on the table. She did not say anything. She did not have to. The family story was being rewritten in public, gently, the way a tide rewrites a beach — not all at once, but certainly, and for good.

When Gran found her voice again, she finished the talk, and the town clapped, and the clapping went on so long that Pip thought the walls could not hold it. In the front row, Mum and Gran sat with their hands together, and Pip watched them and thought: this is the sea delivering. Late, but delivered.

Chapter 41: The Celebration

The day of the anniversary came round again, and this time the whole town knew about it.

Mrs. Pemberton had seen to that. From the post office door she told everyone who came for stamps, and everyone who came for stamps told someone else, and by midsummer the date was written on the church-hall noticeboard in blue ink: Lowest tide of the year. The Glass Pool. All welcome.

The lane was strung with lights. Pip helped — she and Moe and Mr. Corwin wound the string from the sign at the top to the garden wall at the bottom, and the bulbs hung like beads of sea glass on a thread. When dusk came and they lit, the whole lane glowed green and blue and amber.

At the door of the house a small brass plate shone, new and clean. Pip read it twice to be sure she had it by heart.

The Sea Glass House. Kept by A. Corwin for I. Marlow, who came home.

Mr. Corwin stood beside her and did not say anything, and Pip did not say anything either. Some sentences do not want company.

Down on the beach the sea had pulled away, farther than the town had ever seen it, and the pool lay open at the foot of the headland, shining like a window lit from inside. The children of Saltcove lined the rocks above it and pointed. The grown-ups held hands and went quiet. It was the kind of quiet a church is.

Gran walked down with them. She wore her old red coat and carried her glass jar on its string, and when she saw the pool shining she stopped and put a hand on Pip's shoulder, and Pip felt the small fierce hand press and press.

"You found it," Gran said. "All of you."

Someone had brought a fiddle. Someone else had brought bread and cheese and a big pot of tea, and Mrs. Pemberton had brought her best tins of biscuits, which she had sworn she was saving. The tide was at its lowest, the water miles out, and the town stood on the beach that had never been theirs and danced.

Pip danced with Moe, who was terrible at it, and then with Mr. Corwin, who was worse, and then with Gran, who was wonderful. The lights ran up the lane like a string of small suns. The sea kept its distance and watched.

And at the very end, when the water began, slowly, to come home, Pip stood in the foam with the green sea glass warm in her pocket and thought: this is the story now. We are the ones who found it.

Chapter 42: The Sea Glass

After the festival, the beach was different. Not because the water had moved things — it had, a little — but because Pip saw it now the way Gran saw it, the way Corwin saw it: as a place that had been waiting all along.

She found the piece on the morning after, at low water, half-buried in the shingle by the cove. Green. Worn smooth as a thumb. It caught the light the way the pool caught the light, the way the house caught the light at dusk.

She took it to Gran, who was sitting on the seawall with her bare feet in the sun, and held it out in her open palm.

"That's sea glass," Gran said. "Once it was a bottle. Someone drank from it, or shipped something in it, and it got broken, and it went into the sea. And the sea — " Gran picked it up, turned it in her fingers. "The sea knocked it around for years. Sand, stones, tide, tide. It took the sharp edges off. It made it beautiful."

Pip turned that over in her head. "So the sea fixed it?"

"The sea was patient with it," Gran said. "That's different. The bottle didn't ask to be broken. But it let the sea work, and look what came of it." She put the piece back in Pip's hand and closed Pip's fingers over it. "The town's like that. The house is like that. Even me. All of us were something sharp once, and the sea — well, the sea worked on us."

Pip looked down at the green glass, worn smooth as a thumb.

"Will you keep it?" Gran said.

"Always," said Pip, and she put it in her pocket, and she could feel it there all the way home, a small round warmth against her leg.

From then on she carried it. On the lane, on the beach, to school when summer ended and the town got quiet again. When she was worried, she touched it. When she was happy, it was there. It was a broken thing the sea had made beautiful, and so, she decided, was the whole family. They had been patient with one another, the way the sea is patient, and look what had come of it. This house. This lane. Gran, home. The piece of glass in her pocket that was not a secret and not a key but a kept thing, worn smooth as a thumb.

Chapter 43: The Key, Kept

The key lay on the kitchen table for a whole day before anyone decided what to do with it.

They were all there — Gran, Mr. Corwin, Moe, and Pip — and they had washed it clean of barnacles the week before, and it gleamed now, dark iron, old, heavy. The key that had opened the house. The key the storm had brought.

"I don't think it should lock anything anymore," Pip said. "It's done that."

Gran nodded slowly. "The door's not meant to be locked. It's meant to be open. But the key — the key deserves to stay."

So Corwin found a length of ribbon, faded blue, the color of the sea on a calm day, and threaded it through the key's old eye, and tied it around the latch of the front door. It hung there, swinging a little in the wind off the water, saying nothing at all. It had said everything already.

"It doesn't lock," Moe said, testing the door. It swung wide on its hinges.

"It doesn't have to," said Pip. "It says what it did."

She thought about the day she had found it, crusted with barnacles on the tideline, the morning after the storm. She had hidden it in her pocket, afraid and thrilled, a stolen thing. And now here it was, on a ribbon over the door of a house that belonged to them, and it was not a stolen thing at all. It was a kept thing. A key could do that, she was learning. It could make a thing a mystery, and then make it a home. The sea had given it up, and they had given it a home.

Moe said, "So nothing locks it now?"

"Nothing locks it," Gran said. "The house locks nothing it can hold. That's the point of a house."

Pip reached up and touched the key, cold and heavy on its ribbon. It had opened the house. It had opened the lane. In a way it had opened the whole family, the way you open a jar, or a book, or a door you were told never to try. It hung over the door like a word, and Pip knew the word, though the key could not say it. The word was home. And the house, which had waited twenty years behind a locked door, waited now behind an open one — a different kind of waiting, and a better one.

Chapter 44: The Note, Written

The jars had a rhythm now. People brought them. People took them. And one afternoon, when the house was quiet and the tide was out, Pip knew it was her turn.

She sat at the kitchen table with a clean jar, empty and green, and a piece of paper, and a pencil. Moe was on the seawall, keeping watch, because some things you write alone, even with your best friend three doors away.

What do you say to the sea?

Gran's note had been a message to a stranger: I am looking for something I lost. I will come back on the anniversary. Signed I.M. The sea had carried it for years, and the house had kept it, and it had come home.

Pip put the pencil to the paper. She wrote her name, because that seemed right — Philippa Marlow. She wrote the date, because Gran's notes always had dates, and a date is a way of saying: this happened, this is true, this is when. Then she stopped.

A promise. That was what a note to the sea should be. Not a wish — wishes were for the girl's jar, and the sailor's, and the ones who had sent their hoping. A promise was different. A promise was what you could be counted on to do.

She wrote: I will keep the house open. I will keep the lane a lane. I will tell the story, and when I am older I will tell it again.

She read it twice. It sounded like her. It sounded like something she could be counted on to do.

She rolled the paper small and slipped it into the jar, and the jar felt suddenly heavy in her hands, the way a held door feels heavy, the way a real promise does.

She carried it to the jar room. The shelves shone, green and blue and amber, every jar a small life handed to the sea. Hers was the newest, the smallest, the one that had not traveled yet. But it would. That was the whole point. The sea delivers, late, but it delivers.

She set her jar on the shelf among the strangers, and it looked at home there, the way a new voice finds its place in a song. "That's that," she said, and she went out to the seawall, and Moe did not ask what it said, and she did not tell him. Not yet. The sea could have it first.

Chapter 45: The New Jar

The next morning there were two.

Pip came in early with Gran to open the windows and air the house, and there on the shelf beside her jar was a second one — a small blue jar with a note rolled inside, and on the note, in Mrs. Pemberton's round handwriting, a letter to the sea.

"It's catching," said Gran, smiling.

They read it on the doorstep, the two of them. Mrs. Pemberton had written to a man she had loved when she was young, before the war, before the town, to say that she had kept his letters and that she still had the photograph and that she hoped he was well. The sea, she trusted, would deliver it, if not to him, then to someone who needed it. "The sea always delivers," Gran said, "late," and she and Pip stood together in the doorway, and it felt like the two of them had started something, or rather like the house had.

By the end of the week there were seven.

Mr. Finn came with a note about a ship he had kept lit for, a name, a date, a thank-you. Mr. Ogden came with a note that was mostly a list of things he remembered, in case the sea was interested in memory. Two jars came in the post, from children Pip did not know, who had heard the story and wanted to send their hoping to the sea. One of them was a wish, one of them was a "we made it," and Pip read them both and felt the hair stand up on her arms, because they sounded exactly like the strangers in the old jars — the sailor, the girl, the man with the wedding ring. Only now the strangers had faces, and the faces were her town.

"It's the same," she said to Moe that afternoon, standing in the jar room, counting. "The old jars and the new jars. It's the same thing, happening again."

"That's the point, isn't it?" said Moe. "The sea keeps delivering."

The community of strangers had become a community. The notes went out, and the notes came back, and the jars went out and came back full, and the shelf filled, and Corwin built another shelf, humming under his breath, and the house hummed along with him, full of small paper lives waiting for the tide.

Chapter 46: The Winter

Winter came to Saltcove the way it always came, gray and wet and long, and this winter the sea-glass house was warm.

Moe had gone home with Aunt Ruth, and school had swallowed the other children, and the beach was empty but for the gulls. But the lane was never dark now. The windows held their light all evening, amber and green, and the key hung on its ribbon over the door, and the door was open whenever the weather allowed.

Pip came after school most days. She would find Corwin and Gran at the jar-room table, the chessboard between them, a pot of tea going cold between moves. Gran played fierce and fast; Corwin played slow and certain. They could sit an hour without a word, and the silence was the friendliest thing Pip had ever seen. It was the silence of people who had waited a long time and were in no hurry now.

Pip did her homework at the end of the table. Sometimes the jars watched her. Sometimes she read them — the old ones, the new ones, all of them — and the room smelled of tea and salt and paper, and the rain came down beyond the bottle-glass windows, green and blurred, and the house held her the way a held door holds a room.

On the darkest evenings, when the tide was in and the sea was loud, she walked the lane anyway, boots in the puddles, breath in the cold. At the door she would reach up and touch the old iron key on its ribbon, cold and heavy and true, and she would think: open. The word lived in her like a small warm coal. The house was open. That was all. Open, and open meant alive, and alive meant everything had turned out the way a promise could.

Rain ran down the bottle-glass windows in green rivers. Inside, the chess game went on. Outside, the sea kept its long, patient calendar. And Pip, at the door, with the key above her and the sea glass in her pocket, was home.

Chapter 47: The Next Summer

The summer came back, and Moe came back with it.

He arrived on the first bright day, taller than the year before and just as quick, and he stood in the lane with his bag and looked at the sea-glass house with its open door and said, "Last year we broke in. This year the light's on." Pip laughed, and they ran down the lane the way they had never been allowed to run it, and the house took them in like a room that had been keeping their places.

The anniversary was three weeks away, and the town had not forgotten it. Mrs. Pemberton had a rota for the lights. Mr. Finn had polished the brass plate on the door until it gleamed. And the marine station — a new thing, a trailer on the harbor with two students and a lot of waterproof paper — had come down from the university to count the shrimp.

They came to the house, the students did, with clipboards and waders, and Gran walked them down to the pool at low water, and they counted, and they counted again, and one of them, a girl with glasses, kept saying "This is not in any book" in a voice that got higher each time.

"There are more than I ever counted," Gran said, coming up the beach that evening, pink with cold and happiness. "More than in my paper. The pool is full of them. It was waiting for us."

And the council met, and the pool was made a protected place — the first in the county, they said, a pool where a shrimp no one else had found lived and was looked after. There was a small sign on the rocks: do not touch, only look. It was the same sign, Pip thought, that had once been at the top of the lane, only backwards. That one had said do not come. This one said come, and look, and keep it.

On the anniversary the whole town went down at low water. Not to find anything this time — it was found. Not to solve a mystery — it was solved. Just to stand at the edge of the water and watch the pool shine, and the shrimp drift like glass motes, and to keep it, together, the way you keep a thing that was given to you.

The secret had become a thing everyone keeps. That, Pip decided, was the best ending a secret could have.

Chapter 48: The House, Alive

In August the house was never dark.

Lights burned in every window — the kitchen, the jar room, the little bedrooms upstairs — and the light spilled down the lane in long green and amber pools, and the house hummed with voices the way it had hummed with silence for twenty years. The two things sounded, Pip thought, very much alike, if you were listening for the same thing, which she was.

People came and went all day. Mrs. Pemberton came for tea and to check the jars, because she had decided the jars were partly hers now, and no one disagreed. Mr. Finn came to sit by the jar room and tell the children which ship each note had washed in from, though he made most of it up, kindly. Mr. Ogden came with armfuls of the archive, books and clippings and the map, and built a corner of the house into a small museum, because, he said, "a story this good should be kept in more than one place."

And the jars themselves had a life now. They went out in bags and came back full. A jar went out with a girl's wish and came back with a "we made it" from a family in the next town. A jar went out with a thank-you and came back with a question. The sea kept delivering, and the town kept writing, and the house kept both, the way a house should keep its things.

Pip stood at the top of the lane one evening and counted the lit windows, and Moe stood beside her and did not make a joke, which was its own kind of seriousness.

"It's not a mystery anymore," Pip said.

"Nope," said Moe.

"Is that — is that sad? That it's not a mystery?"

Moe thought about it. "I don't think so. I think it's the best thing it could be. A mystery is a door nobody's opened. This is a door that's open. You can't live in a mystery. You can live in a house."

And there was Corwin, at the door, letting people in. Small, careful, kind, and busy with it — setting out tea, finding jars, showing children the note in his teacher's handwriting. The recluse was gone. The keeper was home. And the house was not a mystery anymore, and that was the best thing it could be.

Chapter 49: The Lane

The lane was loud all summer.

Children ran where children had been banned. They swung on the gate at the top and tore down the slope to the seawall and dared each other to touch the sea-glass house's walls, which glittered green and blue in the sun, and the house bore it all, patient as the sea. The sign that had said NO CHILDREN was gone. Mrs. Pemberton had taken it down herself, the day of the meeting, and hung it on the church-hall wall with a card that said: taken down the year the sea gave the key back. Moe had pointed out that it was not the sign's fault, and Mrs. Pemberton had said, "No, but it's time it retired," and that had been that.

Pip was on the seawall on a bright afternoon, the green sea glass in her pocket, the tide out, when she saw the girl.

She was maybe twelve — Pip's age — and she stood at the top of the lane where the old sign had been, one hand on the gate, staring down at the house. She had a backpack and a look Pip knew by heart. It was the look of a person standing at the edge of a story and deciding whether to step in.

Pip had worn that look. At nine. From this very seawall.

She slid off the wall and walked up the lane, and the girl watched her come, a little wary, a little brave.

"You wondering about the house?" Pip said.

"It's been locked for twenty years," the girl said. "Everyone says so."

"Not anymore," said Pip. "It's open. Do you want to see the jars?"

"What jars?"

Pip smiled. The question was the whole thing, the whole story, the whole summer and the winter and the storm and the key. It was the question she had asked, and the question Gran had asked, and the question the sea had been answering all along, late.

"Come on," she said, and held out her hand. "I'll show you. The story's best from the start."

And she thought, as the girl took her hand and they walked down the lane together, that the story would be told again. That was what a lane was for. That was what a house was for. A door opens, and someone comes in, and you tell them, and then they tell someone, and the sea keeps delivering, and the lane goes on being a lane, and the house goes on being a house, and that is how a story stays alive.

Chapter 50: The Beach, Ours

The morning of the last day of summer was the lowest water Pip had ever seen.

The sea had drawn away from the beach until the headland stood bare and the pool lay open at its foot, shining like a window with the light on inside it. Pip and Moe and Gran walked out across the uncovered rocks, and the whole of Saltcove was behind them on the shingle, quiet, because the pool still had the power to make a crowd go still.

Gran lowered herself onto a rock and took off her shoes, and Pip and Moe did the same. The three of them sat at the edge of the water. At the bottom of the pool the glass shrimp drifted, tiny and translucent, like glass motes in a glass of light, and the green and blue walls of the pool held them and shone.

Gran reached into her bag and brought out a jar on a string — an old one, green, worn smooth — and put it in Pip's hands.

"For your own work," Gran said. "Every scientist I ever knew had one. A jar on a string. You lower it into a pool, and you wait, and you look. That's the whole method. Wait and look."

Pip turned the jar in her hands. It was heavier than it looked, full of a question.

"And this," said Gran, "is for whoever comes after." She lifted out a second jar — small, clean, corked — and handed it to Pip. "Your note's answer. You wrote to the sea. Now the sea — well, the sea has its own reply, or it will. You throw it in. You let the tide take it. Someone, sometime, somewhere, will find it. The sea always delivers. Late."

Pip held both jars. One for looking. One for leaving.

She stood up, and the beach was all light — the pool shining at her feet, the house open at the end of the lane like a door held wide. Behind her she could feel the town on the shingle, and Moe at her elbow, and Gran with her bare feet in the water, and the house and the lane behind her, all kept, all open.

She threw her note's answer back to the sea.

The jar arced out over the water and went in with a small, round sound, bobbed, turned, and began, slowly, to travel. It carried her name. It carried the date. It carried her promise, read once and kept forever.

The sea took it. The sea always took things, and kept them, and gave them back changed, late, beautiful.

Pip stood at the edge of the water with a jar on a string in one hand and the green sea glass in her pocket and her grandmother's hand on her shoulder, and understood at last: you give the sea a note, and the sea gives you back a stranger's, and the house keeps both, and the lane goes on, and the story is told again, and the sea keeps everything you give it.

The house was open behind them. The lane was full of light. And the sea, patient as ever, was keeping everything.
