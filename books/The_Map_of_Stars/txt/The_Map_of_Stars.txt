The Map of Stars
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Wrong Chart

The library at Morrow Observatory was the coldest room Juno Mercer knew, and she knew every room in the building. The radiators coughed and died at the east wall, where the photographic atlas took up two shelves that reached to the ceiling, each volume the size of a paving stone. Tonight she was laminating the 1976 set, plate by plate, because her father had said the spines were going, and a caretaker's daughter does not let the sky crumble in its own library.

Her breath went before her in small white comets. The winter dark pressed at the tall windows, and beyond them, somewhere in the black, the real sky was out doing its real work — patient, enormous, indifferent to the cold of one girl in a freezing room.

She worked from nine to eleven. At the eleventh plate she stopped.

It was the chart of Corvus, the Crow. The plate was dated March 1976, printed the winter of that same year. Kraz, Beta Corvi, magnitude 2.6 — she knew it the way you know a house you grew up in — was marked half a moon's width southeast of where it had to be.

Not where she thought it should be. Where it was. Juno had been drawing the sky from direct observation since she was nine, which meant she had drawn Kraz — its position against Algorab and Gienah and Minkar — close to nine hundred times, and she had drawn it correctly every single time. The atlas was wrong.

She checked the plate once, twice. Held it to the light of the single lamp. Her heart did something strange and quick, like a bird going up from the grass.

The observatory kept everything. Her father kept everything. Her mother had taught her the names of the sky before she died, and the drawings were the only family language left that worked, and she had nine years of them, and all of them said the same thing.

The chart is wrong, she told the freezing room, and the room did not answer, and the wrongness stayed put.

Something small and precise had been moved. It was not a mistake that happens by accident. It looked deliberate. It looked like the kind of deliberate you build a life around.

She set the plate down gently, the way you handle something that might, given enough attention, speak.

Half a moon's width, she said aloud. Someone did that on purpose.

The winter held its breath. Above the hill, the Crow was rising in the southeast, crossing just above the East Ridge line, carrying its wrong star like a message nobody had opened yet.

Chapter 2: The Observatory

The Morrow Observatory had been a research station for fifty-three years and a museum of itself for fifty. It stood on Lookout Hill above the town of Morrow, a white brick cylinder with a dome on top that someone had once compared, in the town paper, to a bishop's hat on a tin of ashes. The comparison stuck. The building had been closed to research in 1976, which meant it had belonged ever since to caretakers — and for the last decade, to Sam Mercer, and to his daughter, who had the run of everything and was the only person under sixty who used any of it.

Juno could have walked the building in her sleep. The visitor center with its dusty diorama of the 1923 dedication. The two rooms of exhibits behind ropes. The library, where she had spent last night. The cold little theater with the film that no longer ran. And the dome itself, home of the Old Glass — the twelve-inch refractor, brass and mahogany and ninety years of cold — the telescope her father wound up with a crank, winter and summer, because a telescope that is never turned on forgets how to turn on.

The rituals of the caretaker's winter were as fixed as stars. October: drain the pipes in the west wing. November: check the roof. December through March: the building became a siege — frost on the inside of the dome shutter, thermometers read twice a day, the thermostat a god she and her father served with coal and patience. The observatory leaked heat like a sieve leaked water. It leaked it on purpose, as if it liked the cold, as if the cold were part of the point of a telescope.

Once a week her father walked the full circuit with his clipboard, and once a month she walked it with him, and every time, at the same point in the west corridor, they both did not stop.

The west corridor was the one place Juno did not have the run of. An iron grate, padlocked with a lock older than her father, sealed the hallway that led to the last staff astronomer's office. The door at the end was pale oak with a brass nameplate — WREN, I. — and it had been locked since March 1976, when the astronomer walked up the East Ridge in a storm and did not come down. Fifty years. The observatory had kept her office the way a house keeps a dead person's room, and nobody had ever told Juno why, and she had never asked, because asking would make it real.

Tonight, walking home through the snow with her father, the cold like a hand laid flat on her face, she said, Who was Iona Wren?

And her father, who had not looked up at the sky in years, looked up at the sky, and then looked down again, and said: Let's get you inside. You're cold.

Chapter 3: Juno's Drawings

Juno's bedroom was on the third floor of the caretaker's house, and it had one window that faced south, and above her desk the wall was covered, floor to ceiling, in the sky.

Nine years of notebooks, each one a season. The spines were labeled in her own handwriting — nine, ten, eleven, up to sixteen — and each book held a hundred and twenty-odd nights, minus the clouds, of the sky drawn from direct observation. Not copied from any atlas. Drawn on the hill behind the house, pencil and ink, her eye going up and her hand going down, the same conversation she had had with the night since she was nine, when her mother was dying and the doctors had said, gently, that there was nothing more to do, and Nell Mercer had said, Show her the sky. It's the only thing that stays.

The ritual never changed. Coat, gloves with the fingertips cut out. The hill. The charcoal warm-up sketch. Then the careful placing of the brighter stars, then the faint ones, then the patience of letting the eye find what the eye hides. The drawings were not art. They were measurements — Juno was ferocious about that — but they were made the way you make a thing you love: slowly, in the cold, by hand.

It was her mother's fault, the whole family language of it. Nell had taught her the constellations the way other mothers taught lullabies. Algorab, she would say, and point. Gienah. Minkar. Kraz. And Corvus, the Crow — our little crow, Nell had called it, the one that comes home. Juno had been seven when Nell died. The names had stayed.

At the bottom of the drawer, wrapped in a moth-eaten scarf, was the box her mother had left her — an old biscuit tin with a photograph of a woman on the lid, a woman with her mother's chin. The box held Iona's sketches: the same stars, drawn forty years earlier, in the same patient hand. Juno had opened it once, the day she turned nine, and cried, and never opened it again, and now, in the cold of her room, with the wrong chart burning a hole in the middle of her nights, she opened it again.

The sketches were beautiful and precise. And there, in the corner of the last one, in a hand that might have been her great-grandmother's grandmother's, was a note in pencil:

For Nell. The crow always comes home.

Juno's hands shook. She did not know why they shook.

Chapter 4: The Fraction

She measured it the way she measured everything — by hand, three times, with a protractor and a piece of thread, because her father had taught her that a ruler lies and geometry does not.

The printed chart showed Kraz southeast of its true position by a fraction of a degree. Half a moon's width. The full moon, seen from Morrow on a clear winter night, spans about half a degree of sky — a coin held at arm's length, an error you could hide behind, an error you would never notice unless you had drawn the same star nine hundred times and knew, the way you know your own handwriting, exactly where it lived.

She checked the printed chart against her first notebook, the one from when she was nine. The notebooks agreed with one another and disagreed with the chart. Every single one. She laid them out on the floor in a circle around her, nine books of sky, all of them crying the same thing: the chart is wrong.

But it could be her. This was the part that scared her, the part she had to do honestly, because her mother had taught her that an astronomer's first duty is to distrust her own eyes. So she did the math.

Precession. The slow wobble of the Earth's axis — a full circle of the sky every twenty-six thousand years — shifts every star's position, but it shifts them all together. It is a uniform drift, a slow turning of the whole map. Juno worked the numbers for the fifty years since the chart was printed, using the observatory's old textbooks, and came out with a shift smaller than her thumbnail, and — this was the important part — the same for every star on the plate. Precession could not have moved Kraz alone. Precession had not moved Kraz. Something had.

Plate warp, the survey crew would say later. Dust on the lens. A printer's error. But plate warp bends a whole sheet; a printer's error does not come out to exactly half a moon's width in exactly the direction of the East Ridge; and a chart printed in 1976 could not have warped into an error that pointed, star for star, toward the one place in the sky where a light on the ground could be mistaken for a star.

She sat back on her heels in the ring of notebooks. The cold came up through the floor. The clock on her desk ticked.

Zero point four two degrees. She had measured it, and measured it, and measured it.

It was not an accident. It was a fraction. And a fraction was a ratio, and a ratio was a relationship between two things — and she was the only person on the hill, maybe the only person in Morrow, who had ever noticed that the map of the sky had been quietly, deliberately wrong for fifty years.

Alone with the wrongness. The winter pressed at the window. Above the hill the Crow rose, carrying its misplaced star, and Juno looked up through the glass at the true sky, and the true sky said nothing, and she felt, for the first time in her life, that the sky was waiting for her to say something first.

Chapter 5: The Shift

She told her father first, over breakfast, sliding the laminated plate across the table the way you would show someone a wound.

Dad squinted at it, turned it toward the window, turned it back. Plate's old, honey. Fifty years of the attic. Paper moves.

Paper doesn't move half a moon's width in one direction, she said.

Juno. He set the plate down and poured himself coffee, the same gesture he had used to end every conversation since her mother died. The observatory's been closed fifty years. Old things get things wrong. That's all this is. Leave the sky alone.

Leave the sky alone. It was the kind of sentence a caretaker says to a building, not a father to a daughter. She took the plate back to the library and did not say anything more to him for three days, and he did not ask, and that was the shape of their grief — the two of them orbiting the same sun without looking at it.

The astronomy club met in the school basement on Tuesday. The club was Juno and four boys and a teacher who read from a photocopied sheet. She showed them the plate and her notebooks. Precession, said the teacher, without looking up. It's covered in the textbook. She told him she had done the math; he told her precession did things she was not equipped to calculate; the boys nodded; she left.

The survey crew came in February — three people from the Hawke-Merrin Institute, in a truck with a dish bolted to the roof, measuring the darkness of the sky for a company that wanted to buy it. Juno found their site engineer in the visitor center and spread her evidence across the counter.

Dust on the lens, the engineer said, barely glancing at it. Plate warp. Digital will fix all of it. She opened her mouth to argue; he was already turning away, and so she did what she always did — she went up to the dome, alone, and she disproved them all.

Plate warp: she measured the plate's full grid against a fresh one from the vault. The grid was true; only Kraz was wrong. Dust on the lens: she photographed the same field, same night, with the Old Glass, three focus settings, and Kraz landed true every time — the sky itself said so. Precession: she had already done it.

She stood in the freezing dome with her three proofs and no one to show them to, and through the eyepiece she caught, far below on the road into town, the survey truck's lights, small and foreign, climbing the valley. In the dark behind the windshield a figure sat turned toward the observatory — the driver, or the survey astronomer, a man she could not see the face of, watching her hill.

She lowered the eyepiece.

Half a moon's width, she said to the cold. And they all looked at it and saw nothing.

The dome did not answer. The Crow was climbing the ridge. Somewhere on the East Ridge, where the wrong star pointed, there was nothing but dark trees and snow — and a light that did not exist on any chart.

Chapter 6: The Redraw

The decision came to her whole, the way decisions come when you have stopped asking permission for them.

If the chart is wrong, she told the empty library, I'll draw the true one. She had the skill. She had nine years of proof. The sky had been telling her the truth since she was nine, and she had been writing it down, and nobody had ever asked to see it, and so she would make them see it. She would draw the sky the way it actually was, and the map would be so obvious, so clearly right, that everyone — her father, the teacher, the survey crew, the whole of Morrow — would have to look twice and admit that a sixteen-year-old with a pencil had caught what the observatory had been hiding for fifty years.

That was the plan. That was the whole plan.

She waited three days for clear sky. The observatory had taught her patience the way the river teaches the stone — Morrow's winter was a wall of cloud, and she used the waiting to sharpen pencils, to mix ink, to lay out a fresh notebook with a title page: the true chart of Corvus, drawn from observation, by J. Mercer. In the corner she drew a small crow, in profile, the way her mother had once shown her.

The first clear night came on a Thursday, cold and perfect, the air so dry the stars looked like they were burning. She stood on the hill behind the house for two hours, drawing. Algorab. Gienah. Minkar. Kraz. She placed each star where the sky placed it — patiently, honestly, three checks per star — and when the cold had taken the feeling from her fingers she went in and thawed them over the radiator, and came out again.

Three clear nights she did this. Thursday. Friday. Saturday — and Saturday the seeing was the best of her life, the sky a black dome with every faint star showing, and she drew Corvus as she had never drawn anything: complete, corrected, beautiful, Kraz set true at last, half a moon's width home where the chart had kept it exiled.

She finished at two in the morning. She had been nine years working toward this page without knowing it.

On the hill, in the cold, she held the notebook up against the sky, as if to show it to the constellation itself — see, this is you, this is right — and for a long moment the world was exactly as it should be: the map true, the ghost in the drawer settled, the Crow in its place.

She went to bed, her hands shaking from cold and joy, and she did not know — could not have known — that the sky had been waiting for exactly this, for someone to draw it true, and that it was about to answer.

Chapter 7: The Answer

She woke because something was wrong, and she could not have said how she knew, only that she knew, the way the observatory's old brass knew when the temperature was dropping.

She got up. Her window faced south. Corvus was climbing the sky beyond the ridge, low over the East Ridge line, and she looked for Kraz, found it by habit, and then she was very, very awake.

There was a second light.

Not a star. A point of light where no star was — where no star could be — sitting exactly at the position of the false star on the chart, the place the sky said was empty. Half a moon's width southeast of the true Kraz. Exactly where the wrong map said the Crow should carry its brightest star.

It was the position of the error. It was the position she had just drawn away, the empty socket the corrected map had healed. And it was lit.

She stood at the window a long time, her breath frosting the glass, waiting for her eyes to trick her. It did not go. It did not move. It did not twinkle the way stars twinkle. It was steady and small and wrong, and it was where the wrong chart said the sky was, and the corrected chart said it was not.

She could not have said when she started shaking. The notebook was still on her desk, open to the true chart, the last page she had drawn, Kraz corrected, the whole honest map of the sky. And the sky was answering it. That was the only word her mind would form — answering — a light appearing in the one place she had just moved a star away from, as if the sky had been waiting all her life for her to draw it true, as if her pencil had broken a seal.

She pulled on her boots and her coat and went out onto the hill in her nightgown, and the cold hit her like a wall, and the light was still there — closer now, or she was closer to it, she could not tell — a single patient point above the black trees of the East Ridge, where the land was observatory land, where no one went, where no one had gone in fifty years.

Stars don't do that, she said. Her voice came out small in the enormous night.

The light burned steadily, answering nothing, saying something.

She could not have explained it to anyone — could not have explained why she felt that the wrongness had finally been heard, that she had drawn the map and the map had done the one thing maps never do. It had written back.

She stood on the hill until her teeth were chattering, and the light stayed, and she counted without meaning to: one, two, three, a pause, one, two — a beat, a rhythm, like something with a pulse.

Chapter 8: The Flare

She should have slept. Instead she took the dome key from the hook by the caretaker's door — the key her father trusted her with, the key to the Old Glass — and she climbed the spiral stairs into the dome, and she opened the shutter to the night, and she put the twelve-inch refractor on the light.

It was a hard target. The dome was built for the zenith; the ridge light crouched low in the southeast, over the trees, where the Crow lived. She had to crank the great tube down until it groaned against its stops, and even then she lost it in the finder, searched, found it, lost it again — her hands numb in minutes, the brass colder than the air, the cold of it climbing her wrists like a slow tide.

But she got it. She got it in the main eyepiece, and what she saw made her go still.

It would not focus at infinity.

She racked the eyepiece out, all the way out, and the point became a disc, and a disc meant light that was not parallel — light that had not traveled a thousand light-years — light that had come from somewhere close. From somewhere on the hill. From somewhere on the ridge.

She checked her focus against a real star. The real star snapped to a needle point of light, sharp as a pinprick. She swung back to the light. It stayed a soft disc, out of focus at infinity, refusing, like a thing with a body, a thing with a distance, a thing that was only a few miles away.

Not a nova, then. Not a star at all.

Not a satellite — satellites move, and this did not. Not lightning — lightning does not repeat. It was repeating. It was doing the same thing over and over, and she had seen it do it — she was sure now — a pattern, a pulse, a sequence that began and ended and began again.

Clockwork, she thought, the word coming from nowhere, the word for something that runs by itself, faithfully, without anyone there. Clockwork.

She got her father's stopwatch — the one he used to time the dome's opening, the one that had not been used in years for anything that mattered — and she timed it. The light flashed. She timed. It flashed again. She wrote the intervals in the margins of her notebook, her pencil in her bare hand, her breath frosting, the numbers coming out faster than she could think.

Pause. One, two. Pause. One, two, three. Pause.

She timed it until the sky paled. She timed it until the light began to lose itself in the dawn, and only when it was gone, swallowed by the rising sun, did she let herself stop — and her hand was cramped around the pencil, and the margins of the page were full of numbers, and she sat in the freezing dome with the Old Glass still pointed southeast, at the place where the chart was wrong, and she thought, with a clarity that had no fear in it at all:

It wasn't a star. It was a message.

Chapter 9: The Pattern

She laid the numbers out on the kitchen table the next morning, on the only clear space, which happened to be the middle of the table where her father had left his mug.

She had timed the light through most of a night. When she wrote the flashes down as marks — a dash for the long ones, a dot for the short ones, a space for the pauses — they arranged themselves into groups. Three long, two short, space. One long, three short, space. It was not random. She knew random — she had spent nine years watching a sky full of it. This had a shape. This had a grammar.

Patterns are language, her mother's voice said, from the deep safe place where Nell Mercer still lived. Juno had heard it said of the constellations themselves — lines of chalk between bright points, and once you saw the pattern you could not unsee the language.

She mapped the flashes into a clean table. Long and short, grouped and separated. It was maddeningly, thrillingly deliberate. It was a person, on the other end of fifty years, doing a thing on purpose.

She wanted to tell someone. She almost told her father at breakfast, the next morning, when he came down and found her bent over the table.

He looked at the page of dots and dashes over her shoulder. What's this?

The light on the ridge, she said. It flashed all night. It's a pattern. It keeps repeating. Dad, I think —

Juno. He picked up his mug. That's the Old Pit, or a hunter's lamp, or the survey crew stringing wire. It's not our business. He looked at the page again, and something crossed his face — not anger, not quite worry, something older — and he said, You're seeing patterns where there are none. That's what you're doing. A light on the hill at night is a fire hazard, and you're out on that ridge in your nightgown freezing yourself, and I need you to leave it alone.

She did not argue. She was too good at reading him to argue — she could see he was not worried about a fire hazard. He was worried about her, the way he had been worried about her since she was seven, the way he never said. And the terrible part was that he was right and wrong at once, and she could not explain the difference to him, because the difference was fifty years old and locked in a room at the end of the west corridor.

She took her page of flashes up to her room. She looked at it for a long time, and she felt — beneath the thrill, beneath the certainty — the thin cold edge of fear. Something on the ridge was talking. Something on the ridge had been talking, faithfully, every clear night, for how long? — and she was the first person in fifty years to write it down.

Patterns are language.

She had never been so afraid of being right in her life.

Chapter 10: The Code

She spent a week on the code, and the code spent the week winning.

Morse was the first thing, the obvious thing, and it was not Morse; the groups were too regular, the durations wrong. She tried assigning numbers to the long and short marks and summing them; she tried treating the pauses as separators between letters; she tried reading the flashes backward, which produced nothing, and forward, which produced nothing she could hold. She filled a notebook. The notebook looked, by Friday, like the working notes of a person talking to a wall.

The light went on every clear night. It did not change. It repeated the same sequence, exactly, patiently, like a bell that had been told to ring and would ring until it was answered. That was the worst part, and the best part — the patience of it. Someone had built a thing that would keep its promise longer than it could.

Somewhere in there is a name, she told the notebook. Or a direction. Or a date. Something. It's all there. I just don't have the key.

She needed someone methodical. Someone who trusted numbers the way she trusted the sky — someone who would sit with a page of dots and dashes and not decide in advance that it was a fire hazard. She had never needed anyone's help in her life and said so, frequently, out loud.

Her father found her that Saturday in the library, buried in the code books, and he did not ask what she was doing. He had stopped asking. Instead he said, in the dry, careful voice he kept for difficult subjects, Your physics grades, Juno.

Fine.

They're not fine. They're failing. Your teacher wrote to me. She says you keep derailing the class to argue about the chart.

Because the chart is wrong. She did not look up. The textbook says precession. Precession is uniform. It's not uniform. The teacher is wrong, the survey crew is wrong, and — she did not say, and you, but they both heard it.

Which is exactly why, her father said, I've hired you a physics tutor. Name's Leo Fenn. Seventeen. Top of his year. He's the station engineer's son, and he's good with machines, and his father says he trusts numbers.

Juno looked up. Her father met her eyes for the first time in she could not remember how long, and there was something almost like a challenge in it, almost like a dare, the way he used to look at her when she was small and learning to read the sky, before the looking up stopped.

A tutor, she said flatly.

A tutor, he agreed. Starts Monday. He'll be here after school. Be ready. He turned to go, and then, without looking back, he added, softly, into the freezing air of the library: Maybe he'll even trust yours.

The door closed. Juno sat alone among her books, the pattern laid out on the table, the wrong chart propped against the atlas shelf, and something in her chest — something that had been alone with the wrongness for nine years, and wrong about it for zero of those years — eased, a fraction.

I need someone who trusts numbers, she said to the empty room.

Monday. She had a plan for Monday.

Chapter 11: Leo

The boy her father hired was punctual, which Juno had decided to distrust before he knocked.

He came up the hill on the bus that died at the end of the rail line, carrying a messenger bag and the smell of wet wool, and stood in the visitor center doorway stamping snow off his boots like he had somewhere better to be. Seventeen, maybe a year past her, with the patient look of a person who had fixed things.

"I'm Leo," he said. "Fenn. Your father said physics."

"He said I'm failing," Juno said. "He didn't say failing what."

"Physics." Leo smiled, not at her, at something in the room. "You're the one who drew the wrong constellation. Everyone in town's talking about it."

She had expected dismissal and got a statement of fact. It unsteadied her more than mockery would have.

She brought him to the library, past the freezing rows of the photographic atlas, and laid the 1976 chart flat on the table. Kraz sat half a moon's width southeast of where it actually was — she had stopped being able to see it any other way, the wrongness bright as a lit window.

"There," she said. "That's the wrong chart. Precession can't do it. Plate warp can't. Dust on the lens can't. I've checked."

Leo did not look at the chart. He looked at her, and then he said, "Show me your drawings."

It was the last thing she expected. Nobody had ever asked to see the drawings. They were her private language — nine years of notebooks, the sky as it had looked to her from Lookout Hill on nine hundred clear nights — and she had never once been asked.

She fetched the newest notebook, this winter's, and set it between them. He turned the pages slowly, the way a person reads a letter from someone they love. He stopped at the Corvus pages, three nights running, the corrected constellation, Kraz at its true place, the arrow she had drawn from chart to sky.

"You were right," he said, in a voice gone careful. "You were right, and everyone told you you were wrong."

That night he came back up the hill after supper, in the cold, with a theodolite he had borrowed from his father's workshop and a star chart app glowing on his phone. She stood beside him at the Field behind the ridge, breath freezing, and watched him level the instrument on Kraz and read the angle, and then read it again, and check it against the phone, and check it against the sky.

"Point four two degrees," he said at last, amazed, almost angry. "Southeast. Exactly what you drew."

He lowered the theodolite and looked at her, and the snow had stopped, and the whole sky was out.

"Now," he said. "Show me the flashes."

Chapter 12: The Decoding

For two weeks they sat at the observatory kitchen table, and the library froze and the kitchen held its heat, and the flashing went on outside in the dark like a patient hand tapping a glass.

Leo timed. He had brought his father's stopwatch — the station engineer's, brass and reliable — and he sat with it in his fist, counting under his breath, while Juno called out the flashes as she saw them through the Old Glass. Long. Short. Long. The pauses between them were what made it talk.

"Look," Leo said on the seventh night, pushing a page of his notebook across the table. "Treat it like Morse, but it isn't Morse. Long flashes are ones. Short flashes are zeros. The gaps are separators. Three lengths of gap, like words, sentences, paragraphs."

Juno looked at the grid he had drawn, the neat rows of ones and zeros, and felt the floor of the observatory tilt a half degree.

They worked in silence. Leo converted groups to numbers; Juno looked them up — star positions, magnitudes, times. The first group was a coordinate, familiar by now to her whole body, the southeast corner of Corvus where the chart was wrong and where nothing, by the sky, should be.

The second group was a date.

The third was numbers she did not recognize, and the fourth, when Leo at last pushed his chair back and read it aloud, was not numbers at all.

"I-O-N-A," he said. "Letters. It spells Iona."

The name hit her like a door closing. She had heard it in her mother's voice, in the box of sketches Nell had left her, on the plaque in the west corridor she had never once been allowed past.

"My grandmother," she said, and her hands were shaking, so she put them flat on the table to make them stop. "Iona Wren. That's her name."

Leo was very quiet. Then he bent to the last group, the longest one, and worked it the way he had worked the others, steadily, checking each conversion against his own grid.

When he was done he did not read it aloud. He turned the paper around and slid it to her, and she read it in the yellow kitchen light, her heart going like a clock being wound:

TO THE ONE WHO REDRAWS THE SKY.

She had to breathe twice before the words would stay put. All those nights of drawing, all those years of being the only one who looked closely enough to see the wrongness — and someone had known. Someone had watched the sky the way she watched it, and had made the wrongness itself into a message, and had aimed it at the one person in fifty years who would redraw it.

"Juno," Leo said. "Your grandmother built a light that talks. And it's talking to you."

She looked up at him, and the yellow light held them both, and outside, beyond the steaming window, the patient hand tapped on in the dark, still spelling, still waiting for the rest of the sentence.

Chapter 13: The Message

They worked until the kitchen clock stood past two, and Leo's stopwatch ticked on the table between them like a heart, and one by one the remaining groups surrendered their meaning.

Numbers first, because numbers were the family language. A pair of terrestrial coordinates, marked with a small crescent — moon-crossed, Juno thought, the way a chart marks a grave. And another pair, celestial, with the symbol for a comet and a date beside it, the same date the flashing had given them, written twice as if for emphasis.

Then the words, in the careful order of a woman who had known she would not be there to say them.

"To the one who redraws the sky: I have left the map and the instrument. The crow returns this winter. Come to the place where the chart is wrong."

Leo read it aloud once, and then again, and the second time his voice had gone soft, the way it did when a number refused to be explained.

"The crow," Juno said. "Corvus." The constellation she had redrawn, the wrong place in it that had flared and flashed and finally spoken.

"Not just Corvus," Leo said. He had the celestial coordinates flat on the table, and he was tracing from them to the date, and the date to the bright red circled note he had made two weeks ago, the morning he had checked Iona Wren's prediction against the sky. "The crow returns this winter. Juno, there's a comet coming. Perihelion this winter. The coordinates put it inside Corvus."

The room went very still. Juno felt the floor of the observatory tip under her again, and this time she let it.

"A comet," she said.

"Iona Wren predicted it," Leo said. "In 1976. Fifty years. And it's coming back, through the constellation you spent nine years drawing." He looked up at her, and his eyes were bright and almost frightened. "She didn't just leave you a wrong chart and a flashing light. She left you a map and an instrument, and she told you to come to the place where the chart is wrong."

"Where the chart is wrong," Juno repeated. The southeast corner of Corvus. Half a moon's width from Kraz's true place, out toward the ridge, where the flat stone Field ran down toward the Old Pit.

She reached across the table and took the paper with the terrestrial coordinates, and held it up to the yellow light.

"That," she said, "is where she is."

Outside the window the flashing went on, patient, spelling its one long message into the dark: come to the place where the chart is wrong. And for the first time Juno believed the message was truly finished, and that the next part was hers to answer.

Chapter 14: The Astronomer

The archive lived in the library's frozen back stacks, and Juno went through it the way she had always gone through the sky — by attention, by patience, by refusing to let any detail go.

Dr. Iona Wren, 1929–1976. The observatory's last staff astronomer. Comet-dynamicist — the catalog listed her papers, orbit after orbit, comets the way other people kept gardens. She had built her own instruments, the logbooks said, in a workshop on the East Ridge. The old accounts called her "the last of the hand-made astronomers," as if she had outlived her own kind.

Juno pulled the 1976 paper on Corvus proper motions, the one that proved she had known exactly how far each star had moved — the paper that made the deliberate wrongness of the chart unarguable. She had calculated every proper motion to the second of arc. She had known Kraz to the fraction. And then she had drawn it half a moon's width from where it truly was, and signed her name beside the error, and let the chart go to press.

The Wren prediction was in the same bound volume. C/1976 J1, a comet she had discovered and computed by hand, by torchlight in her field station — period fifty point three years, perihelion in the spring of 1976, next return this winter. The journal had published it with a note: "pending independent confirmation." Nobody had confirmed it. She had vanished that March, and the prediction had drifted into the archive, a number without a believer.

Juno read the papers twice, and then a third time, and the woman emerged from them the way a constellation emerges from scattered points of light. Precise. Warm in the margins — she had annotated her own proofs in the margins, jokes and curses and one underlined line: "the fixed and the faithful." Stubborn. She had argued with the board about keeping the observatory open at night. She had taught the caretaker's boy to use the transit. She had signed her letters "I.W., with the sky on."

She had built a clockwork star on the ridge, and aimed it at the wrong place in her own chart, and left it burning for fifty years until the right person looked.

Juno closed the volume and sat in the cold with her breath smoking, and understood that she had not only inherited a wrongness. She had inherited a woman — fierce and funny and alone with the sky — and that woman had been waiting for her all along.

Chapter 15: The Grandmother

The box her mother had left her sat on the shelf in Juno's room, cedar and brass, and for nine years she had known exactly what was in it without once opening it. Nell had kept it for her. Nell had died before Juno was old enough to open it alone.

Now she carried it down to the observatory kitchen, where Leo was sorting coordinates at the table, and set it between them.

The first layer was sketches. Iona's hand was quick and sure — crows in flight, a kestrel's wing, the ridge in snow, and, everywhere, the sky. Stars scattered across pages the way her mother had scattered them across Juno's childhood. Juno turned them slowly, and the woman in the papers came into the kitchen with them: the same impatience with a crooked line, the same way of filling a margin with a star.

Beneath the sketches, letters. Tied with string, in Nell's writing on the outside: from Iona. The handwriting inside was Iona's — steep, affectionate, full of asides.

Dearest Nell — the sky cleared tonight and I am keeping a comet for you like a promise. The crow comes home, you'll see. Do you know why I called it that? Because it leaves and it always, always comes back.

Juno read a second, and a third. Letters to a child about school and weather and the names of stars. Letters that signed off with the same phrase, every time, in the same warm hand: the fixed and the faithful.

"The fixed and the faithful," Leo said softly, reading over her shoulder. "She meant the stars."

"I know." Juno's voice was strange in her own throat. "My mother used to say it. When she taught me the sky. 'The stars are the fixed and the faithful, Juno — they stay.'" She had thought it was Nell's phrase. It was Nell's mother's. It had run through three women like a constellation through three winters.

At the bottom of the box, wrapped in felt, a small brass locket. Inside, pressed flat, a single grey feather — a crow's feather, or a wren's; small enough to be either — and a photograph of a woman on a stone platform, young, laughing, a telescope at her shoulder and the ridge white behind her.

Juno held the locket and the feather and did not cry, because crying was not how her family grieved. Grief in her family was this: a box, a letter, a name spoken accurately. She closed her hand around the feather and felt her mother in it, and her mother's mother behind her mother, and the whole line of women looking up at the same unchanging sky.

Chapter 16: The Disappearance

The town papers were in the library too, bound in green cloth, and Juno read March of 1976 the way she had read the star catalog — page by page, refusing to skim.

The comet had been due on the night of the thirteenth. Iona Wren had gone up to the ridge alone, the way she always went up on observing nights, with the astrograph in a wheeled crate and a thermos of tea. The Morrow Clarion had run a small story: "Observatory astronomer to photograph comet return." The word last was not in it.

The storm came in after midnight. That was in the papers too — the front page the next morning, barn roof gone, trees down, the road to the ridge washed out. A second story, smaller, three days later: "Search of Observatory Hill yields nothing." Juno turned the page and there it was, the whole weight of fifty years in four lines of type. Dr. Wren's station on the East Ridge had been found empty. The instrument was gone from its cradle. There had been a notebook on the table, open to a final page.

Juno found the transcription in the observatory's own file, in the careful hand of the caretaker who had closed the place:

"If I am late, the map will say where."

She read it until the letters stopped moving.

The storm had taken the road, the search had taken a week, and the woman had never come down. The observatory had closed that autumn — no staff astronomer to keep it, no money to replace one. The 1976 chart, corrected plates and all, had gone to press that winter with Kraz half a moon's width from its true place, and the wrongness had sat in the library ever since, waiting.

"Her astrograph," Leo said. He had come up behind her and was reading over her shoulder, careful and quiet. "The instrument was gone from its cradle. Gone, not destroyed."

Juno looked at the line again. Gone. The storm had not taken it. Iona had built a clockwork star on the ridge and hidden the instrument — the same instrument, the message said, left for the one who redraws the sky.

"She went up for perihelion night knowing the storm was coming," Juno said slowly, "and she took the astrograph, and she left the map to say where." She looked up at Leo, and the freezing library held its breath around them. "She wasn't lost. She was placed. Everything she did that night was deliberate. The wrong chart. The light. The map. She was leaving a message for fifty years in the future, and she wanted us to find the instrument before anyone else could file it away."

Outside, beyond the black window, the flashing light on the ridge went on spelling into the dark: come to the place where the chart is wrong.

Chapter 17: The Coordinates

The terrestrial coordinates resolved the way coordinates always resolve when you stop being afraid of them: slowly, and then all at once.

Leo plotted them on the county map first, on the observatory's kitchen table, and the pencil mark fell in the observatory's own green parcel of land, the East Ridge, in the fold of hill behind the Field. Juno took the same numbers to the deed in her father's desk — land held by the Mercer family since the observatory was built, transferred to caretakers in 1976 to keep it out of the county's hands — and the point landed again, dead center on the Old Pit.

The Old Pit. The abandoned quarry the townspeople had walked past for fifty years, the dark notch in the ridge where nothing grew and the snow lay deepest. She had sledded the lip of it as a child, terrified and thrilled. She had never once thought to look inside.

"The crow comes home," Leo said. He had followed her down to the office and stood reading the deed over her shoulder, which had become his habit and, she realized, one she no longer minded. "The message doesn't mean the star. It means the place. She aimed the whole constellation at it — the flare, the flashing, the false position, all of it points at the Old Pit."

Juno thought of Iona's sketchbook, the ridge in snow, the same fold of hill drawn and redrawn until it was exact. She thought of the locket's feather and the empty cradle in the Crow's Nest and the instrument that was "gone," not destroyed.

"It's a quarry," she said. "It's a hole in the ground. Why would she leave the instrument in a hole in the ground?"

"Maybe it isn't a hole," Leo said. "Maybe it's a door. She built her field station with her own hands. What did she need a quarry for?" He looked up at her, bright. "Weather. Roof. Stone walls already there. A place that would stay dry and cold and closed for fifty years. Juno — she had the whole East Ridge. She could have hidden it anywhere. She hid it somewhere nobody would ever go looking, unless the message told them to."

Outside the window the weather was closing in again, grey and low. Juno looked from the deed to the map to the pencil mark at the heart of the Old Pit.

"Next clear weekend," she said. "We hike to the quarry. We find what she left us."

Leo nodded, and picked up his coat, and at the door he turned.

"Bring the map," he said. "She said to come to the place where the chart is wrong. That's what a map is for — knowing where you're going, and trusting the person who drew it."

Chapter 18: The Comet

The celestial coordinates took longer, because they were not a place but a prediction, and predictions have to be earned.

Leo worked at the kitchen table for three evenings, with the observatory's bound ephemeris and Iona's own 1976 paper and a calculator that whined on every division. Juno watched him the way she watched the sky — without interrupting, trusting the numbers to speak. He computed an orbit, checked it against the catalog, computed it again. On the third night he pushed back his chair and looked at her with the particular stillness of a person who has found what they were afraid to look for.

"It's real," he said. "C/1976 J1. She discovered it. She computed the whole orbit by hand." He turned the paper to face her. "Period, fifty point three years. Perihelion, this winter. It's below the horizon now — a faint thing, barely a smudge at dusk — but it's coming in. It's already coming in."

He showed her the line he had drawn: the comet's path arcing down through the winter sky, crossing the bowl of Corvus at perihelion, exactly where the message's celestial coordinates pointed, exactly where the flash still burned in the false place on the chart.

"The crow comes home," Juno said. "That's what she meant. Not just the constellation. The comet. It comes back every fifty years, and it comes back through Corvus, and she knew. She predicted it to the fraction."

"The institute says it won't be anything," Leo said carefully. "I checked. Hawke-Merrin's model has it as a dust-poor dud — no tail, no show, a boring faint fuzzy. That's why nobody's bothered to recover it. It isn't worth their time."

Juno stared at the paper. A comet that official science had written off, predicted by a woman who had been gone for fifty years, due to return this winter through the constellation her granddaughter had spent nine years redrawing. The wrong chart. The flashing light. The coordinates in the message. It all came together in one enormous, quiet click, the way a lock turns when you finally stop forcing it.

"She was right," she said. "She was right about everything. The comet is coming back, and the message says the instrument is waiting where the chart is wrong, and the chart is wrong at the exact place where the comet crosses the ridge." She looked up at Leo. "She built the whole thing — the wrongness, the light, the map — so that when the comet came back, someone would be there looking. Someone who redrew the sky."

"The crow comes home," Leo agreed. "And someone has to be watching when it does."

Chapter 19: The Return

They found the comet at dawn, on the hill above the observatory, with her father's binoculars and a sky the color of cold steel.

Leo had computed the place to the minute, and Juno had set her alarm for the last hour of dark, and it was her father, not Leo, who came out onto the hill with her — because Leo's bus did not run before first light, and because Sam Mercer had heard the word comet at the breakfast table and had gone very still, the way he did when a thing from the sky was mentioned.

The sky was clearing. The east ridge stood black against the grey, and out beyond it, low, the faint smudge hung where the calculation said it would hang — a pale thumbprint of light, not a star, not a planet, patient and slightly wrong, like a word written in a language you almost remember.

"Binoculars," Juno said, and her father handed them over without a word, and she raised them and found the smudge and held it, and the breath went out of her.

"It's there," she said. "Leo's numbers. The coordinates. It's exactly there." She lowered the glasses, looked at the naked-eye smudge, raised them again. "She was right. She was right about everything."

Sam Mercer stood beside her in the cold with his hands in his pockets, watching her joy the way a man watches a fire he is afraid to touch. He did not ask what she meant. He did not say the comet was named for a woman he had met once, as a boy, who had told him to always look up. He said nothing at all about the deal that would close in the spring — the institute, the dark-sky rights, the archives, the whole observatory signed away before the snow was gone.

"The crow comes home," Juno said, half to herself, and she was laughing, a short sound that surprised her, and her father did not look at her. He looked at the smudge of light over the ridge, and something moved in his face, quickly, like a fish under ice, and was gone.

Down in the valley the institute's survey lights were on, a row of hard white points in the freight hall's yard, and their crews were already out in the dark with their instruments, sweeping the sky as if they owned it.

Juno turned her back on the lights and looked at the comet, the small faithful thing her grandmother had put a name to, returning on schedule after fifty years of not being believed in.

"She was right," she said again, quieter, to the comet, to the ridge, to the woman in the locket. "You were right. And I'm here. I redrew it."

Her father said nothing. But he did not go back into the observatory, and he was still standing beside her, looking up, when the sun rose.

Chapter 20: The Winter

The comet spent the winter learning to be believed in.

It brightened by slow, patient degrees — a smudge, then a soft knot of light, then, by deep January, a star with a hazy fault line behind it that Leo insisted on calling a tail and Juno insisted on calling "a smear, but a beautiful one." Every clear night they went up to the Old Glass, and the old telescope, which had spent decades showing school groups the same patient moon, seemed to wake into a purpose it had forgotten it had.

Juno's first astrophotographs were terrible. Her hands shook with cold; the guiding was rough; the images came out as pale blurs with a smear behind them. But the third week's plate was different — a clean pinprick of light with the tail resolving, faint but certain, out to the east — and Leo framed it in the darkroom's red light and said, in a voice gone unsteady, "You're photographing what she predicted."

The town paper picked it up. The caretaker's daughter and the comet, the Morrow Clarion called it, and there was a photograph of Juno at the Old Glass with frost on her hair, and Sam Mercer clipped it and said nothing, and Juno found it folded in the pocket of his coat weeks later.

The institute noticed too. Their survey team on the ridge grew louder; their truck went up and down the valley road. Dr. Kellar's people had published their model — dust-poor, a dud, nothing to see — and the comet, obliging nobody, kept brightening past the model's every curve. Leo tracked it weekly against their numbers, and their lines fell further and further behind the smudge in the sky.

"Every week," he said, one frozen night in the dome, "they re-run the model. And every week the sky says no."

"It's the same thing that happened to her," Juno said. She was at the eyepiece, guiding, breath smoking in the cold. "She predicted it and nobody believed her. Now it's coming back exactly where she said, and the institute is still telling everyone it can't."

Leo was quiet. The dome creaked. The comet held its small bright ground above the ridge.

"Juno," he said at last. "When she said the crow comes home — maybe it wasn't just the comet. Maybe it was the argument. Fifty years later, and she's winning it."

Juno did not answer. But the cold had never once stopped her from looking, and it did not stop her now. She guided on the smudge, and the shutter stayed open, and the winter went on collecting the light of a woman who had refused to be wrong.

Chapter 21: The Telescope

The institute's truck arrived at noon, low and grey, and Juno watched from the library window as it idled in the visitor lot like something that had forgotten how to leave. She had seen it before, a hundred yards off, on the road to town. She had never seen it stop.

Her father met Dr. Ash Kellar in the cold yard, handshake short. Juno came down the steps with her hands in her pockets and watched the man who had bought the sky.

Kellar was younger than she had imagined, maybe thirty, with the tiredness of a man who measured things for a living. He introduced himself as the survey astronomer for Hawke-Merrin, and he said the word survey the way other people said settle.

"Your father tells me you've been watching the comet," he said.

Juno said nothing. She had been watching the sky for nine years. The comet was the least of it.

Dad said, "Juno—" and then stopped, and she understood, all at once, why his coffee had gone cold for a week. "The institute bought the dark-sky rights," he said. "And the archives. It closes in spring."

The sky, sold. She felt the floor of the observatory move under her.

Kellar walked the building like a man pricing timber. In the library he pulled one of Iona Wren's plates from its sleeve and held it to the light, and his face did something small and dismissive that Juno would remember for a long time. "Scrap for digitizing," he said. "We'll scan what's worth scanning."

"That's my grandmother's work," Juno said.

"Then it's family history," Kellar said, "and it's going to be preserved. That's more than most of it got." He set the plate down gently, and for a moment she almost liked him. "The comet's recovery belongs to the archive, Juno. You understand that."

She understood. She understood that her whole winter — the flares, the decoding, the first smudge at dawn — was about to be called paperwork.

Kellar's crew filed past the visitor center and took the trail up the East Ridge, carrying crates. Dad put his hand on her shoulder and she let it stay there because she was too angry to shrug it off.

"Leave the sky alone," Dad said quietly.

She did not answer. She watched the headlamps climb the ridge into the failing light, and she thought: not the sky. The sky is mine.

But she walked the observatory that evening the way you walk a place that is about to be taken — the library where the plates slept in their sleeves, the frozen corridor to the west door, the dome above her where the Old Glass stood cold and patient. She touched the brass of the stair rail and left her handprint in the frost, and she made herself a promise she did not yet have words for. Whatever happened in the spring, the winter belonged to her, and the sky belonged to her, and she would use them the way her grandmother would have used them: without asking.

Chapter 22: The Stolen Night

Leo came at midnight with a thermos of something hot and a bag of the observatory's own plate holders, and Juno let him in through the service door she had oiled the day before.

"Say it," she said. "You'll say it anyway."

"I'm saying nothing." He set the thermos on the dome floor. "But if the survey cameras pick us up—"

"They're aimed at the sky, Leo. Not at us."

They were both wrong, and they both knew it, but the night the comet crossed the ridge-line geometry of the message did not wait for permission.

The Old Glass had not been aimed by Juno in a month. Kellar's survey had claimed its evenings, and the dome was cold in the way of rooms that have been left alone. The brass was colder than the air, and the air was cold enough to hurt. Her breath made its own small weather.

"Can you guide?" she asked.

"I can try."

She gave him the hand chart — her corrected one, Kraz at its true position, the ridge line drawn in red — and together they cranked the dome slit open to the southeast. The comet was there, faint, a thumb-smudge of patience, climbing the exact geometry her grandmother had laid down. The crow coming home.

The night was long and stiff-fingered and brilliant. Leo guided on the star and she worked the shutter, and between exposures they stood close for warmth and said the names of the sky to keep their minds off the cold and the risk. Vega. Altair. Kraz, the faithful. The dome smelled of frost and oil and old brass.

Twice the survey lights swept the valley below — white, methodical, patient — and both times they froze, breath held, until the beam passed and the dark came back. Juno's heart hammered so loud she was sure Leo could hear it. He did not say anything. He only reset the clock and guided again.

By three in the morning her fingers were so numb she could barely rack the plates, and her hands shook from more than the cold. Leo took the last exposure himself, and when it was done he held the holder up to the starlight and looked at her.

"These are the best images of the winter," he said. "The best this telescope has made in years."

They were the best of the season. They were the best because they were stolen, and Juno thought, with a clarity that surprised her, that Iona Wren would have understood exactly that. The best things done at Morrow Observatory had a way of being done without permission.

They locked the dome behind them and walked down the hill in the dark, and neither of them saw the truck idling at the foot of the drive with its engine running.

Chapter 23: The Rival

Kellar was waiting in the library when they came down for breakfast, holding the night's plates in a gloved hand like evidence at a trial. Juno's stomach turned over before her mind caught up.

"We were just—" Leo began.

"Observing," Kellar said. "I know. I saw. The dark-sky rights cover the observatory dome, Leo. That includes the hours after midnight, and that includes—" he turned a plate over in the light, "—these."

He said the word impounded carefully, as if it were a kind of mercy. The plates went into a lockbox the size of a breadbox, and Juno stood very still and did not look at Leo, because if she looked at him she would break.

"It's observatory property," Kellar said, more gently than she expected. "It always was. I'm not taking anything that isn't already ours."

Then the crew's truck came up the drive, slow under its weight, and everything else went small and far away.

The crate was grey, padded, stenciled with the institute's name, and the two men who carried it to the loading ramp moved like men carrying something heavier than wood. Juno knew, before she could see, by the way her chest went hollow. The astrograph. Her grandmother's astrograph — the brass telescope with I.W. engraved in the cradle, the one she had never once touched, the one that had stood on the ridge for fifty years.

"Your crew found it in a stone hut," she said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "It's not yours."

"Everything on the East Ridge is institute property," Kellar said. "It's in the deed. It was in the deed when your father signed."

Dad was in the doorway. Juno saw him go grey and then go still, and she understood that the deal had promised more than the sky.

They watched the astrograph go into the truck, feetfirst, cradle-first, the way you load a body. Leo's hand found her sleeve and she let it stay.

"Excuse me," Juno said, and she walked to the truck and looked at the crate with her hands on the tailgate, memorizing the stenciled letters, and then she looked at Kellar.

"You said it's ours," she said. "Fine. It's on loan."

Kellar laughed a short, tired laugh. "It's not a library, Juno."

"Then neither are we." She walked back to the observatory and did not watch the truck leave. The pressure, which had been a cold fact of the winter, had become a thing with a driver, and a destination, and a key.

Chapter 24: The Chase

They didn't decide so much as refuse, together, over the observatory's kitchen table, with the plates gone and the sky shut out.

"We can't get it back," Leo said. "It's a crime, Juno. It's a theft."

"Borrowed," she said.

"Borrowed implies return."

"Exactly." She leaned across the table. "One night. Perihelion night. We take it, we use it, we bring it back before dawn. Nobody opens a freight hall at four in the morning to check the crates."

Leo was quiet for a long time, and Juno watched him fight it — the tidy part of him that liked machines that could be fixed, against the part that had looked through a telescope at a clockwork star. She could not make the argument for him. She had learned that in a winter of being the only one who noticed.

"Perihelion night," he said at last. "The night it passes through Corvus."

"The night the crow comes home." She said it the way Iona's letters said it, a fact and a promise in one.

The storeroom was the old freight hall on the rail spur. Leo's father was the station engineer, and Leo had grown up with keys to every lock on the property — a fact he had never once mentioned to anyone, and which he now laid on the table like a burden.

That left the observatory's side of things. That left Old Tom Griggs.

They found him at the school, polishing the hallway floors at dusk, his mop moving in slow orbits like something celestial. He listened without turning, and when Juno had finished he leaned the mop against the wall and took a key ring from his pocket the size of a small family.

"Used to be the caretaker's boy," he said. "Knew your grandmother. Knew her coffee, too." He pressed the keys into her hand, and then the thermos, and then — the most important thing, she would understand later — his silence.

"Perihelion night's clear," he said. "Minus twenty, they say. You mind the brass. She always did."

Juno took the keys and the thermos and the silence, and walked home through the cold with her heart full of something that was not fear. The comet was bright enough now to see from the street, a stubborn small light in the south, and she looked at it and thought about her grandmother building a lantern to keep a promise longer than she could, and about the instrument in a crate in a freight hall, waiting to be borrowed.

The forecast was clear, minus twenty, and the nights of the countdown were very long and very still.

Chapter 25: The Field

They went up the East Ridge on a Saturday, in the false warmth of a winter noon, and the ridge gave back its long history in stages.

First the Crow's Nest, the stone field station, its door hanging open. The astrograph was gone — Kellar's crew had taken it, and the cradle beneath the brass plate that read I.W., 1976 stood empty, a hollow in the dust shaped exactly like a telescope, like the print of a hand that had let go. But the logbooks were still on their shelf, the spines of the 1976 observing run, untouched, as if fifty years were an afternoon.

Juno knelt and ran her fingers around the hollow, and did not say anything. Leo stood in the doorway and did not say anything either, and the two silences were the same silence.

Beyond the station, the Field: the flat stone observing platform Iona had built into the hillside, squared to true north, the seams of her hand-set stone still sharp after fifty winters. Juno walked it end to end, counting her steps, feeling the place where her grandmother's instruments had stood.

The fire-lookout mast stood at the ridge's shoulder, and it was the mast that stopped her.

Something was bolted to the top of it, fifty feet up — a thing of tarnished metal and glass, a lamp of no kind she had seen before, aimed southeast, along the line of the sky where Corvus rose. A parabolic mirror caught the low sun and threw a coin of light onto the snow.

"Your grandmother built a clockwork star," Leo said quietly.

Juno understood, in that moment, the whole architecture of the wrongness: the chart misplotted by half a moon's width, the light that had answered exactly there, the message the light had carried. The Lantern was the answer and the question at once — Iona Wren, aiming a star at the place where a star was not, so that someone would have to look closer.

"Can we get up to it?" she asked.

Leo looked at the mast, at the corroded rungs, at the reach of the mirror above the last of them. "Not tonight," he said. "It'll keep its promise a while longer, or not. That's the gamble she built into it."

The wind moved through the dead grass on the ridge, and Juno thought she could feel the whole winter tilting toward one night.

The forecast held: perihelion night, clear, minus twenty. Old Tom's thermos stood in the observatory kitchen, polished, waiting. And somewhere in the freight hall at the end of the rail spur, in a grey stenciled crate, a brass telescope marked I.W., 1976, was also waiting.

The ridge, which had kept its secret for fifty years, was nearly ready to give it up.

Chapter 26: The Observation

The freight hall was unlocked by Leo's own key at eleven, and the crate was exactly where the crew had left it, grey and stenciled and heavier than Juno had imagined. They opened it in the dark by feel — Leo had forbidden lights — and lifted the astrograph out into the cold air, and for a moment she simply held it, the weight of fifty years of someone else's hands.

"Quick," Leo said, and they moved.

The ridge road was a sheet of ice and the moon was down, so they walked in the dark by the sound of the wind and the memory of the day's recon. Juno carried the tube, Leo the cradle, the tripod's legs between them, and neither of them spoke because there was nothing safe to say.

The Field met them at midnight. They set the astrograph up on the flat stone, on the exact spot where her hand-set seams made a hollow like a socket, and the fit was so perfect that Juno laughed out loud, a small bright sound in the terrible cold. Minus twenty and dropping. Her lashes froze together and she blinked them apart.

She had brought her grandmother's hand-written charts — Iona's own guide stars, the penmanship of someone who loved the work — and by starlight she rough-aligned the tube to the southeast, to where the crow would come.

The comet rose at one-twelve.

Juno saw it first in the finder, a faint gray smudge climbing over the ridge line into the geometry of the message, and her breath stopped in her throat. Leo racked a plate into the cradle, and she took the guiding eyepiece, and the first light through Iona Wren's instrument in fifty years fell on a photographic plate in a brass holder, in the dark, on a stone platform above a town at the end of the line.

She guided on Kraz, faithful Kraz, and her hands were steady because they had to be.

Below, a patrol car's lights swept the road, white and patient, one slow pass. Juno counted her breaths and did not move. The beam moved on. The dark came back, and the comet, and the work.

At three in the morning the plate holders were full and the comet hung at its best, and Juno stood back from the instrument, her hand on a tube that was colder than the air, and she did the thing she had been saving for this night.

"Hello, Grandma," she whispered. "I redrew it."

The ridge was silent. The wind moved. Somewhere behind them, high on the mast, something that had been keeping a promise for fifty years was listening for the same light, and was very close to the end of its charge.

Chapter 27: The Comet

The watch lasted four hours, and the comet was worth all of them.

Through the astrograph it was no longer a smudge. It had a head, bright and near, and a tail that streamed back from it like a banner held at a run, and there was color in it — a pale, cold blue-white in the head, and the faintest gold in the tail's sweep, the way frost catches a low sun. Juno had never seen color in the sky with her own eyes, not like this, and she understood that fifty years ago her grandmother had stood on this same stone and watched the same return, and had known, the way Juno knew now, that no photograph would ever be enough.

The geometry was all one thing tonight. She could feel it in her bones: the comet crossing Corvus at perihelion, the crow come home; the ridge running beneath the constellation like a dark twin of its flight; and the mast above them, the Lantern aimed at the empty place on a chart half a century old. Every part of the message was in its place. The sky had arranged itself for this one night, and she was the one watching.

Leo took over the guiding at two, and Juno wrapped her hands around the thermos Old Tom had given her — tea, strong and sweet, the ridge's tea, her grandmother's recipe, Tom had said — and drank it out of the cap and tasted something like the last warm thing in the world.

"The tail's growing," Leo said. "In the last hour it's grown. That's not dust, Juno. Kellar's model says dust-poor. That's not dust-poor."

She looked where he was looking and did not say what she was thinking. She had said it once, at dawn, with her father's binoculars, and it was still true.

The Lantern fired at three-forty, in the dead of the night, with the comet at the ridge's shoulder.

A light bloomed at the top of the mast — a single long flash, steady and slow, brighter than any star, and then darkness. Juno's heart seized. She had been watching for it for weeks, and still it came as a shock, as if the sky itself had spoken.

"That's it," Leo whispered. "That's the last of its charge."

Juno turned the astrograph — slowly, uselessly, she knew — toward the mast, and the long second filled itself with meaning. The Lantern had fired at the comet's brightest, the way she had designed it to, and it had aimed its light at the false position of Kraz, and for one long second the empty place on the chart had been full of light.

A star answering a star. A grandmother answering.

The dark came back, and the watch continued, and in the margin of Iona's chart Juno wrote, in the space where the women of her family had always written: it was here. It answered.

Chapter 28: The Instrument

In the dead hours, between the Lantern's single long flash and the first gray hint of dawn, Juno found the instrument's secrets.

She was wiping frost off the tube — you minded the brass, Tom had said — when her hand caught on something under the dew cap: a seam in the drawtube that the cold had made visible, a hairline running where the metal had been worked and reworked. She ran her thumbnail along it and a rolled slip of paper came free, yellow as an old tooth, held for fifty years by nothing but the tightness of the tube.

She unrolled it by flashlight. The hand was her grandmother's — she knew it now, from the letters, from the margins of the charts, the precise impatient forward-lean of it.

For the one who finds this — aim true.

That was all. Juno read it four times, then folded it very small and put it in the pocket over her heart, and she looked at the cradle with new eyes.

The cradle was brass, engraved I.W., 1976, and it had seemed to her a solid thing, a weight. But Leo's father had taught Leo about machines that could be fixed, and Leo had taught her that the fixed and the faithful are not the same as the sealed. She ran her fingers along the underside of the cradle's bearing, and one of the rivets was not a rivet.

It unscrewed, warm in her cold hand. Inside the hollow boss was a compartment the size of a shoebox, and inside the compartment, wrapped in oilcloth that had kept its seal for half a century, a letter in an envelope and a map.

The map was the observing campaign, drawn by hand — the ridge, the Field, the mast, the comet's track across Corvus, dates and exposures in Iona's small precise hand, the whole long campaign of a woman who had known she was finishing a thing. Juno's breath went ragged. She laid it flat on the stone and knelt over it, and Leo crouched beside her, and together they read the first lines of the letter by the weak beam of the flashlight, huddled against the wind.

The letter began, in her grandmother's hand, addressed not to an institution and not to an archive.

To Nell, when she is grown, and to the one who redraws the sky —

Juno's mother's name, and hers. The two halves of the sentence held her the way the cradle had held the telescope.

"I can't read the rest," she said. "Not out here. Not yet." But she knew what it was. She could feel its weight and its shape, the way you can feel the shape of a thing before you open it.

"She left us a will," Juno said, and her voice came out strange and bright. "The whole ridge was her will. This is just the part that's signed."

Chapter 29: The Landing

The plates came down off the ridge in the first gray of morning, wrapped in her coat, and the observatory's darkroom was cold and red and smelled of chemicals and old time.

Juno had developed her own films for years, but these were plates, and Iona's instrument had been guiding on them all night, and her hands shook with cold and want as she worked the first one into the tray.

The red light held the room in a kind of holy hush. Leo stood behind her, not breathing.

The image came up slowly, the way a sky comes up at dawn.

First the field of stars — Kraz at its true position, bright and faithful, the corrected sky, the sky she had drawn and redrawn and finally, now, photographed with the instrument her grandmother built. Then the comet, the whole shape of it flooding in gray, tail and head and the faint gold she had seen with her own eyes made silver and permanent. Juno pressed her hands flat on the bench, because they were shaking too hard to hold anything.

The second plate was sharper. The third was the best of the night — the geometry of the message, comet and Crow and ridge arranged exactly as Iona had designed it, all in one frame, proof that could not be argued with, a photograph of the truth.

It was the fourth plate that changed everything.

It was the deepest exposure, the one Leo had guided through the worst of the cold, and Juno saw it first: beyond the comet, low along the ridge line, a faint streak that was not a star and not a defect. A thin thread of light on the dark glass, beginning at the mast and running along the line of the crow's flight.

She held the plate up to the red light, and the streak resolved.

The Lantern's flash. The single long second when her grandmother's last charge had filled the false position of Kraz with light — caught on glass, fixed forever, fifty years of a promise made visible.

"Look," Juno breathed. "Leo. Look."

He looked, and then he looked at her, and neither of them said what the streak meant, because they both knew. The message and the instrument were one thing now. The wrong chart, the flares, the code, the instrument, the light — it had all been one sentence, written across fifty years, and tonight, on a photographic plate, they had finally read it whole.

The red light made the darkroom a place outside time. Juno hung the four plates on the line to dry, and they were the most beautiful things she had ever made.

Chapter 30: The Recovery

They cleaned the astrograph on the ridge before they broke it down, every surface wiped with the soft cloth Old Tom had packed, the brass polished until it could hold the fading stars. It had come to them spotless from its crate and it would go back spotless, and Juno worked the cloth slowly, because each pass was a goodbye she had not asked for.

At the freight hall the lock turned easy under Leo's key. They eased the astrograph back into its grey stenciled crate, cradle first, the way it had been loaded, and Juno laid her note on top of the velvet — a sheet from the observatory's logbook, folded once, in her clearest hand.

borrowed for the night. returned. — J.M.

Leo closed the crate. He did it without ceremony, the way you close a door you expect to open again, and they stood in the cold storeroom for a moment, not looking at each other.

"Same time tomorrow, if we're arrested," Leo said, and his voice shook a little, and she laughed a small bright laugh with tears in it.

"I'm going to read it," she said. "The letter. Today."

He nodded. The map and the letter were in her coat, against her chest, and the four plates were in the observatory's darkroom, hanging on the line like a new constellation.

She walked home through the grey morning, and her father was in the kitchen when she came in, his coffee going cold the way it had all winter. He did not ask where she had been. He had stopped asking, long ago, and she had loved him for trusting her and hated him for it, and this morning she understood both at once.

"Dad," she said. "I have to show you something."

He looked up. The whole winter was in that look — the sealed grief, the deal he had made to keep the roof on, the way he had stopped looking at the sky. Juno set her grandmother's letter on the table between them, and the map beside it, and she thought about how a will was not a sad thing, not really. A will was a way of speaking after you were gone.

"Iona left this for the one who redraws the sky," she said. "That's me, Dad. It's been me all along."

Outside, the morning was grey and cold, and beyond the window, where her father had not looked in years, the comet was bright enough now to outshine the lightening east, climbing the sky over the ridge, the crow come home.

The beginning of the truth was a letter on a kitchen table, a brass telescope back in its crate, and a father and a daughter looking at each other across fifty years of not saying the right thing.

Chapter 31: The Truth

She read the letter a third time in the red light of the darkroom, a fourth time in the kitchen, and the shape of it did not change. It was not an accident that it was in the instrument. It was not an accident that any of it was. Iona Wren had been ill in the winter of 1976 — not the dramatic kind of illness, but the quiet kind, the kind that fills you slowly from the inside, like frost climbing a windowpane in a house no one is heating. She wrote it plainly, with her astronomer's exactness and none of the softness that had gone into the tea recipe. She knew what the doctors knew, and she chose.

The ridge over the hospital. The comet watch over the treatment that would have bought her months and saved her nothing that mattered. Not a tragedy. A choice. She would finish what she had begun, and she would leave a map instead of a goodbye, and she said so without a single word of self-pity: "I am not lost. I am placed."

"To the one who redraws the sky," the letter began. "If you are reading this, the chart did its work. I misplotted Kraz by 0.42 degrees — half a moon's width — on purpose. Not to deceive. To be seen. Wrongness is attention. Attention is love. I am sorry it took so long."

Juno's hands shook around the paper. All her life she had been the only one who noticed — the lonely arithmetic of nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, watching a star sit where the chart said a star sat, and the sky said nothing did. And all along, someone had wanted her to notice. Someone had built the wrongness like a lit window in a dark house, and waited fifty years for a knock.

The lantern's promise, the prediction, the coordinates — all of it was in the letter, all of a piece. She had built something that would keep its promise longer than she could. It had.

Juno carried the letter into the grey kitchen and found her father in his coat, coffee in his hand, not asking. "Dad," she said. "I know what happened to her."

The coffee stopped halfway to his mouth. He set it down carefully, the way you set down something that might break.

"She wasn't lost," Juno said. "She chose. She was ill, and she chose the ridge, and the comet, and this." She held up the letter. "She left us a map."

Sam Mercer stood very still. The winter light crossed his face. After a long time he said, "Read it to me."

Juno read it to him, from the beginning, and when she came to the lit window, his breath caught, and then the light was fully up, and neither of them looked away from the paper for a long, long time.

Chapter 32: The Grandmother's Letter

The letter had a second page, and a third. That first night Juno had read only the beginning — the part that explained the choice. But Iona Wren had not written a legal document. She had written a letter, the way she had written to Nell when Nell was a child, and the way she wrote now to whoever found the instrument. Instructions. Jokes. The recipe for the ridge's tea. Juno read the whole thing aloud in the observatory kitchen, because her father had asked, and because some things need to be said twice — once to be understood, once to be heard.

"To Nell, my small astronomer," Iona had written, in a section dated years earlier, in the same warm hand that had taught Juno's mother the names of the sky. "When you are old enough to read this, the crow will have come home. Do not be sad for me. I have a view of the sky no one else gets, and I intend to be late for everything I am supposed to be late for."

"To the one who redraws the sky," the later hand went on. "Instructions. The astrograph's cradle holds the plates I did not ship. Do not trust the institute to count them for you. The tea on the ridge is ridge-grass steeped in snowmelt — it is terrible, and it is the taste of home. Aim true. Bring a scarf. The sky is cold, and the sky is worth it."

Juno laughed once, wetly, at the tea. Her father did not laugh. He had not made a sound in a long time.

The last page was addressed to no one and to everyone. "I knew a boy once, at this observatory," it said. "The caretaker's boy. He had the best eyes for the sky of any child I ever saw, and I told him the only thing I know for certain: always look up. If you are reading this, tell him I remembered him. Tell him the crow came home."

The caretaker's boy.

Sam Mercer's hands came up over his face. Juno had never seen her father cry — not at the funeral, not in nine years of winters — and she did not watch now. She set the letter on the table and sat with him while the kitchen clock ticked and the snow began again, small and quiet against the glass.

"He was eleven," her father said at last, his voice coming from far away. "I met her once. She took me up the hill and showed me the Crow through the little telescope, and she told me to always look up." He lowered his hands. His face was wrecked and open, and he did not try to close it. "I stopped. After Nell. I just — stopped."

Juno thought of the nine years of drawings, the fixed and the faithful, the one thing the family had never once stopped doing, even when they believed they had. She reached across the table and took his hand.

"The crow came home," she said. "Look up."

Chapter 33: The Legacy

They inventoried it together, Juno and Leo, on a bright frozen afternoon, the logbooks spread across the observatory library table like a deck of cards being dealt one last time.

The prediction. The recovery, observed and timed, the orbit recalculated from Iona's own parameters and checked against fifty years of plates. It held. It had always held.

The data set. In the Crow's Nest logbooks, Iona had recorded every observation of the last return — positions, magnitudes, the slow flowering of the tail across March — in the same precise hand that wrote the letter. The set was complete. It had waited in the cold for half a century for someone to read it, and it was perfect.

The method. Her hand-drawn charts, the guided-plate technique, the proper-motion corrections from her own 1976 paper — the very corrections she had used to disguise Kraz. The method was the map's true content. The instrument was only the shape it took.

The instrument. The astrograph, back in its cradle on the ridge, its brass gleaming — I.W., 1976. It had done its work. It was not scrap for digitizing. It was the physical shape of a person's love, and it had come home.

The map. The wrong chart and the true one, the nine years of Juno's drawings, the new plates. The whole sky drawn twice, once wrong and once right, the way you draw a thing you love twice, to make sure.

"Here," Leo said, laying out the final page of Iona's last logbook. "This is a publication. She didn't just observe — she was preparing to write this up. The recovery, the model, the prediction. All of it, ready."

The observatory's fate and the comet's were one question now. The institute's deal closed in the spring; the archives, the dark-sky rights, the building itself — all of it hanging on a signature. But you cannot digitize a living thing, and you cannot file away a star that is coming back.

Juno looked at the assembled record and felt its weight — the weight of a person's whole working life, kept warm and dry and true for fifty years.

"We write it up," she said. "We finish what she started. The record, the recovery, the method. The Morrow Observation Set. And we publish before the spring."

Leo nodded. "Then the question of who owns the sky answers itself."

They worked until the library lamps came on and the dome light died beyond the windows, and outside, the comet climbed the southeast, small and faithful, keeping its appointment.

Chapter 34: The Publication

They wrote the paper the way they had learned the sky: Juno drew the observations, Leo did the numbers, and together they argued about every decimal until the argument became a kind of agreement. The kitchen table, the red light of the darkroom, the frost on the dome shutter — the winter became the paper.

Title: "Recovery of C/1976 J1 (Wren) and the Morrow Observation Set." Authors: Juno Mercer, Leo Fenn. First authors both, on a paper about a comet her grandmother had discovered and predicted fifty years earlier. The name Wren sat in the title like a star in a constellation, and the whole sky around it was theirs.

The institute tried to block it.

It was not a dramatic blockade. It was a letter from their legal office, cc'd to the observatory's board — which was one man, her father — asserting that the recovery data belonged to the Hawke-Merrin archive under the terms of the dark-sky agreement, and that publication by unauthorized third parties would be contested. Their model had called the comet a dust-poor dud. The comet had, in the weeks since perihelion, out-brightened their prediction by a full magnitude and grown a tail the whole town could see from Main Street.

The data was too good.

This was the part Leo loved, and he said so with a physicist's plain joy: the paper did not argue. It presented. Positions to the arcsecond, the proper-motion corrections from Iona's own 1976 paper, the guided plates, the recovered logbooks, the independent verification. Every number the institute's model had gotten wrong was followed by the number the sky had actually produced. The sky does not have lawyers.

The referees were slow, then careful, then convinced. The acceptance email arrived on a Tuesday morning when the observatory kitchen smelled of coffee and the comet was still hanging in the dawn.

Juno read it twice, then read it to her father, who took the phone and read it himself, holding it like a star map.

"The Morrow Observation Set," he said slowly. "That's her. And Nell taught you the names, and you drew the sky — that's the family name, Juno, in the literature. In the permanent record of the sky."

It was the first time anyone had called the paper a family matter, and it was exactly right. The wrong chart was a cry; the code was a will; the paper was the reply. They had answered, in the only language the family had, and the sky had accepted the answer.

Chapter 35: The Renaming

Juno brought Kellar the letter the day the acceptance came. She wrapped it in a copy of the paper's proof and walked it down to the freight hall herself, because some things are not mail.

"You can contest the data," she told him at the door of the institute's converted office. "You'll lose. But the letter isn't archive property. It was left to the family, not to the archive. I'm giving it to you because you should read it."

Ash Kellar took it with the careful hands of a man who had spent the winter betting against a dead woman's sky and losing. He was tired. He was brilliant. He was not, Juno had finally understood, a villain. He was a pressure — the institute's weight leaning on one good man — and a pressure has a driver only in the way a storm does.

He read the letter standing in the doorway, the cold light falling across the paper. Juno watched his face do the thing her father's had done: the slow opening, the rearrangement of a world. Iona's words did not argue. They simply placed him — she had built a machine to keep a promise longer than she could, and the sky had kept it, and here was the proof in his hands, warm from the walk.

"The recovery observer," he said at last, very quietly. "I called her plates scrap."

"The instrument was borrowed for a night," Juno said. "It was returned. I signed it."

He laughed — a short, honest sound, the first she had ever heard from him. "'Borrowed for the night. Returned.'" He shook his head. "Your grandmother would have liked you."

That afternoon Kellar withdrew the institute's claim. The retraction went out, releasing the archives, and — this part no one had expected — assigning the institute's digitizing equipment to the observatory on permanent loan. The council met the following week.

The vote was not close. The Morrow Observatory would stay open. It would be renamed, in its centenary year, the Iona Wren Observatory at Morrow. The East Ridge station — the Crow's Nest, the Field, the mast — would be restored as a working field station, not a memorial.

Dad told Juno at the kitchen table, his voice doing a careful, steady thing that was nearly joy. "She kept this place alive on nothing, all those years, when I couldn't even look up," he said. "And now the town votes to name the sky after your grandmother."

The observatory's fate and the comet's had been one question, and the answer was the same word: Wren.

Chapter 36: The Comet, Named

The circular arrived in early March, when the snow was softening at the edges of the road and the mornings came a minute earlier every day.

The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Circular. The formatting did not matter. What mattered was the name, in the permanent ledger of the sky, next to the designation that had been hers for fifty years:

C/1976 J1 (Wren).

The comet was named — not after the instrument, not after the institute, not after the astronomer who had rediscovered it, but after the discoverer and predictor, the one who had built the map and the machine and the wrongness that had brought everyone here. Fifty years after her death, Dr. Iona Wren's name went up into the sky she had watched, and it would be there for as long as anything human is there, which is to say: as long as the sky is.

Juno read the paragraph that contained her own name twice, to be sure she had not invented it.

"Recovery observer: J. Mercer."

J. Mercer. In the same paragraph as Iona Wren. The family name in the sky — both of them, side by side, the way a place is named for two people who loved the same thing.

She stood in the dome with the circular in her hand, the Old Glass cold above her, the mid-morning light falling through the open shutter like a pale sheet of astronomy. She thought of the nine-year-old who had drawn her first sky, and her mother bending over her shoulder saying the names like a lullaby. The Crow. The fixed and the faithful. She thought of the letter: "If you are reading this, the chart did its work."

The chart had done its work. The wrongness had been attended to. The attention had become a code, the code a will, the will a paper, the paper a name — and now the name was in the permanent record, where no one could take it, where it would outlast the observatory, the town, the snow, everything except the stars themselves, and even the stars move, and the record does not.

She went down to the kitchen and told her father. He said nothing for a long time. Then he walked to the west corridor — locked his whole working life — and stood at the door with his hand on the frame, and after a while he came back and said, "She would have hated the fuss and loved the name."

Juno laughed, and it came out halfway to a sob, and that was all right. The sky was full of her family now: the Crow, the Wren star, and the comet named for her grandmother, with her own initials in the same sentence — the fixed and the faithful, all of them, keeping their appointments.

Chapter 37: The Observatory

The restoration began in the thaw, and it was not glamorous. It was scaffolding and tar and a man named Pete who fixed the dome shutter with the same patient hands he used on church roofs. It was school groups, bus after bus from the valley, kids stamping snow off their boots in the visitor center while Dad gave the tour he had given for years — except now his voice carried a different weight, and he opened the west corridor with his own key.

The Old Glass found a new lease on life. With the archives released and the institute's digitizing rig installed, the great refractor pulled double duty: survey nights for the observatory's new program, public nights every Friday, and the long careful sessions when the comet still had to be followed, because a named comet is not a finished comet. It is a responsibility. Leo ran the Friday nights with a star projector he had salvaged and rebuilt — a beautiful brass spiderweb of a thing that filled the dome with the sky.

The west corridor was opened in April, in a small ceremony with the council and Old Tom Griggs, who had brought a thermos and stood at the back with his cap in his hands. Iona's office became a reading room. Her plates went up on the walls — the whole Morrow Observation Set in silvered glass, the comet at every stage of its flowering. Her desk was kept, her papers under glass, and the brass plate from the field station, I.W., 1976, hung by the door.

And the Lantern came down.

It took a careful afternoon on the ridge to unseat it from the mast where it had kept its promise for half a century. Its battery was spent; its mirror was dull; its coded flasher had fired its last charge on perihelion night, as if answering the instrument that had come home. Juno carried it down the ridge herself, wrapped in a blanket like something alive, and mounted it by the reading-room door, where its parabolic mirror caught the hall light and threw a soft, steady gleam across the floor.

"A clockwork star," Leo said, standing beside her. "Your grandmother built a clockwork star, and it kept time for fifty years."

Juno touched the dull brass. The mirror was cold, and clean, and done. The message it had carried was answered.

"Heard," she said. "Retired, with honors."

Chapter 38: The New Map

The redraw took all spring.

It was the same work she had been doing since she was nine — the nightly watching, the careful recording, the pencil moving across the page — but it was not the same work at all. Before, she had drawn the sky to keep it. Now she drew it to finish it. Nine years of notebooks lay open across the library table, and out of them a new atlas was being born: the whole sky she had watched, corrected, beautiful, complete.

The corrected chart was the centerpiece — not Kraz this time, but everything: the Crow and the Wren's wing, the faint winter rivers of the Milky Way, the comet's track stitched across the pages like a hem. Every star at its true position. Every constellation named in her mother's voice, drawn with her own hand.

The Wren star went in last.

Kraz, Beta Corvi, at its true position — the star her grandmother had deliberately misplaced by half a moon's width, the star that had been the bait, the door, the whole reason any of this existed. Juno inked it at last, exact and clean, and then she added one small thing the sky did not have: a gold star, painted over the point, no larger than a grain of rice.

"The Wren star," Leo said, watching over her shoulder. "The sky didn't put that there."

"No," Juno said. "I did. The chart can hold one kindness that isn't in the sky."

She lettered the dedication on the first page in her neatest hand, the way her mother had taught her the constellations — patient, warm, over and over until the names stuck:

For Nell — who taught me the names.

She sat with it a long time, her hand flat on the paper, and the room was quiet, and the light came low through the library windows, and the map was correct. Nine years of being the only one who noticed had become this: a sky drawn twice, once wrong and once right, and the right one was going to hang in the dome where everyone could see it.

"Half a moon's width," she said at last. "That's the size of a small wrongness."

Leo looked at the gold star. "And of a long love."

Then he was quiet, because the reading-room door was open, and her father stood there, not having knocked, looking at the atlas on the table the way he had once looked at the sky.

"Is it done?" he asked.

"It's done," Juno said.

He came in and stood at the table, and he did not touch it. He only looked. "Nell would have wanted to see this," he said. His voice was careful. "She'd have said the gold star was showing off."

Juno laughed, and it came out warm. "Then it's perfect."

Chapter 39: The Spring

Winter broke the way it always did in Morrow — not all at once, but in a series of small surrenders. The snow softened. The road to the valley showed black in the middle. The mornings arrived a minute earlier, then two, and the nights, though still clear and cold, began to smell of thawing earth.

The comet faded into the morning sky, but it still showed at twilight, low and patient, a dim torch over the east ridge. The town had gotten used to it, which was the finest thing a town can do with a sky: it stopped being news and became weather, something you looked for on your way home.

Juno's school year ended. The physics coursework she had spent the winter derailing was finished, and finished well, and her teacher — who had once called the chart argument a distraction — asked her, quietly, if she would give the astronomy club a talk in the fall.

Leo's engineering acceptance arrived on a Tuesday, a thick envelope from a university two states away. He told Juno on the ridge, under a sky the color of a bruise at the edges and honey in the middle. He left in the fall. She would be at the observatory, running the summer program. The friendship held steady, the way it had been built to: on numbers, on the sky, on two people who trusted each other enough to be wrong in public.

"Two states," he said. "The sky is the same there. I'll check."

"You'd better," Juno said. "And write."

"I'm an engineer. I'll write." He paused. "Also the star projector comes with me."

"It does not."

"We'll argue about it in the fall."

The observatory prepared for the exhibition. The library was cleared and swept; the plates were re-housed; Juno's nine years of notebooks were laid out in a long case with the new atlas, and the redrawing of Corvus sat at the head of the room under a light of its own. Dad led the painting crew. Leo rebuilt the star projector's motor. The town paper ran a front-page photograph of the dome with the caption "A Sky for Morrow," and every copy sold out, and Old Tom Griggs kept his clipping in his wallet.

The exhibition opened on the first warm night of the year, when the snow was gone from the ridge and the comet hung in the west like a final star, and the whole town climbed Lookout Hill to see the map of stars their neighbor's daughter had drawn.

Chapter 40: The Exhibition

The exhibition was called "The Map of Stars," and it began with Juno's nine-year-old drawings — the first sky she had ever drawn, the paper gone soft with age, her mother's handwriting in the margin: "the Crow, our way home." The room moved in order, from childhood to now, from the wrong chart to the true one.

The plates were there, Iona's Morrow Observation Set in silvered glass, the comet at every stage of its return. The new images hung beside them — Juno's first crude astrophotographs from the winter, then the guided plates from perihelion night, the ones that had landed in the darkroom with the ridge and the mast and the Lantern's light faint in the deepest exposure. The Lantern itself sat by the door, its spent mirror gleaming softly under the hall lamp, a note beneath it: "A clockwork star. It kept its promise for fifty years."

But the centerpiece was two charts, hung side by side in the middle of the room. On the left, the 1976 atlas, the printed chart, Kraz plotted half a moon's width from where the sky put it. On the right, Juno's corrected drawing, Kraz at its true position, the gold star over the point. Between them, on a card in Juno's hand:

0.42 degrees — the size of a small wrongness, and of a long love.

The town came. They came in coats that had been worn all winter and didn't know how to hang properly in warmth; they came with children who pointed at the comet photographs and asked if it was real; they came because Morrow's one great export had always been the sky, and this was the first time the sky had written back.

Dad led the tours. He had given the observatory tour for twenty years, but he had never given this one, and his voice broke once, at the wrong chart, and he steadied it, and the room pretended not to notice, which was exactly right. Leo ran the star projector in the dome, the brass spiderweb throwing the corrected sky across the walls, and he named each constellation the way Juno's mother had taught her to, which was the way Iona had taught Nell: patient, warm, true.

Juno stood at the edge of the room and watched her family name walk past her work. The fixed and the faithful. Nine years of being the only one who noticed had ended here, in a warm room full of people looking up.

Old Tom Griggs came last, after the crowd thinned, his cap in his hands, and he stood before the corrected chart for a long time.

"She'd have liked this," he said. "She always said the crow would come home."

Chapter 41: The Astronomers

On the second Saturday of the exhibition, the valley road filled with cars. Leo counted plates from the projector booth; Dad put out the good thermos. Juno stood at the door of the dome in the cold, watching a dark institute truck park at the bottom of the hill, and she did not know whether to be angry or ready.

Dr. Ash Kellar came up the path alone. He wore no coat against the wind, only his survey jacket, and he walked the way he did everything: as if the ground owed him an answer. He stopped in front of the exhibit hall, where the wrong chart hung beside the true one, and he stood there a long time with his hands behind his back.

Then he turned to the room — the whole room, the town, the retired astronomers, the schoolchildren — and he said, plainly, "I was wrong about the sky, and about you. The recovery belongs to Morrow. It always did."

The institute's digitizing equipment arrived on Wednesday in three crates, freight paid, addressed to the Morrow Observatory, with a card in Kellar's neat hand: "For the archive that was right."

The retired astronomers came the way the comet had come — gradually, then all at once. A small woman who had been Iona's assistant in '72 told Juno about the winter Iona had taught herself to grind a mirror by hand, because the catalog was too slow and she would not wait. A tall man, ninety if a day, said Iona had once calculated his whole career's worth of luck in minutes and mailed it to him. "She always said the crow would come home," he said, and his voice cracked on the old faith. "I thought she meant the star. She meant all of it."

Juno listened to them the way she had listened to the sky — quietly, completely, writing nothing down, letting it enter her whole. She stood in a room of astronomers who knew her name, and it was not the cold thrill of being right. It was warmer than that. It was being expected.

Kellar found her at the end of the day, at the window where the ridge went dark. He did not apologize again. He only said, "Your grandmother's paper on Corvus proper motions — the one that proves the chart was bait — I'd like to include it in the digitization. If the family permits."

"The family permits," said Juno, and she heard how the words sounded in her own mouth, a gate swung open, a door in a west corridor finally unlocked.

Outside, the dome light came on, and the valley below gathered its lights like a second sky. She had made them look. That was the thing. She had made them all look.

Chapter 42: Juno's Name

The circular had been out for a week, and the papers had finally found it. Juno read the sentence aloud in the kitchen on a grey Sunday: "'Juno Mercer, 16, of Morrow, recovered the comet her grandmother predicted before her death fifty years ago.'" She read it twice. It was strange to see her name in the same sentence as Iona's, as if the alphabet itself had decided they belonged together.

The visitors came up the hill with copies folded under their arms. Dad stopped answering the phone and let the machine record. Leo papered the kitchen window with clippings, and Juno left them up, because it was winter, and the light through newsprint was not so different from the light through frost.

The old astronomer — the tall one, the one whose luck Iona had calculated — found her in the reading room that had been Iona's office, in front of the plates on the wall. He told her that Iona had once written to a friend that she hoped the family would have "one more with the sky inside them." She had not been sure it would be blood; she had been sure it would be someone.

"She meant you," the old man said. "She didn't know your name. She didn't need to."

That night, Juno stood at the long table in the library and laid out the new map — the corrected atlas, the gold star at Kraz, the dedication already penciled in. She took up a pen and practiced, in the margin, the way her mother had taught her to sign things neatly: Juno Mercer. Then she crossed it out and wrote it again, slower, the way you would learn a new star: Juno Wren Mercer.

She did not know when she had decided. The name had been waiting, like a plate in a darkroom, and the paper had come up under the red light and there it was. Her mother's daughter, her grandmother's namesake, her father's fierce girl — all of it, one signature, the whole sky of her.

When she signed the guidebook's title page for the exhibition, the pen moved without hesitation. The visiting children asked her to sign their programs. She did, in the new name, and the name was a flag planted, a lantern lit.

Dad watched from the doorway and said nothing, but at dinner he set a place for her and filled her glass first, the way her mother used to do for guests. It was, she thought, the closest he came to saying it out loud: you are the family now, the whole of it, and I am glad.

Chapter 43: The Award

The award was small, and that was the best thing about it. A certificate, a plaque the size of a hand, and a modest sum that Dad insisted they put toward the observatory's new darkroom. The ceremony was held in the dome, under the Old Glass, and the town came out for it the way it came out for a clear night in March — everyone, all at once, glad of the sky.

The mayor spoke too long. Leo ran the dome shutter and let the true sky in through the slit, which was worth more than any speech. Juno sat in the front row in a clean coat, her hands folded, and she could feel her father beside her, warm and still, the way she had learned to feel the temperature of his grief in a room.

When they called her name, she walked up under the Old Glass and looked out at the faces: Leo in the projector booth, Dad in the third row, Tom Griggs in his work coat, Kellar in the back, half-hidden, watching. She had written a speech. She had practiced it into the dome's dark on three separate nights.

She said none of it.

"The chart was wrong," she said. Her voice was steady, the way it got when she was naming a constellation. "It was wrong by half a moon's width, and everyone had a reason it couldn't be. The precession. The plate warp. The dust on the lens. I checked them all. None of them was the truth."

She looked down at her hands, then up again.

"I redrew it. Anyone could have. The stars just needed someone to look. That's the whole thing. That's the whole award." She held up the plaque. "Half a moon's width of looking. And a grandmother who wanted to be found."

The room was quiet. Somewhere in the back, Kellar began to clap, and then everyone did, and the sound went up into the dome and came back down off the brass like a second sky, all of it held.

Afterward, in the kitchen, Dad put the plaque on the shelf beside Iona's plate on the wall — the two of them, brass and glass, the old and the new of the same sentence. Juno stood looking at them for a long time.

"It's not about the award," she said.

"I know," said Dad. "It's about the shelf."

She laughed — a real laugh, the kind that startled her — and he looked at her, surprised, and then he laughed too, and the two of them stood in the warm kitchen laughing at nothing, at everything, at the whole wrong chart, corrected.

Chapter 44: The Father's Pride

Dad was asked to say a few words. It was the one request the whole winter that he could not deflect, because it came from the observatory's new board, and because Leo had put it to him in front of Juno, and because the dome was full of people who had come to see her.

He said fewer. He stood under the Old Glass with the paper in his hand and looked at it for a long time, and then he folded it away without reading it.

"I kept the building alive," he said. "That's what I did. I fixed the roof. I greased the dome. I kept the pipes from freezing. I thought that was the whole job."

He stopped. The room waited.

"I stopped looking up," he said, "when her mother died. I want you to know that. I want it on the record, in front of the Old Glass. For twelve years I kept the building that points at the sky, and I didn't once look at it. I thought I was honoring Nell by holding still. I was just afraid of what I'd see."

He looked at Juno, and his voice broke, cleanly, the way ice breaks at a known fault.

"Your mother taught you the names of the sky," he said. "Your grandmother taught the sky to answer. You did both. I don't know how to say what that is, to me. I don't have the words for it. I only know that my daughter did the thing I was afraid of, and she did it alone, and she did it right."

The room was very still. Juno could feel her own heart, the way you feel the cold at the top of the ridge.

"Your grandmother would have loved you," Dad said. And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Nell would have loved you. She would have — she would have pointed at you. Like a star. She'd have said, that one's mine."

Later, at dawn, they walked up the hill together in the cold, the way they had when she was small, before everything sealed over. The comet was a last smudge in the east, thin as breath. Dad stopped, and Juno stopped, and they stood in the freezing grass and looked up.

He was looking. That was the whole miracle of it, the plain fact of it. Her father, who had not looked up in twelve years, stood at dawn on the hill and looked at the sky until the smudge faded into the blue.

"Always look up," he said finally, in a voice like her grandmother's, though he could not have known he was quoting her. "I think that was the whole of it. All along. Just — always look up."

Juno said nothing. There was nothing to add to the family language; the family language was the looking. She slipped her hand into his, and they stood that way, the two of them, under the lightening sky, until the cold sent them down to the coffee and the day.

Chapter 45: The Night

It was the last clear night of the comet's showing, and the observatory stayed open late. The town came and went in waves, Leo ran the dome, and the Old Glass turned on its great circle, patient, teaching children to look. But at the end of it, after the last car had gone down the hill, Juno and Leo took the astrograph up the East Ridge one more time.

No urgency. That was the new thing, and it felt like a muscle learning a new way to move. They set up the instrument on the Field by feel, by starlight, the way they had on perihelion night, but slow now, and easy, and warm with the ridge wind.

The comet rose into the clear dark, faint and beautiful, a small smudge of returning light, and they let it trail across the eyepiece without guiding a single plate. It was enough to watch. It was, Juno thought, the difference between chasing a thing and keeping it company.

They sat on the flat stone with the astrograph between them, drinking from Tom Griggs's old thermos, and the ridge was the same ridge and the sky was the same sky and everything else had changed.

"I got the letter," Leo said. "Engineering. Full offer."

"Your machines that can be fixed," she said.

"Them." He turned his cup in his hands. "They'll still be there in four years. The sky will wait for us."

"The sky will wait for us," she repeated, testing it, the way she tested a coordinate before she trusted it. It held. It was true. The sky had waited fifty years; it would wait a little longer.

He told her about the city, the campus, the river that ran through it, the observatory there that had already written to him about a work-study. She told him about her summer program at Morrow — her own program now, hers and the board's, teaching the first sessions of the restored station. They talked about the fall the way you talk about a season you can see coming, and it did not feel like goodbye. It felt like an orbit — return, and return, and return.

"Half a moon's width," Leo said at last, looking at the comet, "and it changed everything."

"It was bait," she said. "It was a grandmother leaving the porch light on."

He laughed. The sound went out over the ridge and the crow came down out of the dark at the edge of the Field, and neither of them said anything, because the ridge had its own language and they were learning to be fluent in it.

They packed up the astrograph in the cold, first light smearing the east, and carried it down to the Crow's Nest and bedded it in its cradle, and walked home through the grey morning, not hurrying, the comet fading behind them, the sky holding its place above them, the winter almost over.

Chapter 46: The Sky

The last hour, she wanted alone. She told no one — Dad's light was on in the kitchen, Leo was asleep in the observatory's spare room, and the town was still dark under its roofs — and she walked up the ridge in the pre-dawn cold with the astrograph on her shoulder, the way her grandmother must have walked it a thousand times.

She set up on the Field and guided on Iona's hand-written charts one final time. The comet was there, faint now, a ghost of its winter self, and she watched it through the brass tube until the east began to fill, and then she sat on the flat stone and watched it with her own eyes, the way she had watched the sky since she was nine.

The Lantern sat on the mast, patient, its solar cell catching the first grey. She had thought, all winter, that she would be ready for the moment it finally died. She had rehearsed it. She had told herself it was only a machine — a parabolic mirror, a coded flasher, a photodiode, a promise engineered in brass.

At the first true light, the Lantern fired.

One single flash. Long. Longer than any code she had ever recorded. A held note, a held breath, the whole of the message in one last syllable — and then silence, the battery spent, the promise kept, the light gone out like a star that had finished its work.

Juno sat very still in the dawn, and the ridge was empty and the sky was full of family.

The Crow rose low in the southeast, Kraz at its true position now, the small gold star on her map made real in the brightening air. The Wren star. And above the ridge where the Lantern had gone dark, the faint last smudge of the comet that was named for her grandmother, the crow come home, the return completed.

She did not cry. The sky did not ask for tears; it asked for accuracy, and she was accurate. She packed the astrograph carefully, walked the ridge in the cold, and came down the hill with the light full on the world.

The Lantern had kept its promise longer than Iona could. That was the whole sentence, she understood now, the whole arc of it: a woman building a thing that would outlast her grief and her body and her name, a clockwork star set to fire at the exact hour of its maker's return.

In the kitchen, Dad had the coffee on. He did not ask where she had been. He just poured her a cup and stood at the window with her, watching the ridge go bright, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, and the silence was the right one, the held note, the single long flash.

Chapter 47: The Message, Answered

The logbook was the same ledger Iona had used. Dad had found it in the west corridor when they opened the office — a great cloth-bound book, the spine cracked, the pages the color of old snow, and on the first leaf Iona's handwriting, precise and warm, the family hand before the family had her in it.

Juno carried it to the library table one evening, alone, and opened it to the last entry Iona had written, in March of 1976: If I am late, the map will say where. She had read it a hundred times. She had memorized the slant of it, the way the letters leaned forward, eager, the way her own did.

She turned to a fresh page and took up a pen. It felt, she thought, like the most serious thing she had ever done — more serious than the recovery, more serious than the paper, more serious than the name. Because this was not science. This was the thing science was for, the whole reason the sky mattered.

She wrote the observation first, the way a family answers letters, in the only language the family had. Date. Time. Conditions: clear, minus four, wind light. Object: C/1976 J1 (Wren), recovered, observed, mapped. Then, in her own hand, beneath the coordinates, the part that was not astronomy:

I redrew it. The map is correct. You can rest now.

She set the pen down and looked at the words until the shape of them was familiar, until they were not strange to the page, until they looked like they had always been there, waiting under the red light of the darkroom.

I redrew it. The map is correct. You can rest now.

She signed it Juno Wren Mercer, and the ink took, and the signature sat beside her grandmother's last line like two ends of a single sentence finally brought together, the wrong chart and the true one, the question and the answer.

Leo came in quietly and stood at her shoulder. He did not ask what she had written. He read it over her shoulder, and then he put a hand on her back, once, briefly, the way you would steady a finder scope, and he said, "She'll have seen that."

"The Lantern saw the sky," Juno said. "The logbook's for the people. But — yes. I think. I think she'll have seen it."

He nodded, and they stood together in the library, in the warm light, between the grandmother's ledger and the new map, and the message was answered in the only way it could have been: observed, recorded, signed.

The crow came down on the dome at dusk, as it always did, and settled, and stayed.

Chapter 48: The Instrument, Kept

The question, once the comet faded and the exhibition closed and the papers had their story, was what to do with the astrograph. The institute wanted it for its archive. A museum in the city wrote twice. There was a collector — Dad had taken the call and hung up without a word, and refused to say what the man had offered.

Juno settled it at the board meeting in the way that felt least like a meeting, standing in the reading room with her hand on the brass tube.

"It's a working instrument," she said. "It was made to be used. It goes back to the ridge. To the Crow's Nest. That's where it belongs."

The board agreed. It was, Leo said later, the most obvious decision of the whole winter, and the one that took them all a month to reach.

They carried it up the East Ridge on a clear Saturday, the whole party of them — Dad, Leo, Tom Griggs, Kellar, who had come to help and pretended he had not come to see it placed — and they bedded the astrograph in its cradle in the restored Crow's Nest, on the flat stone where Iona had watched her comet, under the same roof she had built, stone by stone, fifty years ago.

Tom Griggs produced a cloth and a tin of polish from his coat, the same tin, he said, he had watched Iona use, and he cleaned the brass plate until it shone.

I.W., 1976.

Then, with the ceremony of a man who knew exactly what he was doing, he added a second line, in careful, patient letters:

restored by J.W.M.

Juno watched him, and the brass, and the two lines, the maker and the restorer, fifty years of the same sentence. She ran a thumb over the new letters. She did not have to say anything. The plate said it.

The astrograph went to work that summer. Juno used it for the first sessions of the summer program, teaching children to guide on Iona's charts, and it took light the way it had always taken light, patient, faithful, the crow at home. Dad took to walking up the ridge in the evenings to polish it, a rag in his pocket, and he would stand there a while in the fading light, not looking at the sky, exactly, but not afraid of it either — just standing, easy, the way he had used to stand.

The Lantern kept its place by the reading room door, brought down, mounted, silent, a clockwork star that had kept its promise and now kept watch. The schoolchildren asked what it was. Juno told them: that's what it looks like when someone wants to be found.

The instrument was not a museum piece. It was not an archive. It was in use, on the ridge, under the sky it was made for, and its brass plate carried the two names of its long love.

Chapter 49: The Map, Correct

The new atlas had one last page, and Juno left it for the end, the way you save the last of a good winter's light. She had been drawing the sky since she was nine — nine years of notebooks, all of it wrong in the small way that mattered, all of it learning, all of it love. The final page was the whole sky she had watched, corrected, complete.

She drew it over three clear nights, on the ridge, at the Field, the map spread flat on the flat stone and the sky spread above it, the two of them, chart and heaven, compared at every hour until she was certain. She drew the Crow at its true position, Kraz where Kraz actually was, and at Kraz she set the small gold star — the Wren star, the star that had waited fifty years to be put right.

Under it she wrote the dedication, the way she had planned since before the winter began, when it had been only a hope and not yet a fact:

For Nell — who taught me the names.

And in the corner, the correction note, small and clean, the way her grandmother would have written it: Kraz (Beta Corvi), misplotted 0.42 degrees on the 1976 chart; restored here to true position. The Fraction. Half a moon's width of wrongness, half a moon's width of love.

She hung the new map in the dome, beside the Old Glass, where the light came down through the slit on it all day. Leo helped her level it. Dad stood back and looked at it, and then looked at the sky through the open shutter, and then back at the map, and nodded once, the way you nod at a job done right.

The wrong chart — the old 1976 atlas, the printed lie that had started everything — she did not throw away. She carried it down to the archive herself and laid it in a box with a note in her hand, and she wrote the note slowly, wanting it exact:

The most important mistake ever made at this observatory. Do not correct it. It taught us to look.

Tom Griggs was in the archive when she sealed the box. He watched her, and after a while he said, "Iona kept the letter she got wrong, too. First paper she ever published. She kept it all her life. Said it reminded her to look."

Juno looked at the box, then at the old man.

"That's the family thing," she said. "Looking."

"That's the family thing," he said. And they stood in the cold archive among the plates, the two of them, the wrong chart sealed and the true map hung, the whole winter of looking made permanent, the map correct at last, and the stars — the fixed and the faithful — keeping their own silence above the dome, waiting for the next one with the sky inside them to look.

Chapter 50: The Stars, Named

The summer program's first night came in July, late and warm, and Juno stood under the dome with the map under her arm and felt, for the first time, that the building was full of exactly the people it should be. Schoolchildren on the floor, parents on the benches, Dad by the light switch, Leo back for a week before his first term, Tom Griggs in the back row with his thermos.

She opened the dome, and the sky came in.

She taught them the way her mother had taught her — the way Nell had stood at the window with Juno small beside her and named the sky into her, star by star, until the names were part of the furniture of her mind. She moved the Old Glass to the Crow, to Kraz, and the children pressed their faces to the eyepiece one by one.

A small girl in the front row tugged her sleeve, and asked the question her mother had asked Juno once, in a different room, a lifetime ago.

"Which one is yours?"

Juno looked up. The Crow was high, Kraz gold in the eyepiece's memory, and the comet's empty place — where C/1976 J1 (Wren) had returned and would return again in fifty years — lay low in the east, waiting.

"See that empty place," she said, and pointed, and the child followed her finger. "That's where the comet comes home. It's named for my grandmother. She's there. She'll be back. The sky keeps its appointments."

The child nodded gravely, satisfied, the way children are satisfied by true things, and Juno watched her file past the Lantern by the reading room door and touch its brass. The names go down, she thought. The looking goes on.

Leo found her afterward in the kitchen, a postcard in his hand, from his first week in the city — a picture of the campus observatory, its dome identical in the grey to Morrow's own. On the back, his neat engineering hand:

The sky is the same here. I checked. — L.

She turned it over twice, and then she tucked it into the frame of the new map, beside the gold star, where the family letters lived.

The observatory lights came on across the hill, warm in the dusk. Dad stood in the doorway of the dome, his silhouette against the bright slit. He did not say anything — they had passed the point where words were required. He just looked up, and then he looked at her, and the two looks were the same look, the family look, the whole of the winter in it.

Juno stood under the dome with the map under her arm, the way the instruments stood, patient, faithful, turned to the sky. Above her the stars went on being named, the fixed and the faithful, and she went on naming them, the crow and the Wren star and the comet's returning place, the whole long language of her family, spoken and answered, mapped and true.

She looked up. That was all it ever was, in the end. She looked up, and the sky was full of family, and the map was correct, and the night was hers, and the stars were named.
