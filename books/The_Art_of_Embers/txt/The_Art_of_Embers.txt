The Art of Embers
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: January: The Hearth
New Year's Day, and I lay the first fire of the year in the stone fireplace my father built. The house has stood cold all night, and the cold has its own smell — stone, old ash, the faint sweetness of a damp chimney — and I work in the order he taught me, which is the only order there is. Clean the grate. Carry the old ash out, because a fire wants a clear hearth the way a page wants a clean margin. Then the newspaper, twisted loose, not tight, because a tight twist is a wick that starves the flame it feeds.

The house is a hundred years old. The fireplace is not. My father built it when I was a girl, laid every stone himself — feldspar, granite, river stone — with neighbor men to heave the big ones and one mason to lean on, and even the mason, the story goes, stood back and watched, because the man building it would not be told how. He set each stone on its broadest face. That is why the chimney is still true after all these years: he left no gap that time could argue with. I run my hand along the hearthstone and it is warm with nothing on it but my own waiting.

I am forty-six. I measure the room the way he taught me to measure a fire — the draw of the flue, the height of the ceiling, the distance from the hearth to the first chair. A fire must be fitted to its room. Too small and it shivers at the mouth of the flue; too large and it drives everyone back against the wall. I want this one to fill the room without owning it. The kindling goes in first, split side down, then the smaller splits crossed above it, then the log — oak, I have chosen oak — set back where the draft can reach it.

The year ahead lies in the empty grate like a stove that has not been lit. I know what I mean to write: a book about fire, the craft of it, one essay for each month, twelve fires. Standing here in the cold with the kindling in my hand, I understand the truer thing, which is that I do not yet know what the book is for. I know what a fire is for. I am hoping they are the same thing.

The first match of the year. The paper takes, the kindling catches, and the flames go up the chimney with a sound like the first breath of the year. The room fills with the smell of burning oak, and the light moves up the wall, and I am not alone here — my father is in the stone, my mother is three streets away with a stove gone cold — and the year, which was empty, is beginning to be lit.

Chapter 2: The Chimney
Ed Kowalski comes the third week of January, when the cold has set its teeth in. His truck says KOWALSKI CHIMNEY in letters worn almost to nothing, and he shakes my hand in the yard and looks up at the cap and says, "She came back."

Two years back a bird worked past the cap and built a nest on the flue's shoulder, and that winter the fire drew like a man breathing through a blanket. Ed found it with his light — a bowl of mud and dry grass packed smooth on the inside, right at the bend — and he carried it down in a bucket, and we stood in the yard and looked at it. I burned it. That seemed the right use, and Ed agreed, and we watched it go up.

Now he goes up the ladder with his brush and his rods, and I stand at the foot of the house and listen to the scrape of the bristles the way you listen to a doctor's stethoscope. A chimney is a house breathing out. Every fire I light pulls air up this throat of stone, and the breath carries the ghost of every log I have burned. The creosote is the chimney's memory — the smoke that never rose, that hung against the stone and hardened there into black leaves. Ed sweeps it down and it falls onto the hearth, and I think: this is what a house keeps of its fires.

He is down in an hour, sooted at the cheekbone. The cap is rusting, he tells me; the liner is sound; I am burning too green in the spring. That is his standing complaint, and I give him my standing answer, that the wood is what the year gave me. He shrugs; he has said this to every stove and fireplace in Merrow and none of them has ever argued back. He sweeps the last of the creosote into the bucket and sets it inside the door, and I pay him, and we both look up at the sky, which is the color of a flue before it is cleaned.

We settle up, and he leaves the bucket for me to carry out, and I stand in the empty hearth and look up into the dark of the flue, which is clean now — the house's throat, open, ready for the year's breath. The draft moves against my face, faint and cold, and I think of the smoke that will rise through it between now and spring, and of the nest that was there once, and of Ed climbing the ladder to find it, patient as a doctor, and I am grateful for him in the way you are grateful for the people who take care of the invisible parts of your life.

Chapter 3: The First Fire of the Year
The log I chose for New Year's Day was oak, and I chose it the way you choose a coat for a long walk — for what it will do over hours, not minutes. Oak is the long burn. It is slow to catch and slower to give up, and it burns with a heat that sits in the room like a weight. Poplar is the quick comfort. Ash is the even flame. Maple is the bright show. But on the first day of the year I wanted the log that would still be a coal when the night was only halfway home.

Laying it, I remembered the first fire I ever made. I was twelve. The fireplace was new stone then — my father was still finishing the pointing along the arch — and I knelt on the cold hearth with the matches and the newspaper, and he knelt behind me, his hand over mine. I can still feel the size of that hand, the knuckles, the weight. Together we struck the match and held it to the paper, and his voice was right at my ear, saying easy, easy, let it take.

What I remember best is not the fire but the hand. He was not a man who touched easily, and that afternoon all of his attention came down into that one hand, steadying mine, teaching my wrist to be still. The flame caught. He let go. I have lit a thousand fires since, and I have never once lit one without feeling, for a moment, that the hand is still there.

The oak catches. The flames find the split and go up it like a ladder. The room warms by degrees, the way a room warms when it is being done right — you cannot feel it move, only that the cold is retreating from the chairs, from the books, from the glass in the windows. I sit on the hearthstone and watch, and the year turns itself toward me like a page turning.

This is the fire I will measure the others against. I decide, sitting here, to keep a record — every fire I tend this year, the wood, the day, the reason for it. I do not know what the record is for. I will find out. The oak burns, and the year begins, and I have already, without knowing it, written the first line of the book: what we burn, what we keep, what rises.

Chapter 4: The Woodpile
The woodpile stands by the back door where my father taught me to build it — two runners of scrap two-by-four to keep the wood off the damp ground, then the rounds laid bark-up, tight as a stone wall, with a lean of a degree toward the house so the rain runs off instead of in. The pile is my inventory for the year. Oak on the left, ash in the middle, maple on the right, and at the far end the soft stuff — poplar, pine — that will go on the quick fires of spring, the ones you light for an hour and let die.

I know this pile the way I know my own closets. The oak was split from a tree that came down in the ice storm four years back, and it has been seasoning since — you can tell by the cracks at the end of each round, the radial fissures where the water has left, and by the ring of the splits when you knock two together, which is bright, like good china. Ash is the one wood you can burn green. Maple warps and pops and throws sparks, so it waits for the fireplace with the screen up. I could name every log's story. This pile is mostly memory anyway.

Wood is stored summer. The tree stood in the sun and took it in, and the split rounds are sunlight kept for January — the light of four Augusts ago, the rain of two springs, held in the cells and released as heat when I need it. My father taught me that when I was a girl and I thought it was poetry; I know now it is only chemistry, which is a better poetry.

I count the pile the way I will count the year. Two cords of oak, a cord and a half of ash, a cord of maple, a face of soft. It is enough, if the winter is not cruel, and it is not enough if the winter is, and I will learn which. A stack of wood is a bet on the future. My father called it the honest bank, because it pays interest in the form of warmth and never defaults except to fire.

I take two splits of oak from the top and carry them in. The pile settles a little, the way a sentence settles when you remove a word.

Chapter 5: The Kindling
Kindling is the part of the fire no one thinks to name. It is the first voice, and it has to believe in the fire before the logs do. Good kindling is dry, fibrous, and small enough to be careless with — I keep a box of it by the door, split from the soft wood and the trim ends of the year, cedar when I can get it, because cedar catches like a struck match. What catches is what has given up the most water. What does not catch is what is still busy being a tree.

The phone rang in the cold of mid-January, and it was my mother. She did not say hello. "My stove is sulking," she said, "and I am too old for sulks."

Ruth is seventy-four and short with fools. Her stove is the cast-iron wood stove in her kitchen, the one she has cooked on since before I was born, and on a cold morning it will not draw until the flue is warm, and the way to warm a flue is to burn a little paper in the stove door, and my mother would rather complain than prime her own flue, and we both know it.

"I'll come over," I said.

"You will come over and light a fire I have been lighting for fifty years," she said, and I heard her not quite keeping the gladness out.

I brought my kindling box. You want the split-side of the kindling toward the flame. Her soft wood went first, her paper twisted loose, and I held the match the way my father's hand had held mine, and the fire took, and the stove's draw settled into its long low hum. We stood in her kitchen and watched it, and she said, "You always were better at the first flame than the keeping." I did not argue. Two fires now, in two houses, on one winter — mine in the hearth, hers in the stove — and I drove home through the cold feeling the year turn its first small turn, the spiral beginning.

Chapter 6: February: The Kitchen Stove
February, and the kitchen stove takes over from the hearth. The blue flame of the gas burner is the year's second fire, and I stand over it in the gray morning and watch the way it clings to the pan, a flame that does not dance because it has a job. The hearth fire is for warmth; the stove fire is for food. One wants to be looked at, the other wants to be used. I have heard people call the kitchen the heart of the house. It is truer to say the kitchen is the house's second hearth — the one that works for a living.

My morning is a sequence of small fires. The kettle on the front burner, its blue flame a modest crown; the pan for the eggs, set down on the grate with a hiss; the pilot light, burning all winter whether I ask it to or not, a flame that never goes out, the house's small kept coal. I make coffee, I make toast, I stand at the window and eat over the sink, and the stove ticks and settles around me.

The difference between the two fires is the difference between a poem and a recipe — and I say that as someone who has spent her life on the first and her hands on the second. The hearth makes a room. The stove makes a meal. The hearth is a companion; the stove is a worker, and it is rude to admire a worker too long.

I have been keeping my record of fires. January: the hearth, the mother's stove, the kindling box, the banked coals of a week of nights. February is a different arithmetic. Here the fire is a tool, exact and small, and the craft is in the timing — the pan hot before the oil, the flame lowered the moment the toast browns, the kettle pulled off a heartbeat before it screams.

There is a kind of attention the stove demands that the hearth never does. The hearth forgives; the stove does not. It burns or it simmers, and both are correct, and the cook's whole art is knowing which the food is asking for at that instant. I am learning, this February, to read the food's small requests. It is not unlike editing — line by line, heat by heat, you take the thing down to what it needs and no more. My father built fires in the other room; my mother has been a fire in this one, all my life, small and exact and patient, and the morning goes up in steam and the day begins.

Chapter 7: The Soup
The soup starts with the bones. I keep a bag in the freezer — the carcass of a chicken, the shank of a ham, the rib ends of a roast — and when the bag is full I empty it into the biggest pot I own and cover it with cold water and set it on the lowest burner the stove has. This is the fire that does its work by not doing its work. The soup that is improved by neglect. You put everything in and you walk away, and the low flame does the rest, for hours, without once being asked.

My mother's recipe — and it is hers, I make no claim — is bone, water, onion, carrot, celery, salt, and nothing else, and no skimming, and no stirring, and no peeking. "A watched soup never thickens," Ruth says, "and neither does a watched child." She taught me this the way she taught me everything, by standing behind me in this very kitchen and putting her hands over mine on the pot handle — the same weight, the same patience — and letting me do it wrong for a while, then gently.

The house fills with the smell. There is no smell like a stock going, in February, in a small town, with snow on the sills. It is the smell of the year being made safe.

The phone rang while the pot was going, and it was my mother, and we argued about the soup. Hers is clear because she skims; mine is cloudy because I don't; she called mine mud, and I called hers the same soup, emptied of character, and she laughed — I heard it, low, pleased — and said I was the one who had ruined the celery. It was a good argument. It is the kind of argument you only get to have with one person on earth, and I catalogued it as I hung up: the pot, still simmering, the two of us in our two kitchens, the same low flame under both.

The soup is done at dark. I ladle it out and it is exactly as good as her soup has always been, and I eat it alone in the kitchen and feel, for the length of the bowl, the two of us at the one table.

Chapter 8: The Bread
The bread is the oldest firecraft there is, and I came to it late. Grain, water, fire — the three of them have been doing this together for ten thousand years, and my weekly sourdough is just the latest loaf in that long line. My starter lives in a crock on the counter. Her name is Ruth, because she came from my mother's kitchen, alive and bubbling, a cup of it carried over three streets in a jar on a cold morning, and my mother said, "Keep her fed and she'll keep you." I have kept her fed. She has kept me.

The rhythm of the bread is a small liturgy. Saturday night I feed her — flour, water, a little salt, a stir, a cloth over the crock. Sunday morning she has doubled, alive and spongy, and I scoop out what I need and work it on the board, folding and turning, until the dough smooths and the gluten tightens under my palms and the loaf takes shape like a thing remembering what it is. Then the proof, an hour in the warm corner of the kitchen, and then the oven.

The oven spring is the miracle, and it is not a miracle, it is steam. The dough goes in slack and comes out risen because the trapped water turns to gas and pushes the loaf up from inside — but knowing the chemistry does not make it less startling. The crust browns by the Maillard reaction, sugars and amino acids meeting heat, the smell of it taking over the whole house, the whole street, the whole town if the wind is right. Bread is the fire's oldest finished product. Fire made us able to eat grain at all, and the hearth that baked the loaf is the same hearth that warmed the room, and I think about this every Sunday, standing at the stove, watching the loaf give up its last proof.

I have been thinking about what I am building with this year of fires. The bread is the closest I have come to an answer. A loaf is not made fast; it is made on schedule, in stages, with waiting built in, and the waiting is not the waste, the waiting is the work. Baker's hours — the woman at dawn, the dough asleep in its basket — are the discipline of letting time do the part only time can do.

The bread comes out. I tap the bottom and it sounds hollow, which is the whole language of done. I set it on the rack to cool and the house is full of it, and I stand there with my hands in my pockets like a person who has been paid. I will bring half of it to my mother. She will say it is better than the bakery's, which is a lie, and I will say so, and she will say, "It's not a lie, it's an opinion," and we will both be right.

Chapter 9: The Slow Flame
The low flame is the one no one photographs. Every cook knows it; no cook can describe why it works. It is the flame you set and forget, the one under the stock, under the jam, under the long nothing of a winter afternoon. It has a name in my mother's kitchen — she calls it the whisper — and the whole of her cooking is an argument that the whisper does more than the shout.

What the low flame is for: the simmer that would boil away the bones' marrow if you rushed it; the proof that would die in a hot room and rise in a warm one; the caramelization that cannot be forced, the onion that needs forty minutes to become what it is going to be, and nothing you do with the heat can make it take twenty. The low flame is patience with a temperature. It is the acknowledgment that some things are finished by time and not by fire, and that the fire's only job is to stay out of the way and keep the time warm.

I have been reading my year as a low flame. January was the kindling, all excitement and first light. February is the simmer. And the book I am writing — the twelve essays — I keep having to remind myself that it is February for them too. Nothing worth keeping is made fast. I say this to the jam jars on the shelf, my mother's jars from last summer, and they do not argue. They are the argument. Strawberry and rhubarb and one jar of a plum jam she said was an accident and has been hiding the recipe from ever since. She made them last June, in the heat, at the whisper, letting the fruit give up its water and take in the sugar until the jar itself was the only storage it needed.

I take one down. The ring catches; the lid pops. It is like opening a jar of August in February, and I stand there with the spoon and think that this is the whole craft, the whole year, the whole religion of the whisper: you take the ripe thing, you apply the low steady heat, you keep it, and in the dead month it opens and it is still summer. I close the jar, put it back, and leave the flame low on the stove, doing its long work.

Chapter 10: March: The Pruning Bonfire
March in Merrow is mud. The snow goes and the ground does not, and every yard is a slow brown lake, and the light has changed — you can feel it behind the clouds, the year leaning toward the sun. This is the month of the pruning, and the pruning belongs to my mother.

The orchard is three streets away, three acres of apple and pear and one plum tree she has argued with for thirty years. She planted it the year I was born, so the orchard and I are the same age, and it shows its age the way I show mine — a few branches higher than they should be, a little more deadwood every spring. She stands at the center of it in her barn coat, and she is directing.

"Not that one, that's the spur branch, leave the spur." The Reisers are with us — Joan and Bill, neighbors since before I was born — and I am up the ladder with the saw, and the rule of pruning is the rule of the year: take out what is dead, what is diseased, what is crossing, and cut for the open center, so the light can get in. The three-cut method for the big branches — undercut first, so the weight of the cut does not tear the bark, then the top cut, then the flush cut back to the collar, and the wound closes over. Pruning is the honest violence of care. You hurt the tree to save it, and you do it in the cold month, when it is asleep, and it forgives you in the spring.

The brush piles up. Twigs and suckers and the deadwood, and we drag it to the clearing by the shed, and by noon it is a pile as tall as a man, and the wet weight of it settles and steams a little in the cold. It will be lit on Saturday, the first bonfire of the year, the one whose smoke the whole town reads like a signal: spring, the season of burning, is open.

The mother stands at the foot of the pile and pokes it with a stick, checking the architecture — the air channels, the heart of it — and I watch her, and something in the way she does it makes me look twice. She is slower this year. Not much. Enough. She pushes a dead branch deeper into the pile, and for a moment her hand rests on the wood and she is somewhere else, and then she is back, and she says, "She'll go up like a story," and I do not know why my heart turns over in my chest. I do not know yet. The pile stands, tall as a man, and the rain begins, and we go in, and the pile stays, waiting for the match.

Chapter 11: The Orchard

Mud season. The ground gives under every boot and gives back, so we move in the orchard the way you move on ice, slow and sideways. My mother walks ahead of us with the loppers over her shoulder like a rifle, and the Reisers and I follow with the saws. Bill carries the rope for the big limbs. Joan carries the bucket of sealer.

The orchard is hers the way the fireplace is my father's. She planted it the year I was born, every tree a whip of wood as tall as a shovel handle, forty years of apples since. The old ones, the Gravensteins and the Northern Spys, have trunks I cannot close my hand around. The newer ones, the ones she put in after my father died, are still thin-waisted and young, and she talks to them differently, the way you talk to a child who is still deciding what it will be.

The three-cut method first, for anything the size of my arm. You undercut a third of the way through the limb, a hand's breadth from the collar, so the bark does not tear. Then you cut down from the top, and the limb breaks clean at the undercut, and falls. Then you cut the stub back to the collar, flush, where the wound will close over in a season. Three cuts. The last one is the honest one.

"You're cutting too far out," my mother says, and she is beside me, and she puts her hand over mine the way she did when I was twelve and could not hold a pencil. "There. That collar. The tree will do the rest."

The wound that closes. That is what she means, and I think about it while we drag the cut brush down to the pile at the edge of the orchard, a pile as tall as a man already, the dead wood and the crossing branches and last year's windfall. Pruning is violence with intent, the honest kind. You cut a living thing to make it live longer. You cut what blocks the light from what remains.

The mother works the far row. I watch her from the corner of my eye: the loppers going snip snip, the slow bend to a low limb, the pause before she straightens. She is slower this year. I notice it the way you notice the first gray hair in a mirror, and I set it down and go back to my cuts.

Chapter 12: The Smoke

The pile is dry at its heart. My mother keeps a kerosene can by the back shed, and she pours a circle of it around the base, not on the top, because a fire must be fed from below, and she has been lighting these fires longer than I have been alive. She strikes the match on the zipper of her coat and touches it to the kerosene, and the flame runs around the pile's feet like a rumor.

The smoke goes up through the wet March air, and it does not rise so much as lean, because the air is still holding the cold of the ground. It is the whitest smoke of the year, full of the moisture of the brush, the last of the melt, and it pours sideways over the orchard and down toward the road, and it is the town's signal. You can smell it from the post office. You can smell it from the school. When the smoke goes up over the Reisers' hedgerow, the whole town reads it the way you read a clock: spring.

The fire catches. The brush snaps and settles, and my mother takes the poker and works the pile the way you work a stubborn lock, a turn here, a lift there, giving the fire air where it has stopped breathing. I stand back and watch. This is her fire, the way the hearth is my father's and the forge will be Ada's. Mine is the kindling and the carrying and the stacking. Hers is the turning.

She is slower this year. I have the thought again, the way you have a thought you are not ready to keep. The poker goes in, and there is a pause before it turns, a small weight behind the motion, like a hinge that has begun to need oil. She stamps out a spark that jumps the ring of stones and goes on pokering. I do not say anything. The smoke says everything that needs saying, and what it says is: the ground will open, the sap will move, the orchard will come back. It has always said that. It says it this year the same way.

There is the smell of it in my coat for days. I keep the coat on the hook by the back door and I put my face in it when I come in, and it smells like the year turning.

Chapter 13: The Neighbors

Smoke brings people. It always has, because it is the oldest summons there is, older than the bell in the church tower. The Reisers come first, because they are nearest and because the smoke has already crossed their hedge. Joan carries a thermos of coffee, and Bill carries nothing but his hands, which he holds out to the fire like a man paying respects. The next-door boy, ten years old, appears on the fence and then over it, dragging a length of dead limb twice his size, which he feeds to the fire with the solemnity of a ceremony.

Del Proulx passes in his truck, the town's fire chief, and he slows at the drive and leans out. "Smoke's up," he says, which is how Del says hello. He does not get out. He does not need to. This is the first gathering of the year, and it has its own weather.

We stand in a loose circle, the way people do around a fire without deciding to, and the circle has a shape it will keep all year: the July pit on the commons, the October pyre, the mother's stove, my hearth. One flame, many logs. I stand with my mother and Joan, and the boy brings more wood, and Bill tells the story of the winter his truck did not start and the fire in the barrel that saved his hands, and my mother says you've told that one, and Bill says it's still true, and that is the whole of our town's liturgy.

The pile burns down to coals by mid-afternoon, the red settling into the bed of ash. The boy has gone home for supper. The Reisers have gone in. The wind has picked up, and I am about to take the water can to the coals when my mother stops me with her hand.

"Not yet," she says. "Let it have its end."

She walks into the ring of stones, the fire low and honest, and she stamps the last sparks with her boot, one, two, three, each spark put out with a firmness that is not anger and not hurry. It is the small ritual of a woman who has been tending fires for sixty years and does not leave a fire to go out alone. I watch her. I will remember it, in September, with the whole weight of the year between.

"Now the water," she says, and hands me the can, and I do it, and the steam goes up like a second smoke, and the first fire of the town's year is over, and the mud season has truly begun.

Chapter 14: April: The Forge

April is a month of mud and last frosts, and the town's fires go indoors again, and I decide, the way you decide a thing you have been deciding for months, that I want fire tools of my own. Not my father's, because his hang over the hearth, their handles worn to the shape of his hands, and they are his the way the fireplace is his, and I cannot make a fire with them without his ghost at my shoulder. I want my own. I want a poker, a shovel, a set of tongs, made for my reach, weighted for my wrist.

Ada Hollis's forge is at the edge of town, down the road past the last house, in a building that used to be a barn. You can feel the heat before you reach the door; it reaches out through the walls and the gap under the door, a dry heat that has nothing to do with weather. I knock, and she says come in, and I go in.

The forge is not a romantic place. It is an order of tools and materials, each with its place, the way my father's woodpile has its place. The anvil stands near the fire, and the hammers hang on the wall above it in a row of descending weights, and the tongs hang by the fire, and the floor is swept. There is coal dust in the air, and the light is yellow and low, and the fire itself is the thing you notice last, because it is not what you expect.

A forge fire is not a hearth fire. It is a coal fire in a steel box, a deep bed of coke, and the air is forced through it from below by a blower, and the fire does not dance. It crouches. It is a small, concentrated sun, blue-white at its heart, and it is hot the way a room is hot, not the way a flame is hot. You can hold your hand over a hearth fire. You cannot hold your hand over this. The fire does not warm you. It makes things soft.

"You want tools," Ada says. She does not ask why. She has known, I think, from the moment I walked in and looked at the fire and not at her. "You want them heavy or light?"

I have never been asked this question. I say, I don't know.

She nods, as if that were the right answer. "Then you'll work with me a day and find out. Iron will tell you." She turns back to the anvil, where a piece of steel glows in the low yellow light, waiting. "Come tomorrow. Wear clothes you don't care about."

I walk home through the mud, and the heat of that crouching fire stays on my face like a second skin, and I think about my father's tools, blunt and old, and about the word Ada used, work, and I know that whatever the year of fires is becoming, this is the month it turns into something I will have to make.

Chapter 15: The Blacksmith

Ada Hollis is sixty-one, and her father's name is still over the door in letters that have lost their paint, and she has never seen a reason to take it down. She learned the trade in this shop, handed the hammer when she was tall enough to lift it, and the shop has not changed, she says, because it does not need to. A forge is a forge. Iron does not care what year it is.

She works me hard the first morning, which is what I came for. She shows me the fire's parts, the pot where the coal is banked, the blower, the tuyere where the air comes up through the bed, and she has me work the blower until I understand that the fire is a thing you must tend like any other, that it lives and dies by your attention. The iron goes in. You watch its color. You pull it at the color, not at a time.

"People think it's about time," she says, holding a bar of steel in the low yellow light. "It's not. It's about color. The fire tells you when. The iron tells you when. You just have to be looking."

She teaches me to read the steel the way you learn a language: the dull red of too cold, when the metal will not move and will only be stubborn and crack; the cherry of workable, when it bends like taffy; the orange, the yellow-white of too hot, when it will not hold an edge and will burn away like a candle. Heat is a language. I have been around fires all winter and I did not know until this month that they were speaking.

At the end of the day, my arms are full of small burns and my hands smell of coal, and I stand at the anvil with a piece of scrap and Ada shows me the first fire I strike on my own: the hammer coming down, the bright bloom of the stroke, the ring of the anvil, the scale falling off the iron like a skin. It is not my fireplace fire, which gives warmth and takes attention. It is not the stove's blue flame, which feeds the house. It is fire as a maker of things, and it is the first fire of the year that I am not tending so much as working with.

"What is fire for?" I ask her, standing in the heat, and it is a question I have been carrying since January without knowing it.

Ada does not answer. She takes the iron from the fire and lays it on the anvil and taps it once with the hammer, a tap that says watch this. Then she works, and the answer is in the working, and I am quiet and watch, and the iron, between us, begins to be a tool.

Chapter 16: The Iron

The tools take shape across the week, and I learn what Ada meant by iron telling you. The poker comes first, a bar of steel drawn out to a point, the hook turned at the end for the grate. I want it heavy, I find out, because a poker that weighs nothing is a poker you will lose in the fire. The shovel is a flat plate drawn and dished, and the tongs are the hardest thing I have made with my hands in years: two bars, a rivet, the jaws forged to meet exactly, and they will not meet exactly no matter how I try, because I am rushing.

"Slow down," Ada says. "You're in a hurry to be done. The iron's not in a hurry. The iron's just hot."

The scale is what I notice. Every time the iron comes out of the fire, the skin of it flakes off on the anvil, black, brittle, useless, the oxide that the fire burns away, and I sweep it into the tray and think about all the things that come off a thing when you heat it enough. It is a good thing to think about in April, when the ground is all scale and mud, and the whole town is flaking off its winter.

By Thursday I can read the colors without Ada telling me. I work at dull red and it fights me and I wait. I work at cherry and it gives. I work the tongs' jaws again, patient now, and they meet, and Ada comes over and looks at them and does not say anything, which is, from Ada, the highest praise. She has told me this. If I say it's good, it's good, she said. If I say nothing, it's better.

My mother calls on Friday night, and I tell her what I have been doing, and there is a pause on the line.

"Tongs," she says. "You're forging tongs."

"Ada's teaching me."

The pause again. Then she says, low and pleased: "You're like iron." It is her old name for my stubbornness, the thing she said when I was eight and would not leave the piano, when I was sixteen and would not be told, when I came home after my father died and would not fall apart in front of anyone. You're like iron. I have always heard it as a mild complaint, an accusation of hardness.

"I mean it kindly," she says now, and I hear the clock in her kitchen, and I hear the wood stove breathing, and I understand, for the first time, that it is not an accusation at all. Iron, heated and worked and tempered, is not hard the way a rock is hard. It is hard the way a tool is hard. It is useful. It holds an edge.

I take the tongs home and hang them by the hearth, beside my father's, and they are mine, and the year of fires has gained a tool.

Chapter 17: The Heat

The last lesson is the temper, and it is the one Ada makes me do alone, because it is the one, she says, that is all attention and no strength.

The quench first. The blade comes out of the fire yellow-white and goes into the oil, and the steam and the sizzle are the sound of the steel getting its hardness, its edge, its terrible brittleness. A quenched blade is hard and it will break. It will shatter on the first cold shock, and the old blacksmiths, Ada says, learned that the way you learn everything, by breaking things.

So the temper. You clean the scale off the steel until it shines, and you put it back in the fire, but a low fire, a gentle fire, and you watch the color travel up the metal the way color travels across the sky at dusk. Straw yellow first, and that is the temper for a wood tool, for something that will take blows. Then the peacock, the bronze and blue, for the spring steel that must bend and not break. Then the deep blue, for something soft, something that will take a lot and give nothing back.

"The right heat at the right time," Ada says. "That's the whole word. Temper. Not hard, not soft. Hard enough, soft enough, at the right moment. Everything else is just hitting iron."

I pay her on the last day, and she takes the money without counting it and puts it in her apron. I carry the tools home through the mud, and they are heavier than I thought, and the handles are ash, and they are the best things I own, and I hang them by the hearth where I planned.

My father's tools come down. I hold the old poker, worn to the shape of his hand, and I tell myself it does not hurt, and it is true, and it is also not true. I put them in the kindling box by the back door, where they will burn with the first fires of autumn, where a worn-out thing that has done its work can do its last work, warming the house one more time.

I do not know, in April, that this is the first burning of the year, the first thing I have chosen to give to the fire. I only know it is lighter than I thought, and that Ada's word, temper, is following me home like a smoke.

Chapter 18: May: The Burnout

The call comes at three in the afternoon, and I am at my desk, and the voice is the school secretary's, and she says Nora's name and then she says in her classroom and then she says they've taken her to the hospital, and I am already standing.

I drive to the hospital the way you drive to a hospital, which is too fast and not fast enough. Nora. Her classroom. The details come to me in the car, the way you fill in a picture you already know: the May heat, the last month of the school year, the seniors who need their letters, the stack of papers that never gets smaller. Nora, forty-three, who has taught fifteen years and has never once not answered a student's email before midnight.

The EMTs were gentle, the secretary said. She stood up to write something on the board and the room went sideways and she went down, not a faint, something quieter, the body just sitting down without asking permission. By the time I get to the emergency room she is in a curtained bay with an IV in her arm, and she looks at me over the thin blanket, and she says, with the humor I love and hate, "They're going to tell me I'm tired."

They do. The doctor is young and kind and says the word carefully, as if it might break something: exhaustion. The body's account, overdrawn. Nora has been living on the interest for years, and the principal is finally due, and the bank is the emergency room.

I sit with her until visiting hours end, and on the drive home I cannot stop turning the word over. Exhaustion. It is a fire word, though I have not put it together yet; the whole year has been circling it. A fire that has consumed its own fuel. A flame with no log under it, and when there is nothing left to burn, going out, not dramatically, just sitting down in the middle of the board without asking permission.

Nora. She is the one who showed up at my door ten years ago with a casserole, in the November of my father's dying, when I could not feed myself. She is the one who called every week after, on the same day, so that I would not have to remember to be remembered. She taught through the whole of it, and I did not once think about what was burning in her to keep me warm.

At home I stand at the hearth, cold since April, and I put my hand on the stone my father laid, and I think about the year's ledger. And I think about Nora's flame, which nobody kept.

I will take her food for a month. I will sit in that curtained bay and in her living room. I will learn, finally, that a fire has a fuel and the fuel is finite, and that there is no craft in the world that makes a log last longer than it lasts.

Chapter 19: The Friend

Nora is the kind of teacher the town tells stories about, and the stories are all true. The kid who could not read until she got him in October and by May he was reading out loud to the class, halting and proud. She grades through the night and she answers the emails at midnight and she stays late, always late, because there is always one more kid, one more paper, one more thing that cannot wait until tomorrow.

I met her the way you meet everyone in a town of two thousand, which is at the store and again at the church supper and again at someone's kitchen table until it becomes acquaintance and then, without ceremony, friendship. She was at my door in the November my father died with a casserole in her hands, and she did not say the terrible things people say. She said, "This is dinner. There's a card in the pocket with a number. Call it if you need to not talk." And she left. That is who she is. She does the thing and then she steps back.

In the hospital she is small in the bed in a way she is not small at the front of a classroom. The IV drips. She makes jokes, the humor intact, which I take as the best sign, the way you test a fire by its first small flame. She tells me the story of the fall, the board, the sitting-down, and she laughs, and the laugh is wrong, thin, a sound that has been used too hard.

"I'm just tired," she says. "That's all. They're making a production of it."

I think about her classroom. I have never seen it, but I can build it from her stories: the posters, the books, the desk that is not a desk but a citadel of papers. I think about the casserole, and the number in the pocket, and the weekly calls in the year I could not remember to be remembered, and I think about the chair.

Because I have been in that chair. Ten years ago, the chair was mine, the patient's family chair, the hard plastic one, the one you sit in for a month while someone you love is being taken apart slowly, by a disease with a name and a process. The roles were the same. The food appeared. The calls came. And now it is Nora in the bed and me in the chair, and the chair is the same shape, and I understand, sitting in it, that this is what a town is for. It is a room where the chairs get passed around, and everyone knows both chairs by heart.

I stay until they make me leave. On the way out I stop at her home desk, where she grades through the night, and I leave the casserole in the kitchen and the number on the counter, and I write, on the back of an envelope: This is dinner. Call it if you need to not talk.

She calls that night. She does not talk. I do not mind.

Chapter 20: The Exhaustion

What burnout is, in fire terms, is simple, and I cannot stop thinking about it. A flame without a log. The fire burns its fuel, and when the fuel is gone it burns its own structure, the carbon, the air, the last of itself, and what is left is not flame and not ember but the absence of both, a cold box full of nothing. Nora's box was her classroom. Mine is this house, and I stand in it now and inventory what I have been feeding the flame.

The year of fires. The essays, one a month, each one a small furnace I have stoked and tended and written down. The deadlines, which are mine, which I set for myself, which no one asked for. The record I began in January, every fire tended, every wood named, every temperature, and the way the record has grown teeth, has begun to demand its own feeding, the way any habit does.

I count my months the way I count my woodpile. January's oak, and the two houses' fires, and the walking back and forth. February's stove and the bread and the mother's soup. March's pruning and the smoke. April's forge, the tools, Ada's lessons, the work. And it is all good work, all of it, the best work, and I know, because I have been standing at the hearth for an hour now, that good work is still fuel. It burns the same.

The fear is not a thought. It arrives the way a draft arrives, around the edges of a door you thought was sealed. It says: you are Nora with a year's head start. You are the teacher who stays late, only your classroom is a desk and your students are pages and your grades-through-the-night are essays about fire. You have made a fire of your own attention and you have been feeding it yourself, gladly, for months, and you have mistaken the warmth for something that will last.

I go to the hearth and I lay no fire. I do not light one. It is May, and the house is warm enough, and I stand in the dark kitchen and eat bread and jam from the mother's jars, last summer's, the year before her, the jam that was made from the orchard's apples and set on the shelf for exactly this, and I do not write anything down. I do not make anything. I let the fire, for the first time in months, go out.

It is the hardest craft I have practiced all year, and no one teaches it, and Ada could not, and I do it badly, and I do it anyway. In the dark I can hear the house settling, the old timbers, my own breathing, the small and particular sound of a flame that has been given permission not to burn.

Chapter 21: The Rest

For a month I took Nora food, every Tuesday and Friday, and the food was the point and also not the point. She would meet me at her door in the same flannel shirt, her hair still damp at noon, and she would say the same thing: you don't have to keep doing this. And I would set the pot down on her counter and I would say nothing, because we both knew the truth of it, which is that I did have to, in the exact way a fire has to be fed.

The craft of rest is nothing Ada ever showed me. The forge keeps its fire going, always, hot enough to work iron at the end of the day; Ada banks it and I used to think she was greedy with it, keeping the heat alive overnight to save a match in the morning. But that is not what the banked fire is. The banked fire is rest with its eyes open. What Nora had done was let her fire go out completely, and the scorch mark it left was her body.

I learned that month what I should have known in May: a fire does not care how well you fed it yesterday. A fire is only what is burning now. Nora's classroom stayed dark for three weeks; the school found a sub who could not find the light switch, and somehow it was Nora who apologized, from her own couch, in a voice I had to lean in to hear.

I sat with her a lot. We did not talk about burnout, because talking about it felt like poking at the ash. We talked about her mother's hoya plant, which she had finally gotten to bloom, eleven years of patience, and about the student who had written her a note that she kept in her pocket, and about nothing, for long stretches, the way you sit with a fire that needs to rest. I made her soup because it is the one thing I know how to make that does not need to be watched.

And then, one evening in the first week of June, I went home and I did not lay a fire. I left the hearth cold and the kindling box full and I sat in the dark kitchen with a plate of bread and the last of my mother's jam, and I ate, and I made nothing. The night air came in the open window, cool and useful. It is the only night all year I did not strike a match, and I have it in my book of fires, underlined.

Chapter 22: June: The Forest-Fire News

The dry June came in like a held breath. The town's lawns went from green to the color of straw in two weeks, and the hill farms stopped cutting hay because there was nothing left to cut, and Del Proulx put up the same sign he put up every dry year, the town's single acknowledgment that the hills could burn: a red rectangle on the station door with the word extreme, hand-lettered, all caps.

The television ran the western fires every night. I watched them while I worked, the way other people listen to the radio — a fire a thousand miles away, in country I had never seen, taking houses I would never visit. The footage was the same every year and I could not stop watching it: the columns of smoke going up white and then black, the slurry planes crossing the frame, the houses, always the houses, the ones that survived standing alone in a moonscape. Fire at scale is a different animal. I know my hearth, my stove, my March bonfire; I can tell you what a fire will do in a room I have lived in for twenty years. I cannot tell you what a fire will do to a hillside of dry timber in a wind. Nobody can. That is what the footage means, the same awed voice in every anchor.

The language of the news became a vocabulary I collected without meaning to. Containment. Structures lost. The fire made a run. A run — as if the fire were an animal with intentions, and in a way it is, except the intention is only this: to eat what is dry, in the direction the wind points. I wrote the words in my notebook, between the recipes and the fire counts. I thought I was collecting material for the book. I was learning a language I would need, and the knowledge sat in me like a log laid across the grate, not yet lit.

The maps were the worst part, and I loved them. The incident maps online, the orange perimeters traced over the green of the forest service, the little crosses marking structure losses, the names of the fires like the names of towns: the same three names appearing in a rotation, year after year, as if the country had a fixed number of fires it was always re-burning.

I kept my own map in my head, the way the town keeps its fire map at the station, Del's doing, a big board of Merrow and the hills around it, the houses marked, the water sources, the fire roads. Del pins nothing on it in June except the red rectangle. But I started to see the town through the map, the way the fire crews see it: where the water is, where the road narrows, which roofs would catch first. I traced the roads with my finger, the way I traced the perimeters on the screen. It is another kind of keeping, mapping what you love.

Chapter 23: The Maps

I spent too much of June with my finger on the screen, tracing the orange lines. My mother called it worrying, and she was right, and I could not stop. The maps are seductive because they are clean. The fires on them are smooth shapes, contained in a way the real fire never is; a perimeter is just a line the fire has crossed and will cross again. I knew this. I knew the map was a lie the way a photograph of a fire is a lie, still where the fire is moving. But I kept tracing.

The map of the town at the station was different, and I went and stood in front of it for a long time one afternoon, Del leaning against the doorframe, letting me look. It is a real map, hand-annotated, the pencil marks of forty years of fire chiefs. The water sources marked in blue, the dead-end roads, the names of the families inked in beside their houses. Reiser, in March's handwriting. My mother's house, her name in the neat schoolteacher hand I knew from every note she ever pinned to my door. My father's house — no, mine now, my name over his, his pencil still faint underneath.

I asked Del if the map scared him, and he looked at me a long time. "The map is the calm part," he said. "The map is what you have when you're not scared. When it's coming at you, you're not looking at the map. You're looking at the fire." He tapped the glass. "You keep the map for the looking that happens later."

I stood there with my hand on the frame and I understood, or began to understand, that I was not afraid of the western fires at all. A thousand miles is a thousand miles; the fire there was not coming for my woodpile. I was practicing. I was learning the posture of attention, the habit of keeping my eye on the thing that could take what I loved, the way you practice a fire drill in a school you love. I had a year of fires behind me, a book taking shape in my notebooks, and a mother three streets away who was fine, who was fine, who told me on the phone every night that she was fine and tending her tomatoes.

I went home and I drew the town's map from memory in my journal, badly, the way you draw something you love. I marked my mother's house. I marked my own. I drew the water source at the edge of the orchard, the pond my father cleaned out one summer when I was small. I did not know why I was drawing it. I was drawing it because I was scared, and the map was the calm part, and the calm part is where you live.

Chapter 24: The Evacuations

The interviews were the worst of it, and I could not stop watching those either. The evacuation centers, the folding chairs, the people in clothes they had grabbed, telling the camera what they had carried out of their houses. Papers. Photographs. The dog. The cat, in a carrier. My grandmother's rings. The hard drive. The thing they always said, over and over, in every accent, was the same: we didn't think we'd be gone long, and we grabbed the wrong things. Or: we had time, and we took the right things, and we can't believe how little there was.

I made my own list, the way I make all my lists, and it was short, and I was ashamed of it. My notebooks, the fire book with the year in it. My father's photograph. The cup Maren fired — no, that came later, in August, but the cup sits in the list anyway, because a list you write in June knows what the year will hand you. The box of my mother's jam, the whole shelf of it, the shelf I looked at every morning in my kitchen. That was the list. Four things. I had lived in this house for twenty years and the whole of it, the furniture, the books, the records, the things my father built, came down to four things and one of them was jam.

I sat with the shortness of the list for a long time, the way you sit with an unflattering photograph. And then I understood something that did not comfort me and did not need to: the list was short because the keeping had already happened. What I would carry was not the house. The house is not what I love; the house is the skin of what I love. What I love is my mother, and she is three streets away and not in any list, and my father is in my hands and in the fireplace and in every fire I have laid this year, and you cannot evacuate those. You carry them in your walking.

I did not say this to anyone. I said it to my notebook, where I was learning to say the true things first and the pretty things after, if at all. The news went on burning the same fires. But I noticed that the smoke was reaching us now, even a thousand miles from the burn — a haze in the evening sun, the sky the color of weak tea, the moon coming up red some nights.

I called my mother. "Are you watching this?" she said. She was watering her tomatoes with the hose, the water going to waste on the dry ground, and she did not care. "It's terrible," she said, "all those people," and the hose went on running, and I heard in her voice that she had made a list too, and that hers was short, and that she was not ashamed of it.

Chapter 25: The Distant Smoke

The smoke reached us. That is the fact of the week, the fact I keep coming back to. A thousand miles of fire and the smoke found the hills of Merrow, hazed the sun in the evenings, turned the moon red, put the smell of a distant burning into the air we were breathing. My mother said the sky looked like it did after the hurricane, the year of the storm, when everything was lit wrong for days. She was right. It was the same wrong light.

I called her less than I wanted to and more than she would admit to wanting. She was fine. She was always fine. She told me about the tomatoes, which were curling in the heat, and about the Reisers' new puppy, and about the man on the news who had lost his house and cried on camera, and what did I think of that, and I said I thought the smoke had got in his eyes, and she laughed her short laugh and said the smoke had got in everybody's eyes this month. Then she said, in the voice she used when she was done talking, "Stop worrying about me. I'm tending my garden. Go tend yours."

So I tended mine. I went out to the woodpile by the back door, the oak and the maple and the ash, the stack my father taught me to build, and I stood in front of it for a long time in the wrong light. Everything dry is waiting. That is what I thought, and I could not unthink it. The woodpile was waiting. The orchard was waiting. The town, under its red rectangle, was waiting. And I stood there with my hand on a piece of dry oak and I felt, for the first time all year, the fear that the news had been trying to teach me, which is not the fear of a fire you can see but the fear of the dryness that precedes it. The fear of the waiting itself.

I went in and I laid a small fire in the hearth, even though the evening was warm, even though I did not need it, and I watched it burn. I told myself it was a practice fire, a keeping of the hand in shape. I told myself a lot of things that June. The smoke moved east across the sky all week, and the sun set wrong, and I kept the small fire going, and I kept my mother's voice on the phone, and I waited, like everything dry, for whatever was coming.

Chapter 26: July: The Barbecue

On the Fourth the whole town showed up in the smoke of the pit, and I thought, watching them come down the hill, that this was what a year of fires had been pointing at all along. Del Proulx dug the pit on the commons at dawn, six feet long and two deep, and lined it with the stones he kept in a pile behind the station for this one purpose, and by the time the sun was up the fire was lit and the pit was its own small weather system, its heat a shimmer the whole morning stood behind.

I worked the pit with Del. That was my job, the second chair at the fire, and I want to say it plainly: there is nothing in the craft of fire harder to learn, and nothing worth learning more, than the low and slow of a pit. The hearth wants a flame; the pit wants a coal bed with a memory. Del taught me the rub — salt and pepper, a little sugar, nothing fancy, and the patience to leave it alone — and he taught me the smoke ring, which is not a trick but a consequence, the pink line that forms where the smoke has done its work and the heat has not, and he taught me that a man who opens the pit every ten minutes to look at the meat is a man who will be eating his barbecue at dusk, if at all.

"Low and slow," Del said, and I felt the phrase settle into me like a coal into ash. Low and slow. It is the temper again, the right heat applied for the right time, the word Ada uses for iron, only here it is the word for a town feeding itself. I thought about the difference between a fire that makes a thing fast and a fire that makes a thing right. I thought about my mother's jam. I thought about the year.

The smoke went up in a column the whole town could read, and people came to it the way they come to the March bonfire, only more of them, in their Fourth of July clothes, carrying folding tables and watermelon and Marie Vo's pies, which are the town's real reason for gathering. Nora came, recovered, her color back, laughing at the pit's smoke, and she stood beside me for a while and did not mention that it had been her fire that went out, only stood there in the smoke like everyone else, letting the fire do the talking. The whole town in the smoke. I had come home to write, and here was what I was writing about, all of it, standing in the smoke of a single fire.

Chapter 27: The Town

The folding tables ran the length of the commons, end to end, the whole town's contribution set out in a line you could walk the length of Merrow along: the watermelon, cut into wedges that went pink at the heart, the coleslaw in the good bowls, the beans nobody remembered bringing, Marie Vo's pies set out at the center table like a row of lit windows. The town eats together twice a year, at this table and at the bonfire festival, and the rest of the year we are neighbors, which is a different and smaller thing.

I stood at the edge of it with a paper plate and watched. The town as a single fire — that is the image I keep trying to make true, and it is true. One flame, many logs. There was Del at the pit, and the Reisers, Joan and Bill, at the folding chairs with the watermelons, and the boy from March's bonfire, taller, chasing the other children between the tables. There was Marie, watching her pies the way Ada watches her iron, exact and possessive. There was Nora, eating, laughing, alive. And there was my mother, who had come down the hill in her good blouse, carrying a basket of the last of her peaches, and who was sitting in the shade at the far table, talking to the woman from the library with her hands moving in the air the way they always did when she was telling a story.

I came home to write, and the book I am writing has filled up with these people. I did not plan that. I planned a book about the craft of fire, twelve essays, one per month, a neat year. But the fires have people in them. Every fire in this book was tended by someone, and the someone was usually my mother, or my father, or the whole town together, or me trying to be worthy of the ones who came before. I stood there with the smoke in my hair and I understood that the book had stopped being a book about fire and had become a book about the people who keep the fires, and that this was the same book, and I was not going to be able to untangle it, and I did not want to.

Nora came up beside me and put her hand on my arm and nodded at the crowd. "Look at them," she said. "It's just one fire." And I said, "It's the only fire," and she laughed, and I ate my corn and watched the town in its smoke, and I was happy, in the way you are happy at a fire, which is a warmth you do not have to earn.

Chapter 28: The Char

The corn went into the coals in its husks, the silk pulled back and tied, the whole ear turned to the low and patient heat of the pit's edge, and I stood at the fire with the other cooks and watched the husks blacken. Char is the thing I want to say about that day, and I have been turning it over since. The corn comes out of the coals black as a shoe, and you would swear it is ruined, and you pull the husk back and the silk comes away in one piece and there is the corn underneath, steamed and sweet, perfect, saved.

Char is the black crust that protects what it covers. It is what the meat gets in the pit, the crust that holds the moisture in while the low heat works, hour on hour, through to the bone. It is what a log gets when it burns, the black scale that insulates the coal inside, and you can tap the char off a log and find the heat still living under it, patient. I stood in the smoke and read my own life in it, because that is what this year has made me, a woman who reads her life in fire. The routine of the year — the notes, the essays, the small ceremonies of the hearth — is char. The hard black outside that people mistake for the whole thing, the crust you burn on the outside to save the inside.

Nora was the thing that proved it for me. She stood at the pit with a corn in her hand, laughing, the smoke making her squint, and I remembered her in May on her couch, in the same flannel shirt, her voice thin. The black year had kept her. The crust had held while the inside cooked. She turned the corn and it was done and she handed it to me without being asked, and I thought: this is what a friend is, the char on the outside of your life, the thing that burns instead of you.

My mother came up with her plate, and I put a corn on it, and she said it was too much, and I said it was not, and she looked at me with her eyes the color of the smoke and said, "I'm just tired, it's the heat." And I let it pass. I let it pass because the day was passing, because the pit was going, because everyone was standing in the good smoke of the one fire, and because I did not yet know, could not have known, that she was telling me the truth and I was hearing the wrong word. I let it pass. The char of it, the black crust of the day, is the thing I have kept, and it has protected me, and I hate it a little, and I would not let it go.

Chapter 29: The Corn

The corn in the coals is the taste of the year, I think now, writing this in the cool of my kitchen. The black husk, the silk coming away in one piece, the kernels the color of butter underneath, the first bite and the steam rising off it, the smoke in it. My mother's portion sat on her plate and she barely touched it. She moved the corn, she cut off a few rows of kernels with the side of her fork, she ate them slowly, and she pushed the plate away, and when I looked at her she was looking out across the commons at the fireworks scaffolding on the hill, and I thought she was tired, and I let it pass.

The fireworks went up over the commons when the light went, everyone's faces lifted, the red and the blue and the gold falling over the town, and the whole time I kept one eye on her, the way you keep one eye on a fire you are not sure of. She watched the fireworks with her hands folded in her lap, and when the last one went up, the big one, the one that fills the sky, she put her hand on my wrist, and her hand was very thin, and she said, "I'm just tired. It's the heat." And I said, "You need to eat," and she said, "I ate. You worry too much. It's the heat, that's all." And I let it pass, because she had told me the same thing a hundred times and it had always been true before.

We walked home up the hill in the dark, the smoke of the pit still in our clothes, and she walked slower than she had at the March bonfire, and she leaned on my arm at the top of the hill and said nothing, and I felt the heat of her through her good blouse, and I thought it was the July night, the close warm air of the month that holds its heat like a banked fire. At her door she kissed my cheek and said good night and went in, and the porch light went on and then off, and I stood in the street a long time looking at the dark of her kitchen window, and the smell of the corn in the coals was still on me, and I said to the empty street, to the night, to the smoke: it's the heat.

I have kept that walk. I have kept her hand on my wrist. I have kept the porch light going on and off. I have kept the words, it's the heat, and I have re-read them a thousand times since, in the book of the year I did not know I was writing, and every time I read them I know: I let it pass. I was tending the fire and I did not see the log going soft at the heart. The corn in the coals is the taste of the year, and the taste is smoke.

Chapter 30: August: The Kiln

The diagnosis came in late July, in a room the color of weak tea, and it took the summer out of me. I do not remember the drive home. I remember my mother in the passenger seat looking out the window at the dry fields, and I remember that she was the one who said it first, to the window, not to me: "Well." That was all. That was the word. And then she said, "Don't look at me like that. It's just something to do, all this." And I could not speak, and she reached across and put her hand on my knee, and her hand was the thin hand from the fireworks, and I understood, in the car, that she had known before I did. That she had been tending it alone, the way she tended everything.

By August I was at Maren's studio because I could not sit still, and Maren, who is quiet and exact and who does not ask, put clay in my hands. She has the kiln shed behind the studio, the wood-fired kiln, the brick and the steel, two days of firing, and she said, "The wheel's in the back. You don't have to say anything. Just come here and push clay around until your hands forget." And I pushed clay around, and my hands forgot, and that was the first relief of the summer, the honest weight of the wet clay, the smell of it, the way it gave under my hands and took what I gave it.

She taught me that the potter makes the pot but the fire finishes it. That is her whole philosophy, and she said it the way she says everything, as if it were obvious, a fact of the world like gravity. The kiln decides. You can throw a pot that is true, you can glaze it right, you can pack it with your whole heart, and the fire, in its two days, in its own time, will make it a pot or ruin it, and you will not know which until it cools. The craft of surrender, she called it, and I thought about the surrender and I could not do it, and I kept throwing bowls, ugly, honest, my thumbs making the same mistake over and over.

I decided, in August, without deciding, to make a cup for my mother. A cup she could hold, the weight right, the rim the shape of her hands, the size of her palm, the way she held her coffee on the porch in the morning with her eyes closed. I threw it a dozen times before I got one that felt like her. Maren watched me, and when I finally held one up, she nodded once and said, "That one's for somebody," and I said yes, and she said, "Good. Then we'll fire it together. I'll show you the fire you can't tend by yourself." And that was the sentence that got me through August: the fire you can't tend by yourself. She said I could fire it with hers, in the two days, and that it would come out the color of whatever the fire decided, and that I would put it in my mother's hands, and I would let the fire finish it.

Chapter 31: The Potter

The kiln shed at the back of Maren's studio is a low building of brick that smells of ash and cold stone, and in the weeks after the diagnosis I cannot sit still anywhere else. The room the doctor used was the color of weak tea. I have been measuring it since: the walls, the light, the length of time between the words arriving and the words stopping. Maren does not ask me anything. She puts clay in my hands the way you hand a person a broom when you see them standing in the middle of a room with nothing to do.

The clay is cold and heavy. It smells of the ground. She shows me the wedge, the push, the fold, the push, and my hands learn it before my mind agrees to it. The wheel is a motor she built herself from a washing-machine drum, and it hums under my palms like something asleep.

The kiln shed is the real lesson. It is wood-fired, brick, built by her grandfather, and she fires it twice a year, two days of feeding the fire until the cones in the peephole bend like men bowing. The fire is the last and greatest tool, she says. The potter makes the pot, but the fire finishes it. She says this the way Ada says iron wants to be worked hot, not as a motto but as a fact of the material, the way you say water runs downhill.

My first bowls are ugly. They slump, they crack, the rims wobble. Maren fires them anyway. She picks up the worst one, a thing with no right to call itself a vessel, and she turns it in her hands. That one is honest, she says. I cannot tell if she is joking. She is not joking.

I decide, in the middle of a Thursday, throwing a cup that keeps collapsing, to make a vessel for my mother. I keep calling it a bowl, but what I am making is a cup. A cup she can hold. The weight right. The wall thick enough to keep the tea hot. The glaze rough enough to grip. Ruth's hands are not what they were. I want something in them that does not ask for strength.

The cup collapses three times before it stands. Maren watches and says nothing. Keep it, she says. You'll fire it ugly first. The clay remembers what it was; I am learning to be patient with that, and to be patient with what it will become. Somewhere in the cold, dark kiln shed, the fire that will finish my mother's cup is waiting to be lit.

Chapter 32: The Glaze

The glaze room is a corner of the studio Maren calls the mess. There are buckets labeled in her neat hand, wood ash, soda ash, feldspar, whiting, silica, and a mortar where she grinds things down to powder. The dust tastes of metal if you get it on your lips.

The ash glaze is the one she teaches me first, because it is the one that comes from her own fireplace. She rakes the ash after the winter fires, sifts it, washes it, and mixes it with clay and silica until it is the color of a wet stone. Everything the fire burned is in here, she says. The oak, the maple, the junk mail. The glaze is the fire remembering.

I mix mine in the same mortar. Wood ash from my own fireplace, the hearth my father built, the fires of January and March and April. I grind it fine. I add the clay, the silica, the water, and stir until it is the consistency of cream. The cup sits on the shelf, bisque-fired, pale as bone, and I know what I am about to coat it in. I am about to coat it in the winter.

Maren tests a brush of it on a broken handle. The glaze goes on gray and dull. You can't tell anything until the fire, she says. The fire resolves it. That is the word, resolves. The glaze is a question the fire answers.

I dip the cup. The glaze covers it like a skin, and I think about what is covering what. The year's fires, the hearth, the stove, the bonfire, the forge, are going onto this cup in the literal form of their ash, and the cup is going to my mother, who is not well, who has not said the word cancer and does not need to. I am covering a cup in the year, and I am covering the year in something I can hand her.

The ash glaze, Maren says, will come out a gray-green, the color of dusk, or it will come out brown, or it will crawl and bead and fail. The fire decides. I write my mother's name on the bottom of the cup in pencil, where the glaze will burn it away or keep it.

The cup goes into the kiln on a Friday. Maren says the firing will take two days. I mark the days on the calendar like a prisoner marking a wall.

Chapter 33: The Firing

We fire the kiln on a Friday, and for two days the fire is the whole of the work. Maren stacks the wood, dry pine to come up fast, oak for the long climb, and I learn her rhythm: feed, wait, listen, feed. The kiln breathes. It has a sound, a low draw, like a person drinking. The cones sit in the peephole, little towers of clay, and Maren tells me to watch them. When they bend, we're close. Bending is what they do at temperature, the way a knee bends. I have never watched a thing make up its mind more slowly.

We take shifts through the night. I sleep two hours on the cot in the studio and wake to the sound of Maren's shovel, and I go out into the dark with the fire lighting the yard, and we feed it together. At three in the morning she says, the fire is not the enemy. The fire is the only thing that knows how to do this. I believe her because I have nothing else to believe.

The same week, my mother's treatment begins. I drive her on the first morning. She holds the little plastic bag with her things in it and looks out the window at the town going past and says, well, we'll see. We'll see. It is the sentence of the whole week.

The kiln cools for three days. You cannot rush it, the cups would crack from the shock, the glaze would craze. Maren says the opening is the part she never gets used to, the moment of not knowing. I understand the not knowing. I am living in it.

On the day the kiln opens, we take the brick from the door and the heat comes out like a held breath. Maren reaches in with her tongs and brings out the cups one by one. Mine is last. It is the color of dusk, gray-green, deep, the color the sky goes before the light really leaves. The glaze has run in one place and gathered, and where it pooled it is almost black. Maren turns it in her hands. That's a good one, she says. I have never heard her say that about anything.

I take it to the hospital that night. Ruth is awake. She holds it in both hands, and she turns it, and she says, this is heavy enough. That is all. I have kept it, in the days since, as the best sentence anyone has ever said about anything I made.

Burned: the two days of wood. Kept: the cup, and the sentence. Risen: the color of dusk.

Chapter 34: September: The Candle

The candle is a simple one, bought at the drugstore in town, and in September it burns on the bedside table in my mother's room while the weather turns and the first cool nights come in through the window she asked to keep open.

She came home from the hospital on a Thursday. Carol the hospice nurse has set the room up, the hospital bed in the front room where the light is best, the pills in a basket, the basin, the quiet. Ruth is in charge of the window, and the window is open. I want the air, she says. The air smells of apples, the orchard across the street, the year's fruit beginning to turn.

The candle is Carol's idea, or the town's, or mine. I no longer remember which of us bought it. It is a white candle in a plain glass jar. The thing about a candle is that it asks for nothing but attendance. A fire needs wood and draft and a hand that knows the lay. A candle needs only the wick trimmed and the flame watched. It is the small flame, the kept flame, the one that does not ask for wood, only for company.

I learn the craft of it the way I learned the others, with my hands. The wick wants trimming, a long wick smokes and gutters, and the flame ducks and dances in a way that is not good. I learn to trim it with the scissors Carol left, to keep the melted wax from drowning the flame, to set it where the draft from the window does not reach it. There is a trick to lighting a candle that has been burning a long time. You bring the match to the wick from below, slowly, and the flame takes, and the light settles like something that was always there.

The first cool night, my mother sleeps and I sit with the candle and learn what a candle is for. A fire throws heat. A candle throws light. In the room where my mother is dying, the heat is in the blankets and the light is this small flame, and I think, plainly, the way you note the weather: grief gives both, in the wrong proportions. Too much light. Not enough heat. Or the other way. I cannot tell which, and it does not matter, because both are here.

The candle burns down a little each night. Carol says it is fine to keep it burning, that a flame in a room where someone is dying is not superstition, it is company. The wick makes a small sound, a kind of hiss, when it burns well. My mother wakes in the night and says, is it still burning? and I say yes, and she goes back to sleep.

The first cool nights. The window stays open. The candle stays lit. There is nothing else to do in September but attend to the flame and to the woman, and both of them are burning slowly, and I am learning to watch.

Chapter 35: The Mother

I am writing my mother down while she is still alive to be known, the way you bank a fire for the night. You do not wait until the flame is gone to arrange the coals.

Ruth is seventy-four, a retired schoolteacher, and she kept her fifth-grade class in line with an eyebrow. The eyebrow is a real thing. I have seen it quell a substitute, a school board, a teenage daughter, a delivery man. It is not a frown. It is the whole woman lowering a shade over half her face, and children who met it once remembered it for years. She taught in Merrow for thirty years, and there is no one in this town of two thousand who did not pass through her room or through the rumor of it.

She planted the orchard the year I was born. Thirty years of apples, and she knows each tree by name and by trouble, the Macoun that sulks, the Liberty that pours, the Gala that thinks it is a Liberty. She cans jam in August in a kitchen hot as a sauna, the jars lined up on the counter like a small army, and she gives them away the way other people give handshakes. My father used to say the town ran on Ruth's jam and the rumor mill, and both were equally reliable.

She is short with fools. She calls things what they are. When the doctor gave the diagnosis in the room the color of weak tea, she sat through it with her hands folded, and when he was done she asked him one question, how long, and when he answered she said, well, then, we'll have to be busy. And she has been busy. She has reorganized her recipes. She has told me where the deed is. She has argued with me about soup, and the argument was a good one, because it meant she was still herself.

She is a woman of lists and soups and weather. She reads the frost warnings the way other people read the news. She calls me in March to tell me the robins are early. She knows the name of every child who ever sat in her room and what became of them, and she remembers what they were afraid of.

In July, at the barbecue, her hand on my wrist. I'm just tired, it's the heat. I let it pass. I let it pass because that is what you do with your mother's small lies, you let them pass because they are kind, and because you do not yet know they are lies.

In September she is thin, and the eyebrow is still there. I am writing her down the way you bank a fire, while the coals are still warm, while the shape of the whole thing is still visible in the dark. I want the shape right. I want to have not missed her while I still have her, the way I missed my father, the way you always do.

The orchard across the street is beginning to turn. She asked me to keep the window open. She is alive. I am taking notes.

Chapter 36: The Dying

The dying is a slow burn, long flat stretches where nothing appears to change, and then the small shifts that are the whole story. The breaths change first, becoming slower, with a gap between them, and I learn to listen for the next one the way you listen for the pop of a log settling. Carol teaches me what to watch for: the hands, the feet, the circulation leaving the edges of her. My mother's feet are cold, and I cannot fix it, and I hold them anyway.

The candle burns down. We are into the third candle now, and Carol keeps a replacement ready before the flame reaches the glass. The wick smokes a little at the end; I have learned to trim it with my thumbnail, standing in the dark room in the small hours.

The days have a shape. Morning, the pills. Noon, the broth she does not eat. Afternoon, the visits, the Reisers, Ada, Nora, Maren, Del in his truck outside, not coming in. The town does not knock anymore; it leaves food on the porch. The kitchen fills with casseroles and the freezer fills with soup, and I eat what I can and give the rest away, and the giving away is also a keeping.

The last sentences. They are not about anything large. On a Tuesday she looks at me and says, the tomatoes. I do not understand. She says it again. The tomatoes are going to be done. Somebody should pick them. I say I will pick them, and she is satisfied. That is the last conversation we have, and it is about tomatoes, and I am grateful, because it is the truest thing. My mother was a woman who could not die while the tomatoes were still on the vine.

The night she dies, I am holding her hand. Carol is there, the candle burning, the window open, the room cool, and the breath that was slow gets slower, and then it stops, and there is a silence different from every other silence, and I sit with my hand on her hand and I do not let go, because there is no reason to let go, and there is no other tense for this. It is happening. It is happening now. It is happening to her hand under my hand, and the present tense is not a trick, it is the only thing that does not look away.

The candle burns on. Carol closes my mother's eyes and says, she's gone, sweetheart. I do not move. I watch the candle, burned to its last inch, the flame steady. It does not know anything has happened. The heat of it reaches the small space between us, and I think: grief gives light and heat in the wrong proportions, and here is the light, here is the heat, all of it, all at once, too much and not enough.

The clock on the wall, which my mother wound every night for forty years, has run down. I sit with her until the first gray of the morning comes through the open window, then I get up and go out to the porch, and the air smells of apples, and the world is still there, which is the first and last surprise.

Chapter 37: The Mourning

The week after is a series of small tasks, and I am grateful for each one, because each one has an order. The phone calls. The obituary, which I write in the kitchen of my mother's house, at her table, using her pen, and I get it right the first time, which I take as a sign. The funeral home, the coffin, the questions about the flowers. No flowers, I say. She would have hated the fuss. The man writes it down without looking at me, and I think he has heard that before.

The funeral is on a Saturday, and the church is full. Merrow is a town of two thousand, and it seems to me that all of them are there. The Reisers in the front. Ada in her good coat. Nora holding my arm. Maren. Del in his uniform, standing at the back with his cap in his hands. The town is a single fire, one flame, many logs, and here is the flame, gathered for my mother, who taught half of them in fifth grade and kept the other half in jam.

I do not cry at the funeral. I hold it, the way you hold an ember before you know it is hot. I read the obituary aloud and my voice does not shake, and I think my mother would have been proud of the voice and annoyed at the restraint, and the thought is so exactly her that I almost laugh, which is worse than crying.

The house. I walk through it in the days after, touching things. The stove, cold since spring. The soup pot, empty, hanging on its hook. The jam jars on the shelf, a few left from last summer. The stack of seed catalogs by the chair. The calendar, where she has written the appointments I made for her, in her hand. The last handwriting of hers in the world.

On the Tuesday after the funeral I am alone in her kitchen, and I am hungry, and there is nothing in the house but the freezer, and I open it, and it is full of her soup, quart jars, labeled in her hand. Minestrone, October. Beef, February. I take out a jar and heat it on her stove and eat it at her table, and it tastes the way the year tastes. It tastes like her. It tastes like being fed. And I sit in her kitchen and I eat her soup and I cry, finally, properly, with my elbows on her table and the empty bowl in front of me.

The town leaves the food on the porch for a while longer, and then it stops, as it should. The days get shorter. The stove stays cold. I do not know yet that I will light it.

Chapter 38: The Flame, Kept

The stove in my mother's kitchen has been cold since spring, and I have been coming to the house every day because the house needs someone, and the someone is me. I clean what needs cleaning and throw out what needs throwing out. I leave her chair where it is. The house is a body I am learning to keep, the way the chimney sweep keeps the chimney. The keeping is the work, and the work is the love, and I am learning this late.

The kindling is in the box by the stove, where she kept it. Pine splints she split herself, dried on the shelf above the stove, tied in bundles with twine. I open the box and it smells of her, of wood and cold and the small dry dust of summer. I have not opened this box since the spring.

I lay the fire the way my father taught me, the way she watched me lay it at her stove a hundred times. The newspaper, crumpled, not too tight. The kindling, split-side toward the flame. The two small oak logs from her pile. I check the damper, I open the flue, and I can feel the draw, the chimney pulling, the house breathing in, the way the house has been holding its breath all summer.

I light it. The match, the paper, the small blue-and-gold that catches in the kindling and climbs, the fire takes, the stove ticks as the iron warms, the kitchen begins to warm, and I stand in my mother's kitchen with my mother's fire burning in her stove, and this is the moment the whole year turns.

Burning to keeping. All year I have written about burning, the hearth, the stove, the bonfire, the forge, the kiln. Fire has been the subject, and I have been careful with it, the way you are careful with a tool. And now I understand the thing the whole year has been trying to teach me. Grief is a slow burn, and a slow burn must be fed, or it dies. You have to tend it, bring it wood, sit with it in the small hours and watch it, the way I watched the candle and the cone in the peephole. Grief is a fire, and a fire is not a feeling, it is a craft. It is something you keep.

I sit in the chair by the stove, her chair, and I let the heat come up around me, and I make a decision. The year of fires, the record I began in January, the book about the craft of fire I have been writing all year, is not a book about fire. It was never about fire. It is about this: what grief keeps burning, the ember under the ash that is still alive, that must be fed, that must be kept.

The cup from the kiln sits on the windowsill, empty, the color of dusk. I bank the fire before I leave, the way she taught me, the coals raked, the ash drawn over, the damper set. The stove will be warm when I come back in the morning. I will feed it, and I will keep it, and I will write it down.

Kept: the fire. Kept: the house. Kept: her.

Chapter 39: October: The Bonfire Festival

The bonfire festival is the last Saturday of October, and the pyre has been growing on the commons all month, and I am on the committee because keeping busy is keeping warm. It is a true sentence. I learned it in September, standing in my mother's kitchen, and I have been practicing it ever since.

The pyre is a town project. The whole town brings its brush and its broken furniture and its dead leaves and its griefs, and it lays them on the pile, and the pile grows until it is higher than a man, and the committee keeps the shape of it. Because there is a shape to it, and the shape matters. Del Proulx, who runs the committee, has been building bonfires since he was a boy, and he is the one who teaches the architecture. A fire needs two things, he says: air and a heart. The brush goes in layers, crossed, so the air can get in. The heart is the middle, the dry kindling, the place where the first match will land. You build a fire the way you build a house, the frame, the drafts, the heart, and then you light it and let it do what it was built for.

I like the committee. I like the work of the hands, the dragging of the branches, the stacking of the broken chairs, the arguments about the shape. The arguments are good arguments, the way the soup argument was a good argument. The town argues about the pyre the way it argues about everything, and the arguing is the togetherness, and the togetherness is the warmth.

The effigy committee meets on the same nights. They are building the straw man in the barn behind the firehouse, a figure of a man, stuffed with straw, wearing an old coat. Del says the town has burned an effigy on the commons since anyone can remember, and what it means changes every year, and that is the point of it. What we burn in effigy. The year's losses. The winter ahead. The grievances.

I walk past the barn and I see the straw man leaning against the wall, unfinished, and I stand in the doorway for a long time, and then I go home, and I get the cardboard box from the back of my closet, the hospital papers, the bracelets, the schedules, the whole record of the dying, and I bring it back to the barn and I put it with the things the town is burning, and I do not say anything about it, and I do not have to.

The pyre stands on the commons, waiting for the last Saturday. The leaves come down. The frost is on the grass in the mornings. I am on the committee. I am keeping busy. I am keeping warm.

Chapter 40: The Effigy

The straw man is finished on the morning of the festival, and the effigy committee is proud of him. He has a coat from the charity shop and a hat that used to belong to someone's uncle, and he stands against the barn door with his straw arms at his sides, and the whole town looks at him and the whole town sees something different.

What we burn in effigy. Del says it changes every year, and I believe him. Some years it is the weather. Some years it is the school budget. Some years it is the year itself, the whole gone year, everything we could not say out loud, all of it stuffed into a straw man and sent up the chimney of the sky. The grievances. The winter ahead. The losses.

My cardboard box is with the brush and the broken furniture, at the heart of the pile where the fire will find it first, and I have not looked inside it since I put it there, and I do not look now. I know what is in it. The bracelets from the hospital, the ones with her name and number printed on them, the color of weak tea. The schedules, the appointments, the directions to rooms I can still find in my sleep. The papers, the forms, the results, the printouts, the whole machinery of the dying. It is the record. It is not her. I have to believe in the difference, because I am about to burn one of them.

The straw man goes into the pyre at dusk. The town has finished him, a cardboard box in his straw arms, a note pinned to his coat, a scrap of paper for every grievance, every loss, every name. We do not say what is in ours. That is the rule of the effigy, and the rule is the kindness: the fire does not read, and the fire does not tell.

I stand with the town while the light goes out of the day and the torches are lit, and I think about the record, and I think about the woman, and I think that burning the record is not forgetting. It is the opposite. The record burns so the memory can stand. The papers go up so the woman can stay. The fire takes the schedule of her dying so that the living days of her can stand in the light of it.

It is the hardest thing I have ever burned, and it will go up fast. Straw does not struggle. Paper does not struggle. It gives itself, all of it, in the time it takes to draw a breath, and then there is the light, and the warmth, and the year, and the town, and me, standing in it, ready to be warm.

Chapter 41: The Spark

Del Proulx has done this lighting since before I came home to Merrow, and he does it the way he digs the barbecue pit: no fuss, no speech, the work itself the whole ceremony. He holds the torch, a length of pipe wrapped at one end in rags soaked in kerosene and lit from a cookstove lantern, and he waits at the base of the pyre until the wind is right. The pyre is a house of brush and broken chairs and dead leaves, fifteen feet to its peak, built over three weeks from the whole town's leavings.

The crowd has gone quiet. That is the part no one expects from two thousand people: how quiet they go. Children held back by their parents' hands. The old people up front on folding chairs. I stand with the committee, my hands in my pockets, and my hands are remembering something else. In September, in my mother's bedroom, I held a match to a candle on the bedside table, and that small flame, one wick, one pool of wax, was all the fire in that house. I kept it burning for eleven nights. I would have said that was the whole of what a fire could be, a flame you could cover with your palm.

Del touches the torch to the kindling nest at the pyre's heart. For a beat, nothing. Then the fire finds its air and goes up the inside of the brush like water filling a cup, and the sound arrives after the light, a crackling that becomes a roar, and then the heat arrives, and it is not like standing near a fire. It is like standing inside weather. I step back with everyone else. At a hundred feet my cheeks feel it. The updraft takes the sparks straight up into the dark, and the whole commons is lit, and Del stands back with his arms crossed, watching his work, and I think: I lit a candle in September and called it tending. This is the same fire, grown to the size of a house, and it was always this. The spark does not know how big it will become. It only knows the wood is there, and the air, and it goes.

Chapter 42: The Dancing

By nine the pyre has burned down to what Del calls its best light: a bed of coals the size of a room, giving off a heat you can stand in, the flames that remain low and constant, the color of the inside of a good ember. This is the hour the festival is really for. The music starts from the bandstand, the high-school band, then the Reisers' old friend on the fiddle, and the town begins to dance on the packed dirt around the fire.

The old people go first, a circle of them, slow, their own rhythm, some dancing the way they danced at their own weddings. Then the children, who have no self-consciousness and no choreography, who simply run and spin and fling themselves at the light. Nora finds me where I stand at the edge with my arms crossed. She has been herself all autumn, full of color again, grading papers until seven instead of midnight, and she takes my hand without asking, the way she took it in the hospital corridor in May, and pulls me into the circle.

I dance. I am not good at it. The firelight is on everyone's faces, and I watch the town, Joan and Bill Reiser stepping together, Marie Vo laughing at the children, Del nodding time from the bench, and I am crying, and I do not mind, because the light is on everyone the same way and nobody is looking at me to judge it. The embers go up off the coals in a slow festival of their own, hundreds of them climbing the dark, and the smoke goes with them.

Nora keeps my hand. When the fiddle stops and the town slows, I say it out loud, the inventory I have been keeping silently all year. Burned: the papers, the bracelets, the schedules, the record of the dying. Kept: the cup, the jam, the recipes, the orchard. Risen: this. I do not explain it. Nora nods, which is the whole friendship, and we stand in the heat of the coals until the fire is down to ash.

Chapter 43: November: The Furnace

The first hard freeze comes the Tuesday after the festival, the kind that silvers the roof by mid-morning, and my furnace does not come on. I wake to a house at fifty-eight degrees and check the thermostat, then the breaker, then the pilot, all the things I know, which is almost nothing. The blower is silent. The house, for the first time since I came back to Merrow, feels empty of its own heat.

Ray Corrigan comes that afternoon, a man in his sixties who has kept the town's furnaces going for forty years, and he goes straight down to the basement without small talk. I follow. My basement is a place I mostly forget exists, a low room with the furnace, a squat steel box in the corner, ducts rising from it like a ribcage. Ray opens the access panel and looks in. Limit switch, he says. The flame comes on, heats the box, the switch should tell the blower to move the air. It is not telling. He shows me: the thin copper tube, the little disc that warps with heat and closes the circuit. A dime-sized part, and the whole house depends on it.

I watch him work. There is craft here too, I am learning, the same craft as the hearth, moved indoors and made invisible. The furnace is the house's hidden fire: no flame to watch, no kindling to lay, only heat delivered through pipes and registers, a fire that runs on a small blue pilot and a switch the size of a thumb. You cannot see it burn. You can only tell, by whether the rooms stay warm, that it has been burning all along. Ray replaces the switch, and the blower comes on with its deep hum, and the first heat climbs the ducts.

He packs his tools. This one has got another ten years in it, he says. Your father kept it right. My father, who died in a November, who kept this furnace and this house and this woodpile. I pay Ray and walk him to the door, and the house is already changing, the cold coming out of the walls, the radiators ticking. The craft of the mechanical fire is attendance, the same as any other: you watch for the signs, you catch the small failure before it becomes the large one, you keep the thing burning whether or not you can see the flame.

Chapter 44: The House

The morning after the furnace is fixed I walk the three streets to my mother's house, and I stand in the yard with the cold coming up off the grass, and I understand what I am going to do before I have decided it. The house is empty. It has been empty since September, when we carried the last of the hospice things out, and I have been letting it stand empty, the way you let a wound stand until you are ready to touch it. Her furnace has not run since spring. The rooms are forty-eight degrees. The apples are still on the orchard trees, unharvested, gone soft.

I keep it warm. That is the decision, and it arrives whole. I will walk here every morning and every evening through the winter, I will turn the thermostat to fifty-eight, I will check the pipes and the drafts and the water heater, I will keep the house alive the way you keep a body alive, a body I am learning to tend. It is not her. It was never her. But the house held her, and the rooms hold her cooking, her books, her soup pot, and a house that goes cold is a house that has been given up on.

In the evenings, after I light my own fire and before I sleep, I walk back. The thermostat at fifty-eight is the smallest flame I keep all year, no light, no smoke, only the sound of the furnace turning over and the knowledge that the pipes will not freeze and the rooms will be waiting. I think about my father's November, ten years ago, the furnace of that winter, how the whole house was a grief that had to be kept warm, the pipes bled, the wood stacked, the cold kept at the door because the living still had to live. I did not understand, then, that keeping a house warm through a grief is a kind of grief itself, a way of staying beside something without having to say what you are staying for. My year has been folding toward this since January. The book has been folding toward this. The mother's house, warm through November, this is the fire I am learning to tend.

Chapter 45: The Pipes

The second time I call Ray Corrigan it is not for a failure. It is because my mother's house has a radiator in the spare room that knocks and stays cold, and I want to learn to fix it myself. Ray comes, patient, and shows me. Air, he says. The water comes up from the furnace, hot, and the air gets trapped at the top of the radiator, and the hot water cannot get in. So you bleed it. He gives me a small key, square-headed, and shows me the vent at the top of the last panel.

The craft is precise. You put the key on the square, you turn it a quarter turn, and the air comes out with a hiss, a thin, high sound, like a kettle far off, and then a spit of water, and then, when the water runs clear and steady, you close the vent. The hiss is the air, Ray says. When you hear water, the heat is on its way. I do it in every room of my mother's house, and I listen to each radiator sing, and the warmth comes up the pipes room by room, and I think about threading, the way one thing runs through another, a thread passing through every room and every fire of the year, and I see the pipes for what they are.

Every fire I have tended this year has been a pipe to this house. January's oak in the hearth, February's blue flame over the stock pot, March's smoke over the pruning pile, April's forge, May's exhaustion, June's news from the far hills, July's pit, August's kiln that fired the cup, September's candle, October's pyre, all of it running hot under the floors and up the walls of this one small house, warming the room where my mother sat, where I sit now. The air has to be let out or the heat cannot arrive. The hiss, the spit, the warmth coming up. That is what a year is, I think: a long bleeding of the air, so the heat can get through.

Chapter 46: The Warm

By the third week of November my mother's house holds its heat, and I sit in her kitchen in a sweater with my coat unbuttoned, and I understand what warmth is for. It is not for the body's comfort, though it is that. It is for the room you can sit in without a coat, the room where you can stay. A cold house tells you to leave. A warm house tells you nothing at all, and lets you sit, and that is the whole of the craft: to make a room that does not ask you to go.

I make tea on her stove. It is the first time I have cooked on it since September, the same two-burner wood stove she kept a fire in all winter, the one she was always complaining about in January when I came over and lit it for her. I set the kettle on, I wait for the whistle, and I pour it into the cup, the cup from Maren's kiln, the color of dusk, the one I carried to the hospital and put in my mother's hands. It holds the heat the way it was made to. The warm comes up through the glaze into my palms, and I hold the cup the way she held it, in both hands, her two hands wrapped around the clay, and I drink tea made on her stove in her kitchen, and the rooms are warm around me, and the orchard is bare outside the window, and the year is almost kept.

Almost. The November nights are long now, and I walk the three streets home in the dark, and I light my own fire, and I keep them both, the house behind me warm, the house before me warm. This is what warmth is for, I think. Not to banish the cold, which will always return. To make the cold bearable by keeping a room, or two rooms, in which a person can stay. I have been trying, all year, to learn to burn. What I am learning, in November, is the smaller and harder thing: to keep warm.

Chapter 47: December: The Candle of the Year's End

December comes to Merrow the way it always does, with the first of the month cold and the town putting a single candle in each window, lit at dusk, a custom older than anyone can remember. The first time I saw it I was home from the city for the holidays and thought it was pretty. Now I understand it as a discipline: the whole town, every evening for a month, lighting one small flame and attending it. The candles are short and fat, made to burn about four hours, so the person who lights one must be home for the evening or trust the wind. Some windows hold theirs steady. Some gutter out by nine. You can read the town's evenings from its candles.

I light one in my mother's window. I had decided, without deciding, that her house would have a candle too, that the house I was keeping warm through the winter would also be kept lit. I walk there at dusk with a new candle from the hardware store, set it in the window above the kitchen sink, and light it with a match from the tin on her shelf. The flame steadies. It is the same candle I lit at her bedside in September, the same one flame, one wick, attended. I stay until it has burned past its first hour, and I come back at nine to check it, and I come back at midnight to blow it out, and the walk becomes the shape of the month: dusk to light, midnight to put out.

I read back over my year's notes. I have kept them the way you keep a woodpile, every fire recorded, dated, labeled: January's oak, the kindling lesson; February's blue flame, the soup; March's smoke over the orchard; April's forge, the tools, the temper; May's exhaustion; June's news and the maps; July's pit and the corn; August's kiln and the cup; September's candle; October's pyre; November's furnace and the bleeding of the pipes; and now December's window-candle, this one, in the room where she taught me to make soup. Every fire accounted for. Not one of them lost. I close the notebook, and the candle burns on in the window, one flame, attended, and the month goes by that way, the small ceremonies of the year's end holding the dark at a distance of one wick.

Chapter 48: The Retrospect

On the last day of the old year I sit at my desk with the notebook open and the year laid out in front of me, twelve fires, one per month, and I read them in order. January's oak, laid in the hearth my father built, the first fire I recorded. February's blue flame over the stock pot, the soup, the argument about soup that was a good argument. March's smoke rising through the wet air over the pruning pile, my mother poking the brush, slower this year. April's forge and the tempering colors, straw yellow, peacock, blue, and my father's old tools going into the kindling box. May's exhaustion, the burnout of a fire that had consumed its own fuel. June's news from the far hills, the maps, the list of what I would carry. July's pit, low and slow, the corn, her hand on my wrist, I am just tired, it is the heat. August's kiln, the cup the color of dusk. September's candle, the bedside, her hand under mine, the tomatoes. October's pyre and the box of papers going up. November's furnace and the bleeding of the pipes. December's window-candle, burning now, in her window.

And I see it, on the page, the way you see a fire you have been feeding without knowing it: this book has been about her since January. Before the diagnosis, before the hospital, before the candle, since New Year's Day, when I laid oak in the fireplace and called the year an empty hearth, she was the fire I was tending. The mother who complained about her stove and needed her fire lit. The mother whose jam lined my shelf, whose soup I could taste in February. The book began, in January, as a book about the craft of fire. It has been, all along, a book about keeping her, her orchard, her kitchen, her stubborn warmth, by learning the craft of fire well enough to keep it.

I inventory what burned and what did not. Burned: the year, my father's blunt tools, the record of the dying, my old idea that I was writing about wood and flame. Did not burn: the cup, the jam, the recipes, the orchard, the house I have kept warm, this notebook, this book. What I thought I was keeping was a record. What I have been keeping is the fire itself. I close the notebook. Somewhere in my mother's house, in the window, the candle burns, one flame, attended, and I am not surprised by it anymore. I have been writing my way here since January, and the page knew before I did.

Chapter 49: The Ember

New Year's Eve, and the last fire of the year is laid in the hearth my father built, and I lay it with the full ceremony of the craft, the way I laid the first one in January, because I want the year to end the way it began, in front of this stone, with my hands doing a thing they know how to do. Oak for the base, the split-side of the kindling toward the flame, the draft open, the match at the paper. The fire catches, climbs, steadies, and I sit in front of it for the whole dark afternoon, watching it burn down, and I let it burn lower than I usually would, because tonight I am not burning the fire. I am learning to bank it.

The craft of banking is the craft of the whole year, and I do it slowly. I wait until the fire is a bed of coals, deep and even, no flame left but the blue that runs over the surface. I rake the coals together in the center of the firebox, mounded, with the largest and most solid coal at the heart. Then I draw the ash over them with the shovel Ada made, a clean layer, an inch, no more, enough to smother the flame but not the heat, because ash is an insulator, not a killer, and a coal covered in ash will hold its heat for hours and be found alive in the morning. I set the damper halfway, so the draft is slowed but not stopped; a banked fire needs just enough air to keep the coals breathing, not enough to wake them into flame. I close the glass door. The room goes dark. Under the ash, the ember holds, the whole year's heat gathered in one place, asleep and alive.

I sit in the dark and think about what an ember is. It is the flame that is gone and the heat that remains. It is the fire reduced to its truest form, not the show, not the roar, but the keeping. Everything I have burned this year has come down, in the end, to this: something that stops being a flame and starts being a warmth that can be carried, banked, found again. My father built this fireplace stone by stone, and I put my hand flat on the stone, warm from the box behind it, and I understand the answer the year has been giving me all along. This is what keeping means. Not holding on to the flame, which will not be held. Raking the coals, drawing the ash, setting the damper, and trusting that in the morning there will be a live coal under the gray, that what we tend will be there when we come back. The ember is the year's answer. The fire is gone. The heat remains. I will re-light it in the morning, from this same coal, and that will be the first fire of the new year, and it will be, as it has always been, her fire, kept.

Chapter 50: The Keeping

New Year's morning, and the house is cold, and I am up before the light, because the whole point of banking is the morning. I stand in front of the hearth and I open the glass door, and I brush the ash back with my hand, and there it is: a live coal, low and red, the same coal I covered at midnight, holding its heat through the night like a small animal breathing. I have kept something. This is the whole lesson, and I have learned it, and I am not ashamed to feel how much it means to me, a grown woman, to find a coal still alive under the ash.

I lay the new kindling over it, the split cedar, the dry birch bark, and I blow, gently, the way my father taught me on my twelfth birthday, and the coal flushes red, and a thread of smoke goes up, and the kindling catches, and the first fire of the new year is lit from last year's coal. The flame climbs into the oak. The year turns. It is the same fire. It was always going to be the same fire; that is what keeping is for.

I dress and walk the three streets to my mother's house, the morning cold and bright. I let myself in, I check the furnace, I listen to the radiators, I go into her kitchen, and I take the cup, the color of dusk, from Maren's kiln, made in August, carried to the hospital in September, held in her hands, and I set it on the table, in her place, the place where she sat for seventy-four years, and I stand there a moment, and the year's fires are all accounted for, every one of them, from January's oak to this morning's re-lit coal. Burned: the year, and what the year burned, the record of the dying, my father's tools, my old idea of what I was writing. Kept: the ember, the cup, the jam, the recipes, the orchard, the two houses warm, the notebook, her. Risen: this book, and the fire that will come out of it.

I make tea on her stove. I pour it into the cup. I sit in her kitchen, in her place, and the warm comes up through the glaze into my hands, and outside the window the orchard is bare and waiting, and I think: this is the book. Not the wood, not the flame, not even the craft, though the craft is what let me do it. The ember under the ash. The cup in the hands. The grief kept burning, kept warm, kept, fed every month, banked at night, found alive in the morning. I carry it out of her kitchen, out of the book, and I give it to you, the way you carry a coal from a house to start a fire in the next one. The fire is gone. The heat remains. It is still warm in my hands. It will be warm in yours.
