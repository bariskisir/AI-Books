The Last Wrangler
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Last Herd

The wind woke him. It was the Sweet Grass wind, the same wind that had waked him forty years, and he lay in the dark of the line shack and listened to it work the eaves. Gray light came through the one window. Outside, the mule stamped once and was quiet.

He built the fire with dry cedar, set coffee to boil, and went out into the cold. The range lay under a skin of frost to the foot of the Beartooth uplift, snowfields holding to the high shoulders of the pass like a white shawl. Cole Hayden was fifty-eight and he had buried two lives, and the country did not care.

The rider came up the trail at first light, a man half his age on a good horse with a letter in his vest. Samuel Brandt's brand on the envelope. Cole read it standing in the dooryard, coffee cooling in his hand. Come to the house, Samuel had written. I am putting the herd over the pass this spring and I mean to have you for it.

Inside, he looked at Martha's saddle. It hung from the rafter where she had left it, the leather gone soft with oil, the stirrups cut down a hand's width for her short legs. Two winters the pneumonia had taken her, and he had not touched the saddle since. He had a mind to leave it hanging.

He saddled the bay gelding, tied the pack behind the cantle, and left the shack standing open, the way a man leaves a place he does not mean to come back to. The mule followed because it had always followed.

He rode south across the Sweet Grass in the long light, the wind coming down off the uplift with the smell of the range, grass and snowmelt and the sour old smell of cattle. Out on the draws the longhorns stood watching him pass, thin from the winter, horns wide and pale in the sun. Four hundred head of the Spanish cattle, the last of the breed that had built the trails, and Cole knew them the way he knew the faces of men he had worked with and lost.

He did not think about the railroad, or the date certain, or the wire going up while a man's back was turned. He thought about the saddle on the rafter and Martha's hand sitting light on his arm at the end, as if she were already going.

A man must do one last true thing. He had known it before the letter. He rode to say yes.

Chapter 2: Samuel's Offer

The Brandt house sat on the bench above the Sweet Grass, a two-story frame never given to paint. Samuel waited on the porch with his hands on the rail, and Cole saw in the set of his shoulders what the winter had taken. Seventy-eight, a bad heart, and he stood looking out over his range like a man reading a letter.

They sat in the front room where the light came low and yellow through the glass. Samuel poured two fingers each, did not offer food, did not apologize. On the table lay the map of the Indian trail over the Beartooth, drawn by a man dead forty years, the old route marked through the timberline and across the notch.

"Wainwright come to see me in March," Samuel said. "Brought a paper. Price for the range and the herd both, and a talk about progress. Wire, registered Herefords, the railroad hauling beef that'll never see a trail. He calls it the last of the old waste going to its rightful end."

"And you told him no."

Samuel's mouth worked. "I told him the herd is not money. It never was money. It's the last of the Spanish cattle that walked up out of Mexico on the old drives, and it will walk one more time or die in the pens."

The railroad had set the date. The last cattle train out of Millbrook, in the valley beyond the pass, would run the third Monday of June, and load what came down the mountain and nothing more.

"The cars will be there," Samuel said. "The rails end against the mountain and they load what the mountain sends."

Cole looked at the map. The old trail ran straight as a thought up the uplift, through the timberline, through the notch, down into the far valley. He had not been over the pass in twenty years.

"The men?" Cole said.

"Coming. A vaquero out of the border country that knows Spanish cattle. A rifle hand out of Texas that don't say much and don't need to. A cook with a wagon and a bottle."

"You've been planning this a while."

"Forty years," Samuel said. "I just didn't know it." He looked at Cole with the pale, hard eyes of a man who had outlived his countrymen. "You'll take it? The drag, the gather, the whole of it. You're the last of the real hands on this range, Cole. You and the herd are the same story."

Cole thought of the saddle on the rafter. He thought of the wind.

"I'll take it," he said.

They shook hands over the map of the old trail, and outside the wind came down off the uplift and moved across the grass like the country turning over in its sleep.

Chapter 3: The Crew

They came in on the same wind, over three days, and the yard filled up like a hand opening.

The remuda came first, thirty head strung in a rope corral between the big cottonwoods, and Cole walked the string with coffee gone cold and made up his mind about each one. He picked the four he would ride, gentle and tough, and left the rest for the drag and the cut and the night watch.

Ezra came up the wagon road on a good mule, leading a packhorse, a Winchester '94 in the scabbard looking as old as he did and as cared for. Forty-five, up out of Texas in the '80s with the last of the big drives. He sat his mule a long minute looking at the herd on the far slope before he got down, shook Cole's hand, and said his name once.

Dutchy came that afternoon in the chuck wagon, a Studebaker with the box built high and the canvas roof patched in four colors, and the smell of the wagon was coffee and woodsmoke and the sour note of a bottle under the seat. Sixty, broad, pink-faced, he sang in German while he set up the kitchen, and every third song he stopped and looked around as if he had lost something and found it again between his feet.

The boy came last, at dusk, on a horse ridden too hard. Nineteen, brown as cured hide, and he spoke Spanish to the horses before he spoke English to the men. Joaquin. He stood in the light of the cook fire and looked at the longhorns bedded on the flats with a face that was not young at all.

"Cuernos largos," he said, half to himself. "I thought they were all gone. I thought I was the last one left who remembered them."

Cole looked at the boy and saw something that cost him. He remembered when the old knowledge was common as weather. Now here was a boy out of the border country carrying it, and the herd it belonged to, and the two of them not yet dead.

They ate at the long table under the cottonwoods, Dutchy's coffee like hot varnish, and Samuel came out and sat at the head and did not say much. The old work was beginning. Cole felt it in his hands, the way a man feels rain in a scar.

"Tomorrow we gather," he said. "There's cattle scattered wild from the winter, calves unbranded. We bring them all in or we drive a lie to the pass."

Ezra lifted his cup. "Then we bring them all in."

The wind moved in the cottonwoods. In the rope corral the remuda shifted, and out on the dark flats the longhorns lowed once, a sound as old as the breed, and settled.

Chapter 4: Joaquin

Joaquin's story came out in pieces over the gather, the way a man's story does when it is good and he is not proud of all of it.

He was born on the border, in a village of adobe and dust the revolutions had crossed and crossed again, and his father had been a vaquero on the haciendas that were dying even then. The boy had followed the cattle north with the last drives, riding the drag because the drag was where children rode, and he had learned the old knowledge from men already old when he was born. He read a longhorn the way Cole read the wind: the set of the hip, the angle of the horn, the way a bull carried its head when it meant trouble. The Spanish breed had come out of that country, and the boy carried its memory like scripture.

On the first morning of the gather, Cole put him on the point and watched.

The herd had scattered over the winter into every draw of the Sweet Grass, and the gather was not rounding up cattle so much as calling a scattered nation back into one body. Joaquin worked the far edge of the bench on a bay cutting horse, and he moved through the wild stuff like a man who had never been in a hurry. He did not chase. He got ahead of them, quiet, and let the cattle choose the way he wanted them to go, and the wild calves came off the benches in a string as neat as if he had laid them out by hand.

At noon they met in the middle of the flat. Cole had forty head off the south draws; Ezra twenty off the coulee line; Joaquin sixty, the boy's horse blowing foam and the boy calm as the flat sky.

"Where did you learn to work a calf that close?" Cole said.

Joaquin shrugged. "They are not calves. They are longhorns. The breed is older than the wire. You cannot chase what is older than the wire. You have to ask."

Cole looked at the boy and the wild stuff standing quiet behind him, and thought about the word ask, and what it would mean to teach it to the men he had ridden with all his life.

"You're on the point for the drive," Cole said. "That's yours."

In the evening, when the cook fire went up, Joaquin walked among the longhorns on foot, slow, talking to them in Spanish, and the old bulls turned their great horned heads to watch him pass. The boy was nineteen and already a relic, and the herd was a relic, and the whole of it — boy, cattle, and the knowledge that held them together — was being carried to the pass like a lamp in a wind.

Chapter 5: Ezra

Ezra was the quietest thing in camp, and Cole learned early that a quiet man with a rifle is a country you do not need to measure twice.

He had come up from Texas in the '80s, when the last of the great drives were still rolling north, and he had ridden them to the railheads and watched the railheads move west until there was nowhere left to ride to. He did not talk about the country he came from, and nobody asked. A weight sat at the bottom of his stillness, and the crew respected it the way you respect a held breath.

His rifle was a Winchester '94, carbine length, the wood worn dark where his hand had lived for twenty-five years. When he took it down to clean it, the camp went quiet without meaning to. Cole had seen his kind on the old drives: the rifle hand who rode the high ground, who settled arguments at three hundred yards that other men would have settled at thirty, and never made a show of it.

On the second day Ezra earned his place in one minute. A young bull, gone rogue in the winter, broke out of the gather and ran the flat with three riders useless behind him. Ezra had ridden onto the bench without a word, the rifle across his saddle, waiting.

"Don't shoot him, Ezra," Cole said. "He's only scared."

Ezra did not answer. He lifted the rifle and put a shot into the ground six feet ahead of the running bull, the dirt throwing into the animal's face, and the bull turned like a struck horse and came back into the gather on his own, his head down, done. Not a hair on him touched. Ezra worked the lever, snugged the rifle home, and said not one word about it.

"Thank you," Cole said, when the horses were quiet.

Ezra nodded. That was all. He would not carry a rope the way the rest did; his hands were for the reins and the rifle, and on a drive where the herd had enemies — and Cole had begun to suspect it did — that was worth more than any two ropers.

Later, at the fire, Dutchy asked him, half drunk and not meaning harm, why a man of his years rode the mule instead of a horse.

Ezra looked into the fire a long time. "The mule don't want anything from me. A horse wants. A mule just carries. I got tired of being wanted for something."

The fire popped. Nobody said anything. It was the most Ezra had said in three days, and it sat in the camp the rest of the night, a weight laid down and left where a man could step around it.

Chapter 6: Dutchy's Wagon

The chuck wagon was the whole world turned on its side and loaded into a box, and Dutchy was its priest.

It was a Studebaker, the box raised high on the springs, the canvas bowed over the bows. The rear was the kitchen: the pantry box hinging down into a worktable, the coffee mill bolted to the tailgate, the dutch ovens nested in their ropes so they would not ring and bruise the bread. A hundredweight of flour, the beans, the salt, the sourdough starter in its crock with a cloth tied over it. On the underside, slung in a boot of canvas and rope, rode the things the wagon was not supposed to carry.

Dutchy was sixty, built like a barrel, a face that had started red and gone to weather. A German out of the old country, he had lost his family over there — the whole of it, in a way he never finished a sentence about — and he had come to the range and found a man could make himself useful with a skillet and keep the past at a distance with whiskey.

His coffee was a legend before the drive was a day old. It would strip paint, the hands said, and it was not a joke. He boiled it black as a night without stars, served in tin cups that had never known soap, and the men drank it because men on a drive drink what the cook gives them and are glad of the warmth.

At night he sang. The songs were German, old ones, and he sang them low and true while he scrubbed the ovens or whittled a peg, and the men did not understand the words and did not need to. Joaquin said once that Dutchy sang like a man who had lost a country and was singing it back into being, one verse at a time.

The bottle lived under the seat, in a compartment padded with a blanket, and he visited it at regular hours. On the old drives a drinking cook was a danger, but Dutchy never let the bottle near the work — the wagon rolled before the bottle was touched, and the bottle, in its way, was the price the man paid to stay upright.

Late on the third night, Cole found Dutchy on the tailgate with a cup of the black coffee, looking up at the pass standing white against the stars.

"They told me the railroad takes the country," Dutchy said, not looking at him. "They have told me that in two languages now. I am still here."

"So are the cattle," Cole said.

Dutchy nodded and drank to something, and the wind took the steam off the cup and carried it north toward the uplift, toward the place where the trail went over the top of the world.

Chapter 7: The Trail North

They left the home range on a Tuesday in May, in the first full light, and the Sweet Grass wind came down off the uplift to see them off.

Cole had thought he knew what the leaving would feel like. He had done it a hundred times on the old drives. But this was not the old drives. Those had been going somewhere the country still wanted to go. This one went over the pass to a railhead where the rails ended against the mountain and the last train waited, and every man knew what the cars meant, and nobody said it.

Samuel stood on the porch with his hand on the rail, smaller than he had been a month before. To Cole he had said only, "Bring them over the top, Cole. That's all I ask. The rest is the country's business."

The herd left the flats in a long body, four hundred head of longhorns walking north into the wind. Cole rode point, then dropped back to the swing and then the drag, finding the rhythm of the country again. The herd found its own pace, the slow, unhurried walk of cattle with a hundred miles of instinct in their legs. The old bulls led, great horned shapes with the Spanish blood still high in them; behind came the cows and the calves and the brindle cow Cole had known since she was a heifer, and the whole of it strung out along the trail like a line of history.

The rhythm came back faster than he expected. There is a music to a drive, he thought — the creak of leather, the low of the cattle, the click of hooves on stone, the wind — and it was a music that had nearly gone out of the world, played one last time by a band with no business still being alive.

Joaquin rode the point as promised, a small dark figure ahead of the herd, and the cattle followed him like they had signed a treaty with him personally. Ezra rode the high side, his mule picking along the bench with the rifle across the saddle. Dutchy's wagon creaked along at the back with its own rhythm, the smell of the coffee drifting forward even empty, like a promise.

By afternoon they had left the home range and entered the country of the uplift, the grass giving way to the first timber, the wind freshening off the snowfields. Cole rode drag and watched the herd walk north, and he counted them as he counted his breaths, and the counting was not fear. It was love. The last herd in Montana walking one last road, and him behind it, the last wrangler on the last drag of the last drive.

Chapter 8: The Sweet Grass

The gather took a week, and the Sweet Grass itself was the teacher.

Cole had ridden this country forty years and thought he knew it, but working it the old way — slow, on horseback, reading every draw for what the winter had hidden — he saw it fresh, the way a man re-reads a letter and finds the words he had been skipping. The range was not flat, despite the name. It rolled and folded and lifted toward the uplift in long swells, and every fold held a pocket of grass and water and a handful of cattle that had drifted there in the fall and been forgotten by the snow.

The winter had been hard, and neglect had done the rest. Samuel's heart had kept him off the range since the fall, and the cattle had scattered wild, running free of any hand for six months. The calves had never seen a branding iron, and they came off the benches with their tails high, running the way their great-grandparents had run across a country with no fences in it at all.

Cole worked the south draws with Ezra, cutting the scattered remnants into the growing body on the flats. Joaquin took the high ground and worked it like a fisherman reading water, letting the wind carry the scent of the gather down to the hidden pockets, so the cattle came out on their own, curious, and were turned and brought in almost without being chased.

The country gave them things as they worked. A spring running clear out of a rock face. A stand of bluestem tall enough that a man on horseback had to look over it, saved by luck from the winter grazing. The track of a grizzly, big as a dinner plate, crossing the trail and not returning, the land sovereign and unbothered. And the tracks of a rider — Cole found them twice, fresh, on the rim where a man could look down on the gather — and he did not mention them to the others, and he thought about his son.

The Sweet Grass was a living thing, Cole decided, and the gather was not rounding it up so much as listening to it. The wind moved over it in waves you could see, the grass silver and green by turn. At the end of the week, four hundred head stood on the flats below the Brandt house, wild calves and all, a nation called home.

Samuel came down to look on the last evening. He walked the length of the gathered herd with his hand out, and the old bulls watched him pass, and he said, "There they are. The last of them. Look at them, Cole. They came in for you."

"They came in for the grass," Cole said.

Samuel shook his head. "Cattle know. They came in because it's time."

Chapter 9: Will's Outfit

On the second day of the gather, Cole saw a rider on the skyline, and the day changed color.

He was working the west rim alone, cutting a pair of wild heifers out of the upper coulee, when the movement caught his eye — a horse and rider on the long ridge to the east, small against the sky, sitting still and watching. Cole reined up and shaded his eyes, and at that distance there was nothing to see but a man on a good horse. But the rider moved then, dropping off the skyline toward the Wainwright range beyond the divide, and the way he sat the horse — the seat, the balance, the rise and fall in the working gait — hit Cole in the chest like something thrown.

He knew the seat before he knew the face. He had known it for twenty years, the way a man sees the son he left: growing, changing, becoming a man on a horse, always on a horse. Will. His son. Twenty-eight now. Foreman of the Wainwright outfit, riding the rim above Cole's gather with a pair of glasses and no reason to be there except to watch.

And Cole saw what the rider had been doing. There were tracks going east, fresh, and a gap in the gather where cattle had been taken out. The rider had been cutting cattle, and cutting them toward Wainwright's range, drawing the scattered remnants of Brandt's herd off the bench while the gather worked the west.

Cole sat his horse on the rim and watched the small figure drop out of sight over the divide, and the wind came up and took the moment away, leaving the shape of his son printed on the inside of his eyes.

He did not tell the others. A man who names a thing before he understands it has named it wrong. That night at the fire, while the crew ate Dutchy's beans and the talk went around, Cole was silent in a way the men noticed, and Joaquin looked at him once with the dark, knowing eyes of a boy who had grown up among men who carried more than they said.

Ezra alone seemed to understand without being told. He came up to Cole where he sat apart, scouring his plate with grass, and stood a moment.

"Man on the rim," Ezra said. It was not a question.

Cole looked at him. "You seen him too."

"Seen him three days running. He's a good hand. Cuts clean, don't spook the stock." A pause. "He favor you in the saddle."

The fire popped. The wind worked the cottonwoods. Cole sat in the dark and felt the word settle into him like a stone into water, and he did not answer, because there was nothing he could say that would not be a lie or a wound.

Chapter 10: The Rival Ranch

Cole rode the east ridge on the morning of the third day, alone, to see for himself.

The Wainwright yard lay below him in the long valley, a different country from the Sweet Grass, a country re-made on purpose. The wire ran in clean lines for miles — Glidden barbed wire on squared cedar posts, not the ragged bobwire of the old range but wire that had been planned. Beyond it the pasture lay in tame green blocks, and in them stood the registered Herefords, white-faced, thick-bodied, the new breed that had come in on the railroad and was eating the old world one range at a time. They stood in the grass like a promise kept.

The house was painted. The barn was painted. A windmill turned above the well, and beyond the corrals, strung on single poles, ran the telephone line, the wire that carried a man's voice faster than any horse could run. Cole looked down at it all and felt the country he had known closing like a hand.

He found Will in the big corral, working a string of young horses with two men Cole did not know. Cole stayed in the timber, but he had brought the glasses, and he watched his son the way a man watches weather he is afraid of.

Will was good — the boy had the gift in his blood, and Ruth had seen it, and she had written it to him once, the only letter she ever sent after the leaving: Will can ride anything that walks, Cole, he has your seat, and I have to tell you that even though I hate you for it. He worked the young horses with a quiet hand, no spur, no temper, and the horses came to him the way they had always come to Cole.

This was his son's life, chosen or given or fallen into. Wainwright had made Will foreman at twenty-five, young for the work, and promised him more: a spread of his own, a piece of the Brandt range when the old man was finally broken. That promise was the hook that held him — a deed that did not exist yet, and Will, who had been left once already, believed it.

Cole lowered the glasses and sat a long time in the timber, watching the smoke from the painted chimney go up straight in the still air.

He did not know how to call his son home. He only knew that the drive had gained an enemy it had not named, and that the enemy wore the seat of a horse that Cole Hayden had taught to ride before he had taught it to walk.

The wind came up off the Sweet Grass. Cole turned his horse and rode back to the gather, and he did not look back, because looking back at his son's life was a country he could not afford to enter.

Chapter 11: The Broken Bridge

They came to the fork on the third day out, and the bridge was down.

The planking lay stacked on the far bank like cordwood, neat as a man's morning work. The stringers still stood, the cedar piers still square in the water, and the Sweet Grass fork ran under them brown and quick with the melt. It was not a flood that had done this. A flood takes a bridge apart the way it takes anything — sideways, broken, scattered. This had been taken down by hand.

Cole stepped off his horse and looked at the far bank a long while. The county man who met them was small and apologetic and full of excuses. High water, he said. Backing up. Surprised they'd come this way at all, with the season what it was. He said the county would have her rebuilt by fall, most likely, weather permitting, funds permitting.

Cole looked at the cut ends of the planking. The marks were fresh and clean, made with a saw, not the weather. He did not say what he thought. He was a man who had learned to keep his thinking inside his hat, where the wind could not take it.

The crew made camp on the near bank and stood looking at the water. Ezra walked the length of the ruined bridge and came back with nothing said. Joaquin squatted at the water's edge and read the river the way he read a bull — patient, still, seeing what moved in it. Dutchy poured coffee that would strip paint and did not make a joke about it, which told Cole more than words.

"It'll be the river, then," Samuel said from his wagon seat. The old man had come the whole way so far, wrapped in a blanket though the days were warm, watching the herd string out ahead of him like a man watching a long thing end. "The old way."

"The old way," Cole said. He had not crossed a herd over a swollen fork in twenty years. The horses would go over first, then the cattle, swimming in the string. A steer lost on a crossing like that was not a shame. It was the price of water, and every man who had ever pushed cattle had paid it or watched it paid.

Nobody said Wainwright's name. That was the measure of how sure they were. The county man went on talking — reimbursements, warrants, the spring of 1913 — and the crew let him talk, and the river ran, and the far bank stood green and quiet and knowing nothing.

Cole bedded down that night on the near bank with the sound of the water in his ears and the knowledge of the saw-cut ends behind his eyes. Somebody wanted the herd stopped. The question was only whether they wanted it stopped enough to put a man in the river.

Chapter 12: The River Crossing

They crossed at first light, in the old way.

Cole went over first with the saddle horses, and the mule, and the remuda, and he did not watch the water. The horses swam with their heads high and their eyes white, and the current carried them down a hundred yards before they found footing and climbed the far bank dripping and shivering. He came back across on the wagon horses, and then the work began.

The cattle hated it. Longhorns would face a grizzly or a man with a rope, but water that moved and made no sound was the oldest fear in them. They milled on the near bank, lowing, while the crew hazed them down to the ford. Cole worked the point with the gray, pushing the leaders in until the water took their chests, until the first of them struck out and swam, stringing out behind him like beads on a snapped wire.

The herd crossed in a long line, and it was beautiful and terrible the way all true things are. The old cows went first, the brindle cow in the lead with her calf pushed ahead of her, and the bulls came behind, and the yearlings, and the drag came last with the calves strung out and bawling. Cole watched the water for the shapes of them, the horned heads cutting the current, and he counted them in his head the way he had counted cattle his whole life — the way other men counted their children, he supposed, though he had had less luck with that arithmetic.

The steer died at the far side.

He was a big dun, three years old, and he came over strong and struck the bank and then turned back — nobody ever knew why. Water in his ear, a scare, the pull of the current. He turned and swam out into the middle again, and the current took him down toward the snag where the dead cottonwood lay in the water, and the snag took him under, and he did not come up.

The crew stood on the bank and watched. There was nothing else to do. A man did not swim after a steer in that water; that was how a whole crew drowned for the sake of one cow. Cole watched the place where the dun had gone under, and the river ran smooth over it, and he turned away.

The herd stood on the far bank, dripping, lowing, shaking themselves. But the trust was shaken in them. They did not settle. They stood humped and watchful, and the calves stayed tight against their mothers, and the brindle cow turned and stared back at the water as if it might follow them.

Joaquin read it. He said nothing, but Cole saw him look at the herd and then at the river, and the young man's face was still and old. Even the ones that could not be taught the crossing by fear had learned it now, and a herd that had learned fear was not the same herd.

Cole caught the gray and turned north. The far bank opened out ahead of them, the grass green and tall, the trail clear. They had paid the water, and the water had taken its one, and the drive went on.

But Cole carried the dun in his mind all day, the way the water had taken him down, quick and done, and he knew — because he had been at this work a long time — that a herd remembered the shape of its losses. Somewhere in that humped, watchful line of cattle there was a hole where the dun had been, and the herd would feel it for the rest of the drive.

Chapter 13: The Stampede

The shot came in the dark, and the herd exploded.

There was no warning. One moment the camp lay under the stars, the night watch humming, the herd bedded down and breathing in the great slow rhythm of four hundred animals at rest. The next there was a single flat crack from the trees to the east — a rifle, not a stick or a dream — and the herd rose as one body and ran.

Cole came out of his blanket with his gun in his hand and his heart already counting. The ground shook. That was the thing about a stampede — you did not see it so much as you felt it, the whole earth hammering under the weight of the running. Four hundred head of longhorn, and every one of them running blind, and the night full of the sound of horns and hooves and the terrible lowing.

"Ezra!" he called. The rifle hand was already there, saddle and all, his horse wild-eyed but steady. "East side. Whoever's out there—"

Ezra was gone. Cole did not see him go. He was on his own horse and riding into the dark after the herd, because that was the only thing a man could do — ride the shoulder of the running, get ahead if he could, turn them, slow them, any part of it. The horses did not need telling. They had been raised to this. They ran.

The stampede ran two miles and broke itself on the river. That was the mercy. The herd hit the Sweet Grass fork again — the same water that had taken the dun — and the water stopped them, and they milled and bawled on the bank, exhausted, blowing, and the crew came up around them and held them, and slowly, through the rest of the night, the trembling went out of them.

Two days of gathering. That was the cost. The stampede had scattered the herd across a country too big to see across, and the crew spent the first day finding them and the second day putting them back together. Some had run clear to the timber. Some had crossed the fork again, stupid with fear, and stood on the far side blowing and refusing to come back. Joaquin swam his horse across and fetched them, one bunch at a time, patient as the water itself.

Ezra came back on the second day with a story and no man. He had ridden east after the shot. He had found the place in the trees where a man had stood — the hoofprints of a tied horse, the bent-down branches, the empty shells on the ground. The man was gone. Ezra picked up one of the shells and looked at it a long time and put it in his pocket, and he did not say what it told him, but Cole saw him turn it over in his fingers that night by the fire, and then put it away without showing it to anyone.

Nobody said Wainwright's name. They did not need to. A stampede by accident was a stampede; a stampede by shot was a message, and the message was that somebody had decided the herd would not get there.

Cole sat up that second night while the herd settled, the tired animals standing humped and hollow-flanked in the dark, and he thought about the shell in Ezra's pocket and the saw-cut planking on the fork and the rider he had seen on the skyline, and he understood the shape of the thing they were driving through. The drive had wanted to be one thing — a testament, an old work done one last time. Somebody else meant to make it a war.

The herd's trust was broken now. It would take days to mend, if it mended at all. And Cole knew, watching them, that a herd that had been run once would always be waiting for the dark to crack again.

Chapter 14: The Blizzard

The blizzard came in the night, and it was the last of the mountain spring, and it was everything the country had saved.

The wind had been talking for two days out of the north, and the crew had moved the herd into the lee of the low hills and tied down what would tie. Even so, when the snow came it came like a door closing. Cole woke to a world gone white and featureless, the wind full of it, and the air so thick with snow that a man could not see his own horse's ears.

The herd did not run this time. They had been run, and something in them had learned the shape of terror, and now they stood — humped, facing into the storm the way cattle will, tails to the wind, letting the snow pile on their backs. It was the one gift of the blizzard: whatever it was, it was not a gunshot. The herd stood, and the crew held them by staying among them.

Cole rode the line with the gray, the horse leaning into the wind, and it was like riding through a moving wall. The single-wire telephone lines hummed overhead when he came near them, and he thought of the wire strung across the country, the sound of the new age singing in the storm, and he hated it and then he did not, because a man had no room in a blizzard for hating anything but the cold.

The crew rode blind. That was the truth of it. They worked by feel and by the shape of the country under them, and by the old knowledge that a herd in a storm would drift with the wind, and if you did not keep turning them they would drift into a draw and pile up and smother. Cole rode the point and turned them, and turned them again, and the hours went by like years.

Dutchy stayed with the wagon, and the wagon stayed with the herd, and the coffee was the one fixed point in the white world. Ezra appeared and disappeared out of the wall of snow like a shape out of a story. Joaquin worked the drag, and when Cole came around the flank of the herd he saw the young vaquero riding with a calf in front of him on the saddle, its legs bound, its head against his chest — one that had lain down in the cold and would not have risen again, and Joaquin had simply picked it up out of the white world and was carrying it, because that was the kind of man he was.

The storm died as it had come, in the night, and the morning was clean and still and terrible with cold. The herd stood crusted with snow, the steam rising off them in the hard light. Cole did the count with his eyes, the way he had done it ten thousand mornings, and the count came up one short.

The wind had taken a calf. Somewhere out in the white the small one had gone down, and the storm had covered it, and there was nothing in the world now but the snow and the shape under it.

Joaquin was already off his horse.

Chapter 15: The Lost Calf

Joaquin would not let the calf go.

It had been Ezra's find, and that was the strangeness of it. Ezra had come in off the line with the news — one short, the track lost under the fresh snow, no man could follow it — and he said the thing plainly and without judgment, the way he said everything. A calf lost in the storm. It happened. It was the price of the weather. No one blamed anyone. The crew stood in the cold and looked at each other, and the herd stood crusted and steaming, and the count was one short, and the world was white and still and done with the matter.

Then Joaquin had turned his horse and ridden back out into the white.

Cole watched him go. The young man rode the way he did everything — fierce, direct, without a word of explanation. He had been a boy on the border when the last drives came north, and he had learned the cattle country the way other boys learned to read, from the inside out, and there was something in him that would not let a thing be lost if he could help it, even a calf, even a small one, even in a world that had already decided.

Ezra watched him too, and Cole saw something move in the rifle hand's face — not surprise, exactly, but a kind of recognition. The two of them had come up different roads to the same country, and they understood each other in a way that did not need words.

Joaquin was gone half the day.

The sun came out, weak and white, and the wind came up, and the crew went on with the work of the living — the herd watered, the herd fed, the dead reckoned with and set aside. Cole did not watch the horizon. A man could wear out his eyes watching a horizon. But he heard the change in the herd's sound before he saw anything, the way a herd tells you things, and he looked up, and there was Joaquin coming in out of the low sun.

He was leading the calf. It was a small red thing, near dead with cold, walking with its head down, and Joaquin had got it up somehow — waded to it, got its legs under it, walked it in, slow, one step at a time, out of the white world that had tried to keep it. The calf's mother came out of the herd, lowing, and the calf answered, and they touched noses, and the small red thing went to the udder and stood there, drinking, trembling, alive.

The crew watched. Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that would have been enough, and so the men stood in the cold and watched the smallest life on the drive come back from the dead, and it was like watching a fire catch in wet wood.

That night by the fire, the calf lay in the circle of the light, curled against Dutchy's wagon, and Dutchy talked to it in German, soft, the way you talk to anything you mean to keep. And the herd, as herds do, accepted the small thing back into its number, and from that night on it was the herd's child — carried along, walked with, kept. It had no name. A name would have been a kind of closing, and the herd did not close.

Cole sat and looked at the small red shape in the firelight, and he thought about what it meant. A calf saved in a storm. It was nothing, in the arithmetic of a drive — one small animal that had nearly died and would be sold or kept, remembered or not. But there was something in the way Joaquin had gone out and come back with it, and the way the herd had taken it in again, that Cole filed away in the place where he kept the true things. Some things were not about the money. The longhorn herd was dying out of the world, and the drives were dying, and the whole of Cole's life had been spent watching things end. But this — this one small red calf, drinking in the firelight, saved by a fierce young man out of the white — this was a thing that had not ended.

The testament, he thought, begins with the smallest life.

And then he went to sleep, and the wind dropped, and the drive went on.

Chapter 16: The Rustlers

The rustlers came on a moonless night, and they worked the old way, quick and silent.

It was Ezra who heard them. The rifle hand had the second watch, and he came back into camp at the dead hour with his horse already turned, and he said four words: "Men in the herd." He said it flat, like weather, and the camp rose without a sound.

Forty head. That was what they took. Cole came up on the place in the dark and could feel the hole in the herd the way a man feels a missing tooth — the animals pressed close together, the place where the forty had stood empty and warm. The rustlers had cut them out quietly, a few at a time, hazing them off into the trees with a rope and a stick, working like men who had done it before, who knew cattle and knew the dark.

The drive answered in the dark. There was no plan and no council. The men of the crew split — Ezra taking the high side, Joaquin the low, Cole the middle — and they rode the way the old work was always done, by feel, by the shape of the country, by knowing that men moving cattle at night would be slower than they thought and louder than they wished.

The nearest man came at Cole out of the dark. He was driving a bunch of ten or twelve, hazing them with a coil of rope, and he did not see Cole until the horse was on him, and then it was too late for any talking. Cole hit him off his saddle with the flat of his arm, and the man went down hard and lay still, and Cole reined up and sat there in the dark, breathing, looking down at the shape on the ground.

Ezra's rifle found the second one.

The shot came from the high side, one report, flat and final, and the sound of it rolled down the slope and across the sleeping country, and in its wake there was a moment of absolute stillness — the herd gone silent, the night holding its breath — and then the running. The rustlers broke. They dropped the cattle they were driving and ran for their horses, and the sound of them going was the sound of men who had lost the night and knew it.

Ezra did not fire again. That was the thing about a marksman — he fired once, and he made it count, and he did not spend lead on a broken field. Cole found him afterward on the high side, standing over the second man, and the man was dead, or near enough, and Ezra looked at him without joy and without sorrow, and turned away.

The drive gathered back the scattered cattle through the night and into the dawn. Of the forty, they got back thirty-eight. Two were gone, cut loose somewhere in the dark, and the crew took the loss the way a crew takes a loss — counted, marked, set aside.

The two men they left in the country, dead and living. The living one Cole bound and turned loose at dawn with a message he did not have to speak. The man would carry it back to whoever sent him. That was the old way too — you did not kill the messenger; you sent him home with the news of what the night had cost.

Cole sat at first light and looked at the place where the forty had been cut from the herd, and he thought about the shot in the dark at the fork, and the saw-cut planking, and the rider on the skyline. The war was real now. It was not a suspicion anymore. The herd was being pulled at, a piece at a time, and the crew had answered, and Cole knew the answer would bring the next thing.

The country was quiet. The herd settled. The drive went on, thirty-eight returned, two gone, and the shape of the war drawn in the dust between them.

Chapter 17: The Night Watch

The rim rose above the camp in a long black shoulder of rock, and Cole took the last watch there because it was the quiet place, and the quiet place was where his thoughts went.

He had not been alone with the night long when the horse came up behind him. He knew the sound of the animal before he knew the man — Will's horse, the black with the white foot, the one he had seen cutting cattle on the skyline two weeks before. Cole did not turn. He sat on the rim with the herd a dark shape below and the stars coming up over the Beartooth uplift, and he let the man come to him.

Will reined up ten feet away and sat. The two of them looked out over the herd in silence, and the silence was the truest thing between them — twenty years of it, banked up like snow in a draw, deep and cold and older than either of them.

"Your outfit's got men out tonight," Will said at last. His voice had come up a long way from the eight-year-old boy Cole remembered, and the sound of it was strange and familiar at once, like a door he had known as a child and found grown. "Two of them, on the west flank. They'll pull back when they see me here."

Cole said nothing. The wind moved across the rim.

"You knew it was me," Will said. "Out on the Sweet Grass. The day of the gather. You knew before you saw my face."

"Yes," Cole said.

"Because of the seat." It was not a question. Will said it flat, and there was something in the flatness that was older than anger — a kind of worn-down thing, a boy's grievance carried so long it had worn a path in him. "You knew your own son's seat on a horse. After twenty years."

The words lay between them in the dark. Cole felt them land in him, one at a time, the way a man feels blows land when he has decided not to move.

"I left you," Cole said. "Before you were a man. There isn't a thing I can say about it that you don't already know."

"You left before I was a man," Will said, and the echo of it was harder than the first time, because Will was not repeating him — he was saying the thing he had been saying to himself in the dark for twenty years. "You left when I was eight years old, and you never came back, and my mother tended her grave alone—"

He stopped. The word hung in the air — Ruth, Will's mother, dead in 1905, buried by the boy she had raised alone, tended alone. Cole heard the break in the voice, small and old, and he sat very still on the rim and let it stand.

"I'm not asking you for anything," Will said. "I came to tell you to turn around. The herd won't get there. Wainwright's already bought the country past the pass — every spread between here and Millbrook — and his men are on the trail at the notch. You'll drive four hundred head into a dead end."

Cole looked out at the herd, the dark shapes below, the last of the Spanish breed in Montana. "Samuel means to drive them anyway."

"Samuel means to lose them."

"Maybe," Cole said. "But I'll take the job Samuel offered me, and I'll see it through, because a man's got to do one last true thing."

Will sat on his horse in the dark, and Cole heard him breathe, and the wind moved, and the stars came on.

"You were never there," Will said at last, and the voice was the boy's and the man's at once. "That's the whole of it. You were never there."

Cole had no answer for that. He sat on the rim with his son above him in the dark, and the silence was the truest thing between them, and he thought that silence was the only language he had ever been able to speak well, and that it had cost him everything he loved.

Will turned his horse and went back down the rim the way he had come, and the night closed around him, and Cole sat alone with the herd and the stars and the twenty years.

Chapter 18: Cole's Story

The fire burned low, and the crew sat around it, and for the first time Cole told the whole of it.

He told it plainly, the way he told everything, because he had learned that a plain telling was the only kind that held. He told them about the homestead in the valley of the Big Dry, the place he had taken up in the year of the grasshoppers, the house he had built with his own hands and the well he had dug and the wife he had brought to it. He told them about Ruth, and the boy, Will, born in the second year, and the way the place had looked in the good years — the way it had all looked like a thing that would last.

Then he told them about the drought.

He did not spare them the numbers. The summer of '92, the grass coming up thin and yellow and dying in the crust, the creek that ran to a string of mudholes and then stopped running, the cattle dying on their feet with their hides loose over their ribs, the ones he killed himself and the ones he did not, and the note at the bank that came due in the fall, and the shame of it — because it was the shame he was telling, that was the heart of it, the shame that had worked into him like a splinter and gone deep.

"I left in the night," Cole said. "Will was eight. Ruth was asleep, or I thought she was asleep. I saddled the bay and I rode out, and I told myself I was going to the Territory to make money and come back and save the place, and I knew in my heart — I knew it that night — that I was going and I was not coming back. I couldn't face them with nothing. I couldn't stand in my own yard and tell my wife and my boy that I had lost everything, and that the losing was my own doing."

The fire cracked. The herd was quiet in the dark. Nobody spoke.

"I made money," Cole said. "That's the ugly part of it. I made good money on the trail, and I made more on the ranch work, and every season I told myself I'd send for them, and I'd write the letter, and then I'd burn it, because the money was never quite enough and the place was never quite ready and the shame was always there, telling me to wait until I was something worth coming home to."

He stopped a long time. The fire settled. Ezra's face was unreadable in the light; Joaquin watched him with the still, fierce attention of a boy learning a thing he would need.

"Ruth died in '05," Cole said. "She raised Will alone, and she died alone, and I was in the Blackfoot country when I heard, three months after the burying. I came back then. I stood at the grave and I put my hand on the stone, and I looked at the boy — the man — standing there in his mother's yard, and I didn't have a word that would hold. I went back to the country and I stayed gone, because going back to her grave and to him and not being able to say the truth of why I left was worse than the going."

He looked up. The crew sat in the firelight, and the wind moved over the Sweet Grass, and there was no judgment in any face — just the quiet of men who had heard a hard thing told plain.

"Then I met Martha," Cole said. "And Martha was the second life I buried. And I thought I was done with the telling."

The fire settled to coals. Cole looked into them, and the road he had ridden for twenty years lay out behind his eyes, long and full of dust.

"That's the story," he said. "That's the road that became a lie. Now you know the whole of it."

Chapter 19: The Branding

The branding fire was lit on a still morning, and the old work began.

It was not a roundup day's branding, the quick and businesslike work of a working ranch. This was the old ritual, the way it had been done on the trails when the longhorns were young in the country — the fire laid in a bed of stones, the irons heating in the coals, the crew moving through the gathered calves with the patient, practiced rhythm of men who had done this work a thousand times and would do it, this time, with the weight of it on them.

Samuel himself came down from the wagon to work the fire. The old man moved slow, and his hands were not steady, but he would not be kept from it. He stood over the coals with the running B in his hand — the brand his father had used in the old country, the brand he had brought to the Sweet Grass in the '70s, the brand that had been on his cattle for forty years and would be on these, the last of them, before the world moved on.

Cole worked the head. The calves were brought to him, dragged in on the rope, thrown and held, and he took the iron from Samuel and pressed it to the hide and held it while the hair burned and the smoke rose, and the smell of burning hide hung over the camp — the old smell, the smell of the whole history of the cattle country, the smell that was being fenced and sold and wired out of the world.

The calves bawled. The mothers lowed from the edge of the gather, pressing in, and the crew worked through them, one after another, and the running B went on — on the shoulder, on the ribs, the old mark, the testament stamped into living hide.

Joaquin worked beside Cole, roping and throwing, and Cole watched him work and thought that the boy carried the old knowledge in his hands — the way he read a calf's legs before he threw it, the way he did not waste a motion, the way he knew cattle the way his people had known them for three hundred years, before there was wire or a county or a railroad, when the longhorn was the wild thing at the heart of a wild country.

Ezra held the gather, riding the edge of the milling herd with the quiet authority of a man who did not need to raise his voice. Dutchy kept the fire, feeding it, turning the irons, and he sang under his breath in German — a work song, low and old — and the sound of it mixed with the bawling and the smoke and the smell, and for a few hours the camp was the whole world, and the world was an old thing doing its oldest work.

The last calf was a small red thing — the one from the blizzard, grown some, thriving, the herd's child. Cole took the iron for it himself, and Samuel stood beside him, and the two old men worked together over the small red animal, and the running B went on where the blizzard had almost taken it, and the calf bawled and the smoke rose and the mark was made.

When it was done, Cole stood back and looked at the herd — the calves scattered back to their mothers, the new marks bright on their hides, the old work complete. He thought about the weight of it. A brand was a claim: this animal belongs to this man. But it was also a record — the running B went back forty years, to a German immigrant who had come to a valley at the foot of the Beartooths with nothing but a stake and a will to hold it, and the mark on these last calves said that the claim and the record ran together to the end.

The fire burned down. The irons cooled. The herd settled, and the smoke drifted off across the Sweet Grass, and the testament was made — stamped into living hide, four hundred head and more, the last of the Spanish breed wearing the running B into the mountain.

Cole looked at his hands. They were burned and blackened with the work, and he thought that it was the oldest work there was, and that a man could do worse than spend his last years doing it one more time.

Chapter 20: Wainwright's Men

They came in the late afternoon, and they came with a paper.

Cole saw them first from the herd — five riders coming down the long slope from the east, led by a man he did not know on a big roan. The man rode like a foreman, straight-backed and quiet, and behind him the four spread out in the loose riding order of men who had worked together, and Cole read them the way he read cattle, and he did not like what he read.

He called the word back to camp, and the crew came up, and they stood together as the riders came in — the five of them reining up at the edge of the fire, the man on the roan looking over the camp and the herd and the smoke of the branding fire with the flat, measuring look of a man who had come to do a piece of work.

"Luther Voss," the man said. He did not offer his hand. "Wainwright's foreman." He looked at the herd, the calves with their fresh brands, the smoke still rising. "Branding, is it? A man could say you're marking up property that ain't yours."

"State your business, Voss," Samuel said. The old man had come down from his wagon and stood at the fire, and there was something in the way he stood that Cole had not seen before — not age, not sickness, but a kind of settled weight, the way a man stands when he knows what is coming and has decided to meet it standing.

Voss took the paper from inside his coat. He unfolded it and held it out, and the paper moved in the wind, and Cole saw the seal on it and the signature and the dense black print of a legal thing.

"Bank note," Voss said. "You signed it in '09, Brandt, when you borrowed against the home range to carry through the winter. Wainwright bought it." He let the words sit. "The note's due, and the note's in his name now, and the note's secured against the herd and the grass and the improvements. I'm here to take possession. The herd's claimed for debt."

The crew stood very still. Cole felt the change in the air the way he felt a change in the weather — the way the men beside him went quiet and hard, the way Joaquin's hand moved a half inch toward his belt before he stopped it, the way Ezra stood without moving at all, which was its own kind of stillness.

Samuel looked at the paper for a long time.

Then he took it.

He did it slowly, the way a man takes hold of a thing he means to tear, and he looked at Voss over the top of it, and the old man's eyes were clear and cold and full of the forty years he had spent on the Sweet Grass.

"Mr. Voss," Samuel said. "I've seen men buy notes. I've seen men buy range and cattle and the loyalty of hired hands. I've seen your employer buy the county and the brand inspector and, I don't doubt, the judge that put his seal on this paper. You've bought a great deal of this country, Mr. Voss. But you have not bought me."

And he tore the paper in two.

The sound of it was small and dry in the wind — the sound of a legal thing dying — and Samuel tore it again, and again, and let the pieces fall from his hands, and the wind took them, and they blew across the camp and down the slope, little white scraps going to nothing.

Voss did not move. He sat on his roan and looked down at the old man, and there was no anger in his face, only the flat, measuring patience of a man who had done this work before and expected to do it again.

"That's a fool's move, Brandt," he said.

"Maybe," Samuel said. "But it's mine."

Voss looked at the crew — at Cole, at Ezra, at Joaquin, at Dutchy behind the wagon — and he gathered up his reins. "The note's still due. I'll be back with the sheriff and the paper in proper order, and the herd'll be taken lawfully, and there'll be nothing to tear."

He turned the roan. The four riders turned with him, and they rode back up the long slope the way they had come, and the camp stood silent until the sound of them was gone.

Then Samuel walked to the fire and sat down, and he was an old man again, and his hands were not steady, and he looked into the coals for a long time without speaking.

Cole looked at the scraps of paper blowing across the grass, and he thought about the shape of the thing they were in — the wire and the paper and the law, closing in from every side, the old world being fenced and signed and filed away. The herd stood quiet in the falling light. The running B was fresh on the calves. And somewhere out on the country, the paper was already being rewritten in Wainwright's favor.

But the herd was not sold. Not yet. And a torn paper was a thing the wind could take, but it was also a thing that said: I will not be bought.

Chapter 21: The Horse Trade

The stage stop below the pass was three buildings and a corral where the wagon road gave up against the mountain. The stage man was lean and spare with his words and his prices, and he walked around Bird twice without touching her.

"Fine horse," he said. "Too fine for this country."

"She was my wife's," Cole said. It came out plain, and he let it stand.

They needed pack stock for the climb and grain for it, because there was no grass above timberline. There was no money on the drive; Samuel had refused Wainwright's money and sent none with them. On the trail a man traded what he had, and what a man had was often all he was worth.

The stage man named a price. Cole named the stock he wanted and counted the grain in his head, and the two went back and forth the way men do over a thing that hurts, until the hurt was priced and paid.

"Mind you," the stage man said, "I'll treat her right. She's got the look of a horse that's been loved. I can always tell."

Cole nodded. He could not make himself say thank you. He stood in the barn door and watched the man lead Bird out across the yard, and the horse turned her head once, the way she had turned it when Martha called from the porch, and went on, and the light went with her.

The grain went into the wagon. The pack stock came out of the corral, four honest animals with no history in their eyes. Joaquin came up beside Cole and watched, and said nothing, because a boy who had grown up among the vaqueros knew a trade of that kind when he saw one, and knew better than to talk about it.

"The little horse," Joaquin said at last. "She was good."

"She was," Cole said. "The last thing Martha gave me. The last warm thing." He looked at the pass, white and indifferent above the town. "A man prices his grief when he has nothing else to pay with."

That night he sat on the wagon tongue with the cold coffee, and felt the loss of the horse the way he had felt the loss of Martha — a thing gone out of the country, taking a piece of the light. The wind came down off the pass. The last calf came up and butted his knee for the salt he did not have, and the small warm pressure of it was the only warmth left in the yard.

The drive had cost something, and the cost had a name, and the mountain was not yet begun.

Chapter 22: The Saloon

The saloon was the biggest room in the stage stop, which was not saying much — a bar of oak gone dark with fifty years of elbows, a stove in the corner, three lamps fighting the gloom. The crew came in off the cold and took the table nearest the door, and Cole saw the Wainwright men before he saw Will: three of them at the bar in matched dusters, and among them the quiet one, Voss, who had come to the camp with the bought note and had watched Samuel tear it, and who drank without appearing to drink, watching everything.

And then Will.

He was at the far end of the bar, apart even from his own outfit, a glass in his hand he had not touched. He looked up when the door opened and saw Cole, and the room went very still, the way a room goes still when two men who belong to a war meet on a floor where nobody wants it fought.

Cole did not cross to him. He stood where he was, his hat in his hand, the cold coming off his coat in the warm air of the room.

"Will," he said.

The word sat in the room. The Wainwright men went quiet, watching. Dutchy, who had started for the bar, stopped with his cup in his hand and did not move. Somewhere the stove ticked and the sound was enormous.

Will set the glass down without drinking. "Don't," he said.

That was all. It was the whole of twenty years in two words, and it landed in the room like a thing thrown and not caught. Cole looked at his son across the sawdust and the lamplight and saw the shape of the boy he had left, grown tall and hard and dressed in his enemies' cloth, and he knew there was nothing to be said that the room would let them say.

He turned and walked out. Behind him he heard Joaquin's chair go back, Ezra's quiet word to Dutchy, the crew following him into the cold because that was what the crew did.

At the hitch rail, Will came out and stood a moment in the light of the door. Neither man spoke. Will turned and walked down the road toward the barn where the Wainwright horses were tied, and Cole watched him go, and the wind came down off the pass and took the lamplight and the moment and the last of the warmth.

Nothing was resolved. Nothing had been said but a name and a refusal. But the name had been spoken, and the refusal had named the wound, and the two of them walked away from each other carrying the same sentence, and the mountain waited.

Chapter 23: The Confrontation

Will came up on the drag at noon, out of the timber on the high side, on a black horse Cole had not seen before. He did not look at Cole. He fell in behind the last of the stragglers and worked them quiet and clean, and the two rode the drag together the rest of the afternoon without a word.

The words came when the trail widened and the herd was strung ahead, out of earshot.

"I was eight years old," Will said. "Eight. You left me the winter chores and a mother who cried at night when she thought I was asleep, and you never come back. Not for the spring, not for the harvest, not when the bank took the homestead, not when Mother died."

Cole rode on. He had no answer that would serve.

"I dug her grave. I was twenty-one years old and I dug my mother's grave with a borrowed shovel because there was nobody else, and I stood in it and looked up at the sky and asked where you were. The sky don't answer."

"I know it," Cole said.

"She wrote you letters, every year, near to the last. I found them in the chest after she was gone — clean, never mailed. She never stopped writing to a man who never answered." Will's voice had gone raw. "I carried the name Hayden all my life. Men knew the story of the man who run out on his family, and they thought it was me. A brand I never asked for."

The herd moved ahead, patient as weather. Cole let the silence ride.

"You're right," he said. "Every word of it. I got no defense."

"Then what do you offer?"

"The truth." Cole looked at the white shoulders of the pass. "The drought took the homestead and I was ashamed of what I had become, and a man who's ashamed will leave his own blood before he'll let his boy see him broke. I told myself I'd come back when I had something to come back with. The years went by and the something never come. And Ruth died, and I weren't there, and I'll carry that to the grave."

He said it flat, the way a man reads a list. Will rode a long time without answering.

"I heard what you told the camp by the fire. It's the same story. It don't change the grave, Cole."

"No," Cole said. "It don't. It's the truth, and it's the only thing I've got left to give you, and it's yours if you want it."

Will turned his horse and rode off toward the high timber without another word, and Cole rode the drag alone the rest of the afternoon, counting the herd, and the counting was grief with a shape to it now.

Chapter 24: The Torn Map

At the foot of the climb, where the trail left the wagon road for the mountain, Cole took Samuel's map out of his slicker and unfolded it on the wagon tailgate.

It was an old map, drawn by a man dead forty years, the Indian trail marked in faded ink through the timberline and across the notch. Samuel had pressed it into his hand at the leaving, the way other men press a charm. The crew gathered around it. Ezra held the corners down against the wind.

The notch was torn.

Cole saw it before he said it — a clean, straight cut across the fold where the seam of the pass had been marked, the paper gone for a hand's width. Where the trail went over the top there was nothing but a hole and the raw edge.

"That's not age," Ezra said. "Age tears soft. That's a knife."

The men looked at it in silence. Joaquin studied the cut the way he studied a track. "Fresh. Someone has opened the pass off this map, and the road you cannot see on the paper is the road they want us not to take."

Dutchy looked up, his face going to that place it went when the past moved in him. "The little man with the paper. The one who brought the note to Samuel. He had his hands in the wagon while we all watched Samuel."

Voss. Cole thought it and did not say it. The quiet foreman, who had stood in their camp with the bought note and watched Samuel tear it, whose hands had been in the wagon a minute nobody remembered until now. The cut was a message: the map says the pass is gone, so take the low road, where we wait for you.

Cole folded the map and put it back in his slicker.

"We don't need it," he said. "The man who drew that map read the land, and the land's still here. A mountain you can read with your eyes is better than any map a dead man drew and a live thief cut."

Joaquin nodded slowly. "The old way. The country tells you the road."

"We read it the way the Indians read it, and the Spanish before them. The men who cut it think a torn map turns a herd around. They don't know the breed. These cattle crossed this mountain before there was paper on it."

The wind came down off the uplift and took the loose dust off the tailgate. The herd stood on the flats, patient, waiting on the men.

"The country tells you the road," Cole said again, half to himself, and he looked up at the pass, white against the blue, and began to read it.

Chapter 25: The Bear

The trail through the timber had been a trail for a thousand years, and the bear had decided, for that day, that it was his.

They saw him first as a shape in the gloom of the big timber — an old boar, enormous, humped, the silver of years in his coat. He stood on the trail where it narrowed between two slabs of rock, and he had not moved when the first cattle rounded the bend, smelled him, and stopped.

The herd backed up the trail like a river turning, slow and certain. The lead steer stood with his head up, and behind him the whole body of the cattle stacked and stalled, and nothing the point riders did would move them past the old boar.

Ezra had reined up on the high side, the Winchester across his saddle. He looked down at the bear, and the bear looked up at him, the two regarding each other with the patience of things that had been in the country longer than the other's kind.

"He's on the trail," Ezra said. "He was here first."

"He was here first," Cole agreed.

They made dry camp short of the trail, in a bowl of wind-shorn pines, and they waited. It was the old rule, and it cost them a day they did not have, and nobody argued it. Joaquin sat on a rock and watched the bear the way he watched the cattle: the age in its shoulders, the caution in its eyes, the weight of it that had come up from the low country in the spring and would go back down when the snow came — the country's own, asking nothing, taking its road.

"It is old," Joaquin said. "Like the herd. It does not fight. It waits for the road to open, because the road always opens for a thing that waits long enough."

The bear did not leave that day. It stood or lay across the trail, and the herd, downwind, lowed and would not be pushed. At dark Cole walked out past the picket line and looked at the bear, and thought about the old men who had shot bears as a matter of course, and what the country had been then, and what it was becoming.

In the night the bear went away. Cole did not see it go. He woke in the gray to a trail that was empty, and a stillness where the weight had been, and he stood a moment in the place where the bear had lain, the print of it the size of a man's whole life.

"Thank you," he said to the empty timber, because it seemed the thing to say to a country that had let them pass.

The herd moved up the trail, uneasy at first, then easy.

Chapter 26: The Pass

They left the timberline in the morning, and the country fell away behind them like a door closing.

The climb went up through the last of the stunted pine, then over the bare rock shoulders where nothing grew, and the wind that had been a rumor in the timber became a hand against every horse's chest. The air went thin and hard to breathe, and the cattle felt it, their breath fogging in the cold, their pace dropping to the slow walk of animals that knew the top was a long way off.

The herd strung out a mile. Cole had never seen it this way, the way the point riders saw it — a line of four hundred head drawn along the switchbacks, the old bulls first, then the cows and the calves and the brindle cow, and the last calf, the one Joaquin had carried out of the blizzard, walking at its mother's hip, small and certain. They went up the mountain in a thread of living history, and Cole thought: the herd across the summit against the sky.

Will was on the drag with him, and they worked it together the way two men work a rope, without looking at each other. A calf lagging; a tired cow gone down on the shale; the patient, endless pushing of the slow ones up the long grade. They did not talk. The work was the talk, and it was the truest thing they had ever done together.

At noon they reached the snowfields, the white lying across the switchbacks into July, the trail a dark thread through it. Cole looked ahead at the summit, at the notch against the blue, and felt the moment coming, the one Samuel had asked for, the one the whole drive had been for.

The lead steer reached the top in the long hour of the afternoon and stood against the sky.

Cole and Will rode up the last of the grade together, and the wind that had pushed at them for a day let go at the crest, and the world opened.

Below lay the far valley, green and small, the smoke of Millbrook rising thin against the mountains, the rails that ended there shining once in the low light. The threshold was crossed. The old land was behind them, and the new land lay ahead, and the herd stood on the top of the world, strung across the summit like a line of history drawn against the sky.

Will looked at it a long time. "There it is," he said. "The end of the road."

Cole looked at the smoke, at the rails. "It's the road that ends," he said. "Not the herd."

They sat their horses on the top of the world, father and son, and the wind moved over them, and the herd began, slowly, to move down the far side.

Chapter 27: The High Meadow

Below the summit, in the lee of the mountain, the high meadow opened — a green bowl of alpine grass that had slept under snow for eight months and come up sudden, bright, impossible. The herd spread across it in the long light and grazed, and the sound of it, the steady taking of grass, was the oldest sound Cole knew.

They made camp at the head of the meadow where a spring ran cold out of the rock. The wagon came up the last of the grade, Dutchy holding the brake, and the crew was whole: Cole, Joaquin, Ezra, Dutchy — and Will, who had ridden the drag in with them and had not ridden away, and who sat his horse now looking at the herd like a man who had forgotten what it looked like for a thing to be at peace.

Dutchy built the fire and cooked, and the smell of it went up the mountain. After supper, without being asked, he began to sing.

The song was German, old, a thing about a green hill and a house by a river, and he sang it low and true with the firelight on his face, and the men did not understand the words and did not need to. Joaquin had said once that Dutchy sang like a man singing a country back into being. He was singing it back now, one verse at a time, and the mountain held the sound.

When the song was done, Joaquin lay back on the grass and looked up at the sky, and the sky at that height was a thing a man could drown in, the stars come down so close they seemed to graze the ridgeline. He began to name them in Spanish, the old names, the names the vaqueros had used on the drives when the sky was the only map there was.

"The Drinking Gourd," he said, pointing. "And that one, the Cross — we call it the Palomino's Hoof. The old men said the horse it belongs to is still running, and the stars are the dust off its heels."

Ezra looked up. "The old men had names for everything."

"They did," Joaquin agreed, "and they are going out of the world one at a time, like the herd."

Cole sat on the wagon tongue and watched it — the herd grazing the grass, the fire, the men, the boy who was not yet his son's friend and the son who was not yet his, the mountain white above them, the stars come down. This, he thought, is the whole of it. Not the train, not the money, not the end of the era. This hour, held in the palm of the night.

Somewhere in the dark the last calf lowed, and its mother answered, and the sound went up the mountain and came back, and the high meadow held it.

Chapter 28: The Storm

The wind came up off the summit in the gray hour before light, and it was wrong.

Cole felt it before he saw it — the pressure in the air, the way the meadow had gone suddenly still and watchful. The herd felt it too. They were up and standing, heads high, the old bulls turned into the wind, and the lowing that had been the night's music had gone out of them.

By mid-morning the cloud came over the top of the mountain, gray and heavy, rolling down the headwall toward them, and the light went green and then yellow and then gone. The sleet came slanting, hard as shot, and then the thunder, a sound so close the ground took it and passed it up through a man's boots.

The herd broke.

Not a stampede — they had done that once, and the trust had been paid back in blood and days. This was worse in its way: a slow, terrified milling, the cattle pressing together, the calves pushed to the center, the old bulls turning and turning, looking for a way out that was not there. The whole body of the herd shifted like water in a pan, and any moment, Cole knew, the shift could become a run, and a run on the edge of a headwall was the end of them.

The crew worked them the way you work weather — not against it, but around it. Joaquin on the high side, talking to them in Spanish, low and steady, the voice the cattle knew. Ezra riding the far rim, a dark figure in the sleet, patient, holding. Cole and Will on the near side, turning the edges of the shift, giving the herd a shape to lean against, the way a man gives a frightened horse a shoulder.

The sleet laid a white coat over the meadow. The last calf pressed against its mother's side, head down, small, and Joaquin moved his horse close and stood guard over it without seeming to, the way he had stood guard over it in the blizzard that had made it the herd's child.

They held them. It took the whole of the day, the storm and the men and the herd locked in the white noise of the mountain, and at evening the thunder moved off down the far valley, and the sleet went back to snow, falling soft.

The herd settled at last, standing and blowing steam in the dark. The crew gathered at the wagon, silent, coats stiff with ice, and Dutchy poured the coffee that would strip paint, and nobody drank it yet.

Above them, in the dark, the mountain made a sound — a deep, slow groan, the sound a thing makes when it lets go.

Cole looked up at the notch, white and enormous in the night.

"Drink it down," he said quietly. "We may not have long."

Chapter 29: The Avalanche

The sound came out of the mountain like the mountain turning over in its sleep, and by the time Cole understood what it was, the snow was already moving.

It started as a crack along the cornice above the notch, a hairline in the white. Then the whole edge gave way, and the mountain came down — a white wall, silent at first, then roaring, a thing the size of a country falling. It crossed the trail below the notch in seconds, taking the timber with it, and the dust of the snow went up into the sky in a great white breath.

The herd had been standing in the meadow, half-settled, and the sound hit them like a shot. They broke — not the milling of the storm, but the real thing, the run, the whole body of four hundred head pouring toward the headwall, where the only way off the mountain went down, and down steep, and down wrong.

Cole heard himself yelling. He was riding, though he did not remember mounting, and the herd was going over the headwall in a brown flood, cattle pouring off the edge of the world, and there was nothing to do but ride the edge of it and pray the country did not push them all off.

And then he saw Will.

Will was ahead, working the edge of the run, trying to turn the leaders — a rider alone against the body of the herd. Cole saw him take the point. He saw the small brown shape of the last calf tumbling in the edge of the run, and Will's arm go out and catch it against his chest as his horse stumbled on the shale — and the second wall of snow came down the coulee, the loose snow the first slide had left hanging, and it took him.

It took him the way the country takes a thing that does not belong to it: without asking, without pausing, a white hand closed over horse and man and calf and went on down the headwall, and the place where Will had been was a smooth white tongue of new snow, and nothing moved in it.

The herd came to a stop below, blown and shaking on a bench of shale, most of them whole. The crew stood on the rim above the coulee, looking down at the place where the snow had closed.

"Will," Cole heard himself say. The word went out over the white and did not come back. "Will."

Joaquin was already off his horse and moving down the headwall, Ezra behind him, and Cole followed, and the snow was deep and cold and endless, and somewhere under it was his son.

He dug. They all dug. He dug with his bare hands and then with a shovel someone had brought, and the mountain held its breath and would not say where.

Chapter 30: The Rescue

The snow gave back what it had taken, the way the country sometimes does, in the long hour when the digging has stopped being work and become prayer.

Joaquin found the mark first — a small dark thing in the white, a hoof, moving. He went at the snow like a man digging for water, Ezra beside him, and they opened a hole and then a hollow, and Cole heard Joaquin's voice go up in Spanish, and the whole of the crew was at the hole, and the snow came away, and there was Will.

He was wedged under the overhang of a rock the slide had poured around, a pocket of air holding the snow off his face. He was alive. His lips were blue, his eyes glassy with cold, and in his arms, held against his chest the way a man holds a child, was the last calf.

The calf was alive. It bleated, thin and trembling, and Will's arms came tight around it, and the two of them lay in the hollow of the snow like a thing the mountain had decided to keep.

Cole came up to the hole and stopped. Joaquin and Ezra had reached his son first, and they stood aside, and the snow at the edges of the hole was red where hands had torn at it.

"Will," Cole said. He went down into the hole on his knees, and the snow took him to the chest, and he put his hands on his son's shoulders, and Will looked up at him, the blue in his lips going the color of speech.

"Did I lose him," Will said. "The calf. Is he—"

"He's alive," Cole said. "You saved him. You saved the smallest one there is."

Will's eyes closed. The calf pressed its head against his chest. The cold had taken the fight out of him, but the arms around the calf did not let go, and Cole understood what it had cost his son to hold that calf in the snow, and what holding it had cost the cold.

They got him out. Joaquin took the calf, Ezra took Will's weight, and they came up out of the hole into the wind, and the herd stood on the bench below, blowing steam, watching. One of the cows lowed once, and the calf answered, and the sound went across the white.

Cole wrapped his son in his own coat, there in the snow, and Will's hand came up and found his sleeve and held it.

"You were there," Will said. His voice was small, and cold, and true. "You were there."

Cole held him, and the wall that had stood between them for twenty years came down in the snow, not broken so much as accepted, and the mountain stood white above them, and the herd waited, and the last calf, the smallest life, stood between them and the cold.

Chapter 31: The Divide

The trail forked below the notch like a torn piece of string. One branch swung west and wide, following the easy fall of the land down into Wainwright's valley, a good road worn by forty years of wagons. The other dropped off the mountain's shoulder, steep and old, barely a scar on the grass, falling into the chute country no man had run stock through since the railroad came.

The crew sat their horses at the fork and looked at both, and nobody said anything, because nobody knew.

Will rode up from the drag. He had been riding drag with his father for three days, and the habit sat on him like a coat he had forgot to take off. He looked at the two trails, then at Samuel, and then he told them the truth.

He had scouted both trails for Wainwright. That was the work the promise was bought with. The safe road ran down through the home valley and out onto the flat where Voss's men waited — forty, maybe fifty, camped in the trees where the road widened. They were not there for the herd, not yet. They were there for the end of it.

The old chute, he said, had not carried stock in forty years. The grades were rotten, the switchbacks narrow, a slide of stone had taken half the lower trail the winter before last. It was a gamble a man would not take with a hundred head, let alone four hundred, let alone the last of a breed.

The crew was quiet a long time. Samuel said a man could read the land better than he could read a promise. Ezra said he did not much care for country where a rifle had the high ground on him. Dutchy said the wagon would never make the chute and was told it would be lightened, and he looked at his coffee pot the way a man looks at a horse he may have to shoot.

Then Joaquin said what Cole had been thinking. The old way had carried cattle over these mountains before any of them were born. The new road carried a man to a place he did not want to go.

They voted by the old silence. Nobody argued. Cole looked at his son, and something in him locked for twenty years turned over like a stone in the river. The boy had told the truth at the cost of everything he had been promised.

The crew turned off the wide road onto the scar of the chute, and the last longhorns in Montana followed them down a trail no cow had walked in forty years, into a country no one was waiting to meet them in.

Chapter 32: Will's Choice

They made camp that night in the mouth of the chute, the herd strung behind them in a fold of stone, and the crew ate Dutchy's supper without much talk. A man does not give up a spread easily, and every man there knew what Will had given up when he opened his mouth on the divide.

Will sat apart, in the light of the fire and alone in it. Cole watched him the way he watched a horse that had been frightened once — carefully, letting the animal come to him.

It was near midnight when Will came and sat down across the fire.

"I was to have a spread," he said. "Half of Brandt's range. Wire on it, and a house, and my name on the deed. Wainwright promised me. He said a man with the Hayden name —" He stopped. The name sat in the air between them, the brand neither of them had chosen.

Cole said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not sound like an excuse, and the boy had heard enough of those.

"I scouted those trails for him," Will said. "I mapped the forks and counted the timber and marked where a man could hold a herd against the mountain. I did his work for a year, waiting for him to put my name on the paper."

He lifted his eyes then, and they were his mother's eyes, and Cole felt the old wound open clean as a knife.

"You told the truth today," Cole said. "That is the hardest work a man can do — tell the truth when it costs him everything."

"It cost me the spread."

"It bought you the herd."

Will was quiet. Out in the dark the herd moved, a thousand hoofs shifting on stone, the low music of the longhorns turning in their sleep.

"I never had a spread," Cole said. "I had a wife, and I left her, and I had a boy, and I left him. I walked away from my life in the drought year and never found the road back. I have ridden other men's range my whole life, and I will ride it till I die."

Will looked at the fire a long time. Then he stood up, taller than Cole remembered, a man grown, a man Cole had not seen grow.

"I ride with you, old man," he said. He said it plain, like a man signing a paper, and walked off into the dark.

Cole sat alone by the fire with the words still burning in the air. He had lost the boy, and the boy had come back, and for the first time in twenty years Cole Hayden believed he might live to see the end of the road.

Chapter 33: The Ride Back

The pass made a man small, and it made a lone rider smaller still. Will left at first light, riding a remuda horse with a rifle across his pommel and a canteen that would run dry before the valley did. He would not take one of the crew. The herd needed every hand it had, and the word needed to go back alone.

The night before, he had told them what he knew. Wainwright had not bought Samuel's note for the herd alone. The paper reached further than the longhorns — it reached the range itself, the home place, the bench and the river bottom and the high pasture. The note was the hook, but the land was the catch. With the crew off the range and the old man gone, there would be nothing between Wainwright and the grass but a dying rancher and a man with a pen.

Samuel had turned back below the notch to sell the last of his saddle horses and raise money against the note — the only money he could make without the herd. He was riding home alone too, an old man and a bad heart, to face Wainwright's paper with nothing but horse money in his hands.

So Will rode the pass, and the pass held him a long time.

He rode up through the timber where the grizzly had held them, across the snowfields, up to the notch where the wind never stopped. He stopped at the top and looked back, and he saw the herd — a dark line on the gray of the old chute, moving slow, the last longhorns in Montana strung out a mile below him, going down.

He had given up a spread to ride with that herd, and now he was leaving it.

He was two days crossing the pass, and every hour the herd was behind him, and every hour he was alone. He spoke to the horse. He watched the snowfields go white in the evening and come back gray at dawn. He thought of his mother's grave, tended alone, and of the father who had been gone for it, and of the words he had said by the fire — I ride with you, old man.

He came down off the pass into the familiar country of the Sweet Grass, and the wind came up out of the home range and hit him in the chest like a hand, and he rode for the Brandt house through the long grass with the word he carried growing heavier the closer he got, because he knew what he would find there: a dying old man, and a paper, and a war he had just walked back into with nothing but a rifle and the truth.

Chapter 34: The Ambush

They came down off the chute in single file, the herd streaming after them like water down a sluice, and the timber closed over them as the grade gentled. The light went green and the sound of the hoofs softened into something muffled and wrong. Cole felt it before he saw it, the way a man feels a storm coming — the birds gone, the air holding its breath.

The rifle spoke from the high side of the trail.

Cole had heard a hundred rifles in his life, and this one was close and it was aimed, and the lead pony under Dutchy's hands went down in a heap, throwing the cook clear, and the wagon slewed and tipped on its side in the slash with the coffee pot ringing on the stones. The herd exploded — not forward, the way a frightened herd wants to run, but back, the way a trapped herd does, the whole line of them trying to turn in a space that had no turning in it. The horses went up. Somewhere a man was shouting, and the shouts were not theirs.

It was higher than Will had said. Cole knew that in the same instant he knew the shot had come, and he thought of his son crossing the pass: they were here all along. They were here before he was.

The crew came off their horses into the cover of the wagon, Ezra already in a crouch with the Winchester up, his face gone still and careful. Joaquin had the loose horses by the rope and was dragging them back toward the trees, cursing in Spanish, low and fast, a litany. Samuel had his old pistol out and was standing at the tail of the wagon like a man who had seen this country before and was not surprised to find it here.

The cattle were scattering into the timber, the last of the Spanish breed melting into the green, the sound of them crashing and lowing and dying away. Cole counted the cost without counting: horses down, the wagon finished, the herd broken and fleeing, and the high side crawling with men.

A second rifle cracked and a slug tore the air over their heads and thudded into the wagon box. The crew did not answer. They were pinned, and they knew it, and they counted their ammunition and their cover and their chances the way a man counts days of water in a dry country, and none of the numbers came out good.

Chapter 35: The Gunfight

The gunfight lasted a page, which is how gunfights last when men are not heroes.

The first rush came from the low side, three men working down through the slash to flank the wagon. Cole saw them the way a man sees the weather — all at once, and too late to do anything but the thing he was already doing. He put two rounds through the trees at the nearest man, and the man went down without a sound and did not get up, and Cole did not look at what he had done because there was no time to look.

The shooting opened up from the high side then, quick and vicious and wrong — the sound of men who had never been in a fight before and were afraid of it, and fear shoots high. The slugs tore the canvas and splintered the box. Ezra fired twice, measured, and two rifles went quiet on the high side, and the air between the volleys went dead for a heartbeat.

It was in that heartbeat that Cole saw the man come around the end of the wagon with his rifle coming up, and he saw that the man was not aiming at him. The man was aiming at Dutchy, who was on the ground at the wheel with his shoulder a ruin, trying to get his pistol out with the wrong hand. Cole shot the man. The man folded and fell across the wagon tongue, and the gun dropped and did not fire, and Cole's ears rang and his hands were shaking and he had not known he could still do a thing like that clean.

Then a shout came down the trail, and the fight stopped the way fights stop when something impossible happens.

Will came over the far ridge on the run, doubled back from the pass, two horses lathered and tied behind him, a rifle in each hand, and behind him the road he had come down empty was full of the sound of hoofs. The rifles from the Sweet Grass — the old Henry from the house, the Winchesters off the wall, a scatter of shotguns — they had come to the word he carried.

The men on the high side saw the ridge fill with riders, and they did the arithmetic of it fast and ugly, and they broke. The fight was over in the way fights end — not with a winner, but with the other side deciding they were done. Will swung off his horse at the wagon and looked at his father across the ruin of it, and there was a look on his face that Cole had not seen since the boy was eight years old, and had stopped believing he would ever see again.

"He's coming," Will said. "Voss is coming down the trail. He's coming himself."

Chapter 36: Ezra's Shot

Luther Voss came down the trail the way a man comes down a trail he owns. He rode a big roan with a sawed-off shotgun across his pommel, and he had stopped taking cover a hundred yards out, which told Cole everything about the man. He came the way a man comes to a settlement, with the full weight of a thing settled.

The crew held their fire. There was a discipline in that, and a grief in it too — the herd scattered in the timber, the wagon wrecked, Dutchy down, and here came the man who had ordered it all, riding into the ruin of his own work to see it finished. The men on the high side had gone quiet, the way hired men watch when the boss takes the field.

Voss stopped his horse in the middle of the trail, where the light came down and every man could see him. He did not look at the crew. He looked at the wagon, and at Dutchy on the ground, and then he looked at Will, and his voice carried up the slope like a stone rolling downhill.

"You're on the wrong side of this, Hayden," Voss said. "That spread is bought. That note is signed. You rode all the way back to hand it to Brandt and now you've handed it to me instead. Get your men out of the trees and I'll tell the judge you was just helpin'."

Will said nothing. He stood at the wheel of the wagon with his father's rifle in his hands, and the silence was the only answer a man like Voss was going to get.

Voss reached for the shotgun.

Ezra's shot came from the high side, from the rocks where he had gone when the firing stopped and no man had seen him go, and it came the way Ezra did everything — without hurry, without anger, without a second round. The single report doubled back off the far wall. Luther Voss went down off the roan in a heap and lay in the middle of the trail in the light, and the roan stepped over him and stood.

The men in the trees looked at their boss in the road and then at the high rocks where the shot had come from, and the arithmetic came out the same for every man there. They laid down their guns in the slash, one by one, and stood up with their hands empty.

Ezra came down out of the rocks and stood over Voss a moment, looking at the work of his hands without joy, the way a man looks at a thing he has done because it had to be done and not because he wanted it. Then he turned his back on it and walked to the wagon, and he did not look back.

Chapter 37: The Wounded

They carried Dutchy to the wagon and laid him in the shade of it, and it was only then, with the blood soaked through his coat, that Cole understood what had happened. The bullet had not been meant for the cook. It had come from the high side in the first volley, and it had been meant for Will — for the man standing at the wheel with his rifle coming up. Dutchy had seen it coming the way a man sees a thing he has been waiting for all his life, and he had put his shoulder in the way of it.

"That's a fool thing to do," Cole said, kneeling over him.

"That's what I told him," Dutchy said. His voice was thin and his eyes were too bright and he was smiling at nothing, which Cole did not like. "But the bullet, she did not listen to the cook. She come for the boy."

Will stood at the tail of the wagon, looking at the man who had taken the bullet that had his name on it. He had not said anything since he rode down off the ridge. He said it now.

"Why?"

Dutchy considered the question the way a man considers a hard piece of country. "Because you are the one they came to kill," he said, "and the wagon needed a hospital more than it needed a cook, and it is easier to make coffee than it is to make a son. Your father went twenty years without a son. I am not letting him go another day without one."

Will looked at Cole, and Cole looked at the ground, and the words sat in the air between them like something heavy no one was going to move.

They did what could be done. Ezra brought the wagon sheet and Cole tore it into strips, and Joaquin held Dutchy down, and they got the bullet out the way bullets were got out in that country — with a knife and a hot iron and a man's teeth — and Dutchy sang the whole time, German words Cole had heard a hundred nights at the campfire and never understood, a drinking song, until the ball came out and rolled on the ground.

The herd was scattered in the timber, the wagon finished, the cook shot, and the man who had done it lying in the middle of the trail with the light going down on him. The crew made camp there, in the ruin, because there was nowhere else to make it. They built the fire and hung the kettle, and the smell of Dutchy's coffee — the last pot he would make for a while, made by Joaquin under Dutchy's shouting — went up the mountain, and the last longhorns in Montana drifted back out of the timber toward it, one by one, like men coming home to a light.

Chapter 38: The Camp at Night

The fever came on at dark, the way fever comes in a wounded man, low and mean and patient.

Dutchy burned. They had him by the fire with the wagon sheet over him, his skin hot to the hand, his eyes open and seeing nothing, and he talked in German to people who were not there — a wife, a daughter, names Cole had never heard him say in a year of campfires. The crew heard it all, sitting around the fire where the shadows jumped, and none of them asked who the names belonged to, because every man there knew what a man talks about when the fever takes the walls down.

Ezra sat apart, cleaning his rifle in the firelight. He did it slowly, the way he did everything, and the men who had killed sat silent around him and did not look at each other. There was no talk of the shooting. There was nothing to say about it that would not have been a lie or a boast, and the crew was not built from either. The rifle went back together with a sound like a lock clicking, and Ezra sat with it across his knees and looked out into the dark where Voss lay, and no one asked him anything.

Cole and Will kept the watch together.

They sat on either side of Dutchy, the way men sit on either side of a fire they are both trying to keep alive, and they did not speak for a long time. The night wore on. The fire settled and they fed it. The herd lay down in the timber around the camp, and the sound of them breathing came through the trees like a slow tide. Joaquin sat with the remuda rope in his hands, watching the fire, saying nothing, a boy who had seen a man killed that day and was carrying it somewhere private.

The fever rose and Dutchy thrashed, and Cole held his shoulders down and spoke to him in the low steady voice he used on horses, and Will soaked the strips in the creek and laid them on the old man's head, and the two of them worked over him without a word between them, the way men work together when the words are all used up.

Near morning the fever broke. Dutchy's breathing went deep, his hands relaxed, the German names stopped coming, and Cole sat back on his heels and looked across the fire at his son, and the years were all gone out of the space between them.

"Tell me about her," Cole said. "Tell me about your mother."

And Will, who had carried the grave alone, began to talk.

Chapter 39: The Reconciliation

Will talked until the fire was down to coals and the stars went pale in the east, and he talked about Ruth — the way she laughed, the year the corn came up and the year it didn't, the way she stood at the door of the cabin the last time Cole rode out of the yard, and the way she stopped standing at the door after that. He told of the grave he had tended alone, and of the years he had measured his own growing against the distance of his father's absence.

Cole listened the way he had never listened to anything, taking it in the way a man takes in a country he has to cross.

Then, when Will was done, Cole told the whole truth at last. Not the story he had told around the fire on the Sweet Grass — the shame, and the road, and the lie. That had been the truth without the love in it. Now he told the love, and the failure.

He told of the drought year, and the ruined homestead, and the shame that had driven him out of the yard before he was man enough to face what he had left. He told of the road that had become a lie — each year meaning to go back, each year the distance growing. He told of Martha, and of how the two women and the two lives had been one long flight from a door he had walked out of in 1892 and never once turned around to close.

He told of the night he had seen his son on the skyline, cutting cattle back toward the rival range, and of the recognition that had gone through him like a wire — the seat of the horse, his own boy grown and strange to him.

When he was done, there was a long silence. The fire had gone to ash. The pass stood white against the sky above them, and below lay the valley and the rails.

"You were never there," Will said.

Cole said nothing. He had no defense, and he did not offer one. He had offered the only thing he had, which was the truth, and the truth did not argue.

The silence stretched. Then Will said it, and the words came out of him the way a hard thing comes out of a man, all at once and finally:

"You're here now."

And Cole sat in the ash and the dark with the words settling into him the way water settles into dry ground, and he did not move, because he was afraid that if he moved the words would not stay. The wound was not healed. It was accepted. That was all a man got, and it was more than he had hoped for.

Chapter 40: The Descent

Day came up gray over the mountain, and the crew broke camp in the ruin of the evening before, and the herd came out of the timber with the dawn, the last of the Spanish breed drifting back to the trail the way a river finds its bed. They were not all there. The crew counted them as they passed — the way men count anything they love, with the heart and not the eye — and forty head were missing, and the count sat in every man like a stone.

Dutchy rode in the wagon, or what was left of it. They had patched the wheel and packed him in the shade of the canvas like cargo, and he complained about the coffee in a voice thin as paper, which the crew took as the best sign they had seen in a day. Will rode point with the lead steer, and Cole rode drag, and the herd strung out between them down the last slope of the mountain.

The pass stood against the sky behind them.

Cole looked back at it once, the way a man looks back at a country he will not cross again — the white of the snowfields, the notch where they had stood at the top of the world. It had been the threshold. They had crossed it. What was behind was the old land, what was ahead was the new, and the herd was moving down out of the one into the other, and there was no going back, and that was all right.

The descent was long and gentle and good. The grade fell away, the trees thinned, the air grew warm with the smell of the valley. The herd walked with the old rhythm of the trail, and the hoofs of the last longhorns in Montana beat a slow music on the last road they would ever walk together, and the crew rode their places in it like men riding the last verse of a song they had known their whole lives and would never sing again.

The valley opened below them, wide and green and running with light, and far out in it Cole could see the thin dark line of the rails, the smoke of Millbrook going up against the sky, the pens, and the end of the road.

Samuel rode up beside him. The old man's heart was failing — Cole could see it in the color of his face and the way he held himself in the saddle, riding on nothing but will. He looked out over the valley, at the rails and the smoke, and his voice was quiet and steady.

"Well," Samuel said. "There it is."

And the herd walked on down into the light, and the pass stood white against the sky behind them, and the last longhorns in Montana came off the mountain into the new country.

Chapter 41: The Railhead

The valley of Millbrook opened below them in the last light, and Cole stopped the herd on the low bench to look at it the way he had looked at every valley these forty years — reading it, before trusting the stock to it. The town lay along the river in a scatter of roofs, and beyond the roofs the rails ran east and vanished into the plain like a pulled thread. The pens were peeled poles and new-cut lumber, the chutes climbing to the loading platform, and beside the platform a locomotive stood with steam idling, black and patient, the first Cole had been near since the Dakota yards in '95.

He sat his horse a long minute. Will came up beside him and said nothing, and that was enough. They had come to the end of the walking country, and both knew what the iron meant.

They brought the herd down through town in the morning, four hundred head walking the streets like they owned them, and the townspeople came out to watch the longhorns pass — the horns, the sway-backed Spanish lines, the colors no man could put on paper. Children stood on fences. It was not triumph they were watching, Cole thought; it was a kind of weather, a thing that came through and would not come again.

The agent was a thin man named Ferris, with sleeve garters and a ledger, and he met them at the pens with a manifest already written, the date certain set out in ink — three days, and the last cattle train went with or without them. Ferris was not friendly and not unfriendly. He checked the count against Samuel's letter, marked each number, and told them the chutes would be ready at first light. His fairness had a bluntness like a fence post, and Cole trusted it more than any warmth.

That night they camped beside the pens, the town's lights glittering beyond the slats. Dutchy made supper with his left arm bound and his right hand shaking, and would not let anyone help him. Ezra sat on the wagon tongue cleaning the rifle that had been clean since morning. Joaquin walked the fence line, talking low to the cattle through the poles.

Cole lay awake and listened to the breathing of four hundred animals in a pen built by strangers, under a sky crossed by the smoke of the locomotive. In the morning the telegraph key was already tapping in the depot, its sound running under everything like the wind running under the grass, telling the country the last herd had come down off the mountain and the door was still open. Three days. He counted them on his fingers in the dark, the way a man counts something he is about to lose.

Chapter 42: Wainwright's Court

The sheriff came on the second day, and he came with paper.

Cole saw the horse first, a big roan with a high head, and then the star, and then the men behind the star — Wainwright in a town coat, Luther Voss's brother at the head of a dozen riders carrying carbines, and a thin man in a black hat carrying a leather satchel like a preacher carries a Bible. They stopped at the pens, and Ferris came out of the office with his ledger and stood at the gate like a man counting freight.

Wainwright did not dismount. He unfolded the writ and read it out to the air, to the herd, to the crew standing in a line at the fence, and Cole listened the way he had listened to the flood years — water coming, whether you believed in it or not. The note. The bank at Bozeman. Samuel Brandt's signature in '09, the range and the cattle and the running B brand pledged against the debt, the debt bought now by the Sweet Grass Association, by which he meant himself. The brand inspector had certified the herd. The judge had signed the attachment. It was all there in the satchel, in ink, as neat and dead as a tombstone.

Samuel was not with them. He had stayed at the stage stop above town, too far gone in his heart to ride the last mile, and Cole stood alone at the fence with Will at his elbow and felt the weight of the paper settling over the herd like snow.

"I will not argue the law," Wainwright said. "The law is the law. Brandt had his chance to sell like a reasonable man." His eyes were not unkind, which made it worse. "You did fine work on the drive, Hayden. I'll see you have a place."

Cole said nothing. He could feel the cattle against the far fence, uneasy at the strange riders, the brindle cow shifting her feet, the lead steer watching with his old flat gaze. Ezra had not moved. Will stood very still, and Cole saw him look at Wainwright and then away, and understood that the promise that had held the boy lay on the ground now with the rest of the paper.

Ferris read the writ twice, turning the pages with thin fingers. Then he folded it and handed it back.

"This herd is contested," he said. "The railroad will not load contested stock without a clear title." He looked at Wainwright, then at Cole. "The cars sit on the siding until Thursday. Settle it before then, or the train goes out light."

Wainwright turned his horse and rode back toward town, the riders following, the sheriff last. Cole stood at the fence until dark, the herd lowing in the corrals like a slow bell, and he thought of Samuel at the stage stop, holding his bad heart, waiting for the end of the road to decide what it was.

Chapter 43: The Showdown

Thursday came in gray, and the town came out to the pens to watch the end of the argument.

By the time folks gathered at the depot platform the crew had the herd strung along the alley, the chutes ready, the sun not yet over the mountain. The locomotive was coupled to the cars, steam up, and Ferris stood at the scale house with his manifest open, waiting for a title no one could give him.

Wainwright arrived with the sheriff and his riders and the satchel, and he walked his horse to the head of the alley where Cole sat his grade horse, and the two men looked at each other across forty feet of trodden ground.

"Move your cattle, Hayden," Wainwright said. "The court gave me this herd."

"Samuel Brandt owns that herd," Cole said, "and Samuel Brandt isn't here to sign it away."

"Brandt's note is paid," said a voice from the road.

Cole turned. The last stage had come in off the mountain road, and a man was helping Samuel down — Samuel, gray as ash, one hand pressed to his chest, walking to the fence like a man walking to his own funeral with his affairs in order. He did not look at Wainwright. He looked at the herd, at the brindle cow and the lead steer and the thousand horns along the alley, and something in his old face loosened.

"Paid," Samuel said, and took a paper from his coat. "The note is paid, and the interest, and the court costs. My horses went this morning — the last of the Sweet Grass remuda, sold at Fairview to clear my name."

Ferris took the paper, and read it, and read it again. He went into the scale house and came back with the telegraph ribbon, the wire from the Bozeman bank, and he laid it on the manifest beside the writ, the two papers lying like the two ends of a broken stick.

"The brand records match the running B," Ferris said, "and the debt is satisfied. This herd is Brandt's, and Brandt says it ships." He closed the ledger. "The railroad loads a clean herd."

The sheriff folded the writ and put it in his pocket without a word, and the crowd on the platform made a sound that was half breath and half applause, and died out. Wainwright sat his horse a long moment, his respectable anger held up like a lamp with no oil. He looked at Will, and Will looked back, and there was nothing between them now but the road.

"Progress will finish what you started, Hayden," Wainwright said, and turned his horse.

Samuel walked the length of the fence, slow, his hand on the poles, and when he reached the head of the alley he stopped and looked at Cole, his eyes wet and bright.

"Load them," he said. "While I can still watch it done."

Chapter 44: The Herd Loaded

The loading took most of the day, and it was done the old way, and Cole never once hurried it.

Ferris's men opened the gate, and the lead steer walked the alley and set his feet at the foot of the chute and stood there, the old flat gaze considering the dark of the car. Cole sat his horse at the turn and said nothing, and the steer decided, and climbed — one step, two, the horn-tips tilting through the door — and the floor of the car boomed under his hooves. The rest followed as the herd always followed, the brindle cow next, the yearlings, the old cows who had known the open range before Cole himself was a man.

Joaquin worked the near side with a maguey rope coiled and quiet, talking to the cattle in Spanish, and the cattle listened the way they had listened to his fathers. Ezra stood at the far gate with the rifle in the crook of his arm, his eyes on the town road. Dutchy had come up to watch, his shoulder bound, and he stood apart and sang under his breath, a German song about a river and a girl, and the cattle at the gate flicked their ears at it.

Cole worked the alley all morning, cutting back the ones who balked, speaking in the low voice of a hundred gathers. He had done this work at the end of the Texas trails, a boy loading cars at Abilene and Dodge when the rails first came west, and the rhythm came back to his hands like a thing that had only been waiting — the gate, the chute, the dark, the boom of hooves, the count. One. Two. Five. Each longhorn that climbed into the dark was a page of the book the old range had written, and the book was going east to be read.

In the afternoon a big horned cow with a scarred hip balked and would not move. Cole got down and walked to her and put his hand on her neck and stood with her a long minute, the whole town watching, and then she walked up the chute like a good citizen and the door boomed closed and the count went on.

Samuel sat on the scale-house steps in the sun, wrapped in a coat against the wind, and watched every head go up. He did not count aloud, but his lips moved, and at the end he closed his eyes.

The last head went up at dusk. Cole swung the gate and dropped the bar, and the alley was empty of cattle for the first time in a month, the empty poles standing in the gray light like the ribs of a thing that had finished living. The train sat on the siding, full, patient, breathing steam.

"There's four hundred head of the last of them," Cole said to no one, and no one answered, because there was nothing to add.

Chapter 45: The Last Train

The whistle sounded at first light, and the whole town came down to the depot to see the last cattle train out of Millbrook leave.

Cole stood at the head of the pens with the crew, the five of them in a line, Samuel between Will and Ezra with his hand on the fence. The engine stood with a head of steam, black and trembling, the cars strung out behind it full of the long dark shapes of cattle, and the conductor walked the platform checking the manifest against the cars with Ferris at his elbow.

There was no speech. Cole had thought there might be, the way railroad men liked to talk, but in the end the whistle did the speaking. It let go a long cry that ran up the valley and came back over the town, and Cole felt it in his chest like a thing pulled out of him.

The engine took the slack. The couplings clanked down the whole length of the train, iron finding iron, and the cars began to move — slow at first, the way everything Cole had ever moved had begun, and then faster, the wheels taking up the rhythm, the smoke laying back along the roof.

The whistle sounded again, shorter, the way a man says goodbye to a dog he is leaving at the gate.

Cole watched the cars pass. Between the slats he could see the dim shapes, the horns, the moving hides, the last of the Spanish breed going east at forty miles an hour down a road that had no turning. The whole walk was in those cars, the whole country — the river, the blizzard, the calf, the pass — and the train was carrying the last of a way of being, and when it topped the far ridge it would take the sound of hooves out of the world.

The caboose came by, and the conductor raised his hand, and Cole raised his, and the train ran on and shrank and grew small against the plain, and then it was gone over the horizon, and the rails were empty and the valley was quiet in a way it had not been quiet all the spring.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The wind came up the valley off the Sweet Grass and moved through the empty pens, making the sound the pens had made all winter before the herd came — the sound of nothing waiting.

Samuel took his hand off the fence and turned, and his face was calm, and Cole saw that the old man had been holding something all these months and had finally let it go.

"The rails are empty," Samuel said. "Now we go home and see what's left of the country."

He started up the road toward the mountain, and the crew fell in behind him, and Cole looked once at the rails and once at the pass, and then he went after them, leaving the last train to the plain and the whistle to the wind.

Chapter 46: Samuel's Gift

They camped that night on the north side of the pass, and in the morning Samuel had the wagon brought up and the deed brought out.

Cole had expected the ride home to be a thing of silence and counting, the way any ending was, but Samuel had other work. He set up the cook's table in the morning sun, and on it he laid a sheet of paper as old and careful as the man himself, and he called them all to stand around it — Cole, Will, Joaquin, Ezra, Dutchy with his bound shoulder.

"I was a boy with a name I could not say in this country," Samuel said, "and I took a homestead on the Sweet Grass in the year of the Grant panic, and I have kept it forty years against the wire and the railroads and the men who would buy what they could not keep." He looked at the paper. "I have sold the horses and paid the note. I have nothing left but the land, and the land is not a thing a man sells."

He read the deed out, and Cole understood it in pieces, the legal words moving past him like water. The Sweet Grass range, the home place and the river bottom and the bench and the high pasture, given in common to four names he knew.

"I give the land," Samuel said, "because a man's testament is not what he says when he dies, but what he does with the ground he stood on. Wainwright wanted to break it and wire it. I give it to the people who worked it the old way."

He signed, and turned the paper so the four of them could see their names. Joaquin's name was written letter by letter, the way a man writes a thing he wants to get right. Ezra read his own name without moving his lips. Dutchy laughed once, high and sharp, and then was quiet, and Samuel put his hand over Dutchy's shaking hand and held it.

Will stood last, and he looked at his name a long time, working through it the way he had worked through everything — the anger, the promise, the ride back across the pass, the name he had carried like a brand. He looked up at his father, and then at Samuel.

"I had a promise from Wainwright of a spread of my own, wired and fenced. I gave it up on the mountain. This is different." He looked at the paper again. "This is not a promise. This is land a man can die on, and leave."

Samuel nodded, folded the deed, and handed it to Will to keep, and the weight of it passed from the old man's hand to the young man's and settled there. The wind came off the Sweet Grass and moved through the camp, and Cole stood apart and watched the four portion the country with their eyes, and felt the shape of the whole thing close around them like a door that had swung shut and latched.

"Come," Samuel said. "We'll ride the range tomorrow and see what it wants done."

Chapter 47: The Land Divided

They rode the home range in a long day, the four of them and Samuel in the wagon, and Cole at the drag like a man at the end of a last gather.

The Sweet Grass lay green in the June light, the snow gone off the low hills, the creeks running full, and the range looked the way it must have looked to Samuel in the '70s — empty, and full of possibility, and asking to be kept. They crossed the fork where the bridge had gone down and the herd had swum in the cold brown water, the planking laid again, new and straight. They rode the bench and the high pasture and the river bottom with its cottonwoods, and at each stop Samuel had them dismount and stand and look, and he named what he saw.

"The home place goes to Will," Samuel said at the yard. "It is the heart of the range, and a man who has carried his mother's grave alone all these years has earned the ground she lies in." Will said nothing, but his jaw worked, and he nodded once.

"The bench goes to Ezra. It is the high, quiet country, and it suits a man who keeps his own counsel." Ezra looked out across the bench a long time, and he said, "I'll run a few cattle on it," as if the words cost him something.

"The high pasture goes to Joaquin. It is the Spanish country, the short grass and the long view, and the old knowledge of cattle belongs on it." Joaquin stood on the rise with the wind in his hair and said, "I will learn it," in his careful English, and Cole heard the weight in the words.

"The river bottom goes to you," Samuel said to Dutchy. "There is good ground and good water and room for a garden, and a man who has cooked for the road all his life deserves to grow something in one place." Dutchy stood at the wagon, his good hand on the brake, and the laughter did not come this time. "I never grew anything but blisters," he said, and his voice cracked, and the wind took it.

Cole rode behind them all day and said nothing, because it was not his land to portion and not his name on the deed, and because he knew what the country was doing to them — settling them, one by one, into the ground, the way the range settled a herd into a morning's holding. He was watching the last gather he would ever see.

In the evening they came back to the yard and built a fire, and sat late around the flames the way the drive had sat every night for a month, only now there was no drive left to sit for. The first star came up over the bench.

"We are a nation of four," Will said, low, and no one laughed, because it was true and it was not the whole truth, and the fire held them all in its light.

Chapter 48: The Farewell

Samuel died on the ride home from the bench, as quietly as he had lived his last year, and Cole was there to catch him.

They were coming down off the high pasture in the long evening, the wagon creaking along the ridge road, and Samuel had been sitting up in the box with the reins loose, looking out over the country, when he made a small sound and leaned, and Cole, riding at the wheel, was there to take him before he touched the box. The old man's hand found Cole's wrist and held it with a grip that had no age in it, and he said, in a voice that was mostly a breath, "The herd is loaded. The land is given." And then nothing.

Will drove the wagon down off the ridge with his father's father in his arms, and Joaquin and Ezra rode ahead and came back with a blanket and a board, and Dutchy stood at the tailgate with his good hand pressed to his chest. They laid Samuel on the bench above the home place, in the grass where he had first stood and looked out over the Sweet Grass, and they built the grave by hand, in the old way, in the falling dark.

There was no minister in the country. Ezra said a long prayer in a voice Cole had never heard him use, words from the country he did not talk about, and the wind took them. Joaquin spoke in Spanish, the old words of the vaqueros for a man going to the long grass, and the firelight moved on the faces of the cattle that had come down off the hill to stand at the fence and watch, the brindle cow among them, patient as witnesses.

Cole stood at the head of the grave and did not speak. Samuel had been the first man in this country who looked at him and saw a whole man and not a hand to hire, and the weight of that sat on Cole now like a stone.

Will put the last of the earth down with his hands, and stood, and looked at the grave, and then at the range stretching away gray in the dusk — the whole of it lying open under the sky like a page turned.

"He got his testament," Will said.

They rode down to the yard in the dark, and the fire was still burning, and Dutchy put the coffee on with his one good hand, and no one said the word that was in all their mouths, and the wind came down off the bench all night and moved over the grave and over the yard, the voice of the country saying the only thing it had ever said, which was that it would go on.

Cole sat at the fire until the last of it fell in on itself, and he understood that the drive had not ended at the railhead or on the bench but here, in the dark, with the fire going down and the land given and the keeper of it gone into the grass.

Chapter 49: Cole Rides South

Cole waited until the first grass of the new spring showed green on the bench, and then he told them.

He said it at the yard one morning after breakfast, the four of them standing in the dooryard with the new year's work laid out around them — the fences Will was mending, the seed Dutchy was turning under, the little remuda Joaquin had gathered — and Cole stood with his bedroll tied and his saddle in his hands, and he told them he was going south.

"The land is yours," he said. "I am not made for it. I have been a hand on other men's ground my whole life, and a man does not change his nature at fifty-eight because the country offers him a chair by the fire." He looked at the four of them, and his voice stayed level. "There is a new generation coming up on the border who have never seen the old work done whole. Joaquin and I will go down there and do it in front of them, while there is still a man alive who remembers."

Joaquin stepped forward without being asked, his rope already coiled on his saddle, and stood beside Cole, and it was done — the old hand and the young one, going south to teach the dying work while it still had breath.

Will stood apart, and Cole saw the anger that had been the whole book of his young life rise and fall behind his eyes and settle into something else. Will came forward and put out his hand, and Cole took it, and they held each other's grip the way they had in the dark of the camp at the pass, and nothing more needed to be said.

"Go teach them," Will said. "The cattle will know the way."

Dutchy would not look at them. He stood at the wagon with his good hand busy with nothing, and when Cole came to him he said, "I have cooked for a thousand miles of road, and now I cook for a garden," and he laughed his sharp laugh, and it cracked, and Cole clasped his shoulder and said nothing, and the wind took the rest.

Ezra came last, and the two men stood a long moment, and Ezra said, "There is a rifleman's truth: a man's work is to keep the one beside him alive." He looked south. "Ride safe. The country will be here when you're done."

Cole mounted, and Joaquin mounted, and they turned the horses down the road that ran south out of the yard, and Cole looked back once at the home place where Will stood in the dooryard, and once at the pass standing white above the range, and then he rode on, and did not look back again.

The wind came down off the Sweet Grass and moved through the empty yard, and Will stood watching until the two riders were small against the green country and then gone over the first ridge, and the range lay open around him under the wide sky, given and kept and beginning its new year.

Chapter 50: The New Generation

A year later, in the first spring of the divided land, Will Hayden stood on the bench above the home place and looked out over the Sweet Grass the way Samuel had forty years ago, and the wind moved through the grass in long silver waves, and he understood, standing there, the thing his father had understood and had not been able to say.

The fences he had built the year before were mended and holding. The cattle Ezra ran on the bench were fat and quiet, a good mixed herd now, the last of the longhorns standing among the Hereford cross, the old blood still holding the range. Dutchy's garden in the river bottom was in, the rows green and straight, and on the fence by his door hung a string of peppers and a bottle never opened and never thrown away. Joaquin's high pasture ran a hundred head of the Spanish stock he had brought up from below, the brand a running B over a newer mark.

And in the home-place corral stood the last calf — grown tall now, a yearling with the long legs and the high horns of the old breed, the one Joaquin had ridden back into the blizzard to save, the one Will had carried down off the mountain. It stood at the fence and watched Will the way it had watched the whole world since the storm, patient and unafraid, the testament's seed, still on the land.

Will had a letter in his pocket, read three times, and he unfolded it and read it a fourth time in the wind. The paper was creased and travel-worn, written in Cole's careful hand, posted from a town south of the line Will had never heard of and never would visit. It said the border country was green, that the young vaqueros were learning the old ways faster than the old man could teach them.

There was more, in the way a father writes to a son he is learning to know — the trails, the weather, the price of horses — but the last lines Will read twice, standing on the bench with the wind around him.

"I taught the new generation what I knew," Cole had written. "What they do with it is theirs. The pass is white against the sky, and the wind is on the Sweet Grass, and I am thinking of you standing where Samuel stood."

Will folded the letter and put it in his shirt, and he looked at the pass standing white above the range, and down at the corral where the last calf stood watching, and out over the divided land, the four portions of it working under the wide sky, and he felt the shape of the whole thing close around him like a door that had swung shut and latched on a room full of light.

He stood a long time on the bench, and then he went down to the yard to do the day's work, and the wind moved over the Sweet Grass behind him, the country's voice saying the only thing it had ever said — that it would go on, and that the going was the testament.
