The Amber Loom
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Loom That Stood Dark

Ilsa woke to frost on the inside of the window, flowers of it, the glass furred white along the sill. She lay still a moment, breathing the cold, and watched the rowan tree in the yard. The buds had not opened in three years. They hung there like shut fists, brown at the edges, as if the tree had forgotten what spring was for. She knew the exact weight of that silence now, the way you know a worn tread in a stair.

The summons came before light, through the wall: Greta's knock, three times, hard, the way she knocked when the wool would not take the dye. Ilsa dressed in the dark, the shawl across her shoulders smelling of lanolin and smoke, and stepped out. The village lay under a grey fur of frost, roofs steeped in shadow, and there was no bird singing.

The Weaving Hall was the largest building in Brekkelund, and it stood dark. Inside, the hearth burned low and the lamplight moved on the walls. Greta was there, waiting, one-handed, her sleeve pinned where the hand had been. She did not speak at once. She stood looking at the great loom that loomed against the far wall, twelve feet of dark, old wood, its frame blackened by generations of smoke, and the thread-sound of the empty room: the hum of wool under tension, the creak of a frame under its own weight.

"Third cold spring," Greta said. "The buds have not opened. The well runs grey at the edge of the village, and the lambs are born still. The Hall has stood dark long enough." She turned, and Ilsa saw the whole weight of the village's fear in her face: the sleeplessness, the guilt, the years of it. "The great loom must be threaded at last. The Winter-Waking must be woven this year, Ilsa, or the village will unravel entirely, thread by thread. You have seen the grey at the edges of the people's faces."

Ilsa looked at the loom, standing dark against the wall. It seemed to her not an object but a held breath, a thing waiting. She had grown up in this Hall, had learned to spin at her grandmother's knee, and she knew every inch of the frame: the beam, the heddles, the treadle worn smooth as bone. And she knew that a loom with no warp is a door with no room behind it at all.

"Someone must weave," Greta said. Her voice was low, and the lamplight shook on the wall. "Anke is dead, and the loom knows it."



Chapter 2: Greta's Hand

The warp of the great loom was dressed in the dark, and Greta dressed it one-handed.

She worked the reed and the heddles with her right hand, her left sleeve pinned flat where the hand had been, the cloth folded and tucked. Ilsa watched the empty side of her lean into the frame, how she balanced a whole loom on half of herself. The hearth burned low. The thread-sound of the warp was the only voice in the Hall, a long hum under tension, and the smell of lanolin and smoke lay over everything like a second skin.

"The loom takes what it is given," Greta said, not looking up. She said it the way she said everything about that year — in fragments, the way you pick at a snagged thread rather than pull it. "In the year of the lock it took more than a hand from some. It took whole people from their families, strand by strand, and they did not know they were being taken."

"From some," Ilsa said. "What did it take from Anke?"

Greta's hand stopped on the warp, the pin in her sleeve catching the lamplight. She said nothing, and the thread-sound filled the space where an answer would have been.

"She gave it everything," Greta said at last, and said no more.

They dressed the warp together, one hand teaching and two learning — which thread over which, which shed for which color, how the year hung on the loom like cloth waiting to be made. The Hall was cold even at the hearth. Through the keyhole of the inner door Ilsa glimpsed the cold room where the grey people were kept through the winter, in case the thaw should re-weave them. She saw lamplight on frost and the slow rise and fall of cloth that might have been breathing.

"Every warp needs one thread of the old pattern," Greta said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went when she kept something. "The last of Anke's wool. One thread of her spinning, so the new year remembers the old. Without it the loom will not hold."

"Where is it kept?"

"The stores. The Thread House." Greta did not look at her. "Go and find it. You will know it when you touch it."

Ilsa stood in the doorway of the Hall with the night coming down, the frost writing on the glass, and she understood she had been given a task that was also a test. Somewhere in the dark of the Thread House, among the grey and thinning spools, a single thread of her grandmother's spinning was waiting — and the whole weave would rest on it.



Chapter 3: The Amber Thread

The Thread House smelled of dust and old lanolin, of things not touched. Ilsa let the door fall to behind her and stood a moment in the dark of it, letting her lamplight settle. Spools lined the walls in their racks, grey and thinning, each on its peg like a row of half-sleeping things. The winter had been feeding here longest of all.

She moved down the racks, reading the name-tags with her fingers; the dust had eaten the letters first. The wool of three years hung on them like breath held too long. Every spool she touched was cold to the fingertips, cold as the frost in the thatch, cold as the well running grey.

Then, at the far end of the rack, in the last cubby, beneath a drift of dust so deep it had begun to weave itself into cloth, she found a spool she did not have to touch to know.

The amber thread wound tight, round and small as a knuckle of honey, and it was warm. Warm the way a hearth is warm after someone has left the room. Warm the way a hand remembers. She lifted it, and the warmth came through the dust into her palm, and under her fingers the thread gave a faint hum, a half-song, the rhythm of a treadle someone had stopped counting.

Beside it lay the skein-box, and she opened it, and there was her own birth-thread, a length of grey-white wool spun the night she was born, tagged with her name in her mother's hand. She had not known the tag survived. She touched it, and it was cold, and the cold was not the room's cold but the cold of a thing that had been waiting.

She wound the amber spool into the pocket of her shawl and carried it out through the village, and only then, among her neighbors, did she see what she had walked past every day of three years. The miller's sister stood at her doorway and did not see her. Old Hal's face at the edge of his beard had gone thin as cloth, the look that comes to a hem before it lets go. A child in the lane, running, had one grey hand.

The amber burned warm against her ribs the whole way.

Greta was at the loom when she came in, one-handed at the warp, and she saw the spool in Ilsa's hand before Ilsa could hide it, and went white, white as the frost, white as the dust, white as the well.

"Not that one," Greta said, voice a thread pulled too hard. "Not her color."



Chapter 4: The Weaving Hall

The Hall held its breath in the lamplight. Twelve feet of dark wood rose against the far wall, the Great Loom's beams bare as winter branches, the whole weight of the village standing in its frame like a warp under tension. Greta dressed the sheds one-handed; the thread-sound was a held breath, wool drawn, wood creaking, a year waiting.

Ilsa knew the rite from Anke's kitchen, but to stand inside it was different. Every year Spring was woven over Winter, thread by frost thread covered in green, and the year hung on the warp — the thaw, the lambing, the harvest, cloth coming off the beam. The loom was both cage and loom, Greta said: what it held it made, and the village was held by the frame that shaped it.

The Elders came in through the long shadow of the door: Maren first, the eldest, the shearing knife in its wool-wrapped sheath; then Ulf, then Sina. They stood at the hearth watching Greta dress the loom. Maren's knife had never cut a living warp. It was a mercy, the village said, that it never had.

Dusk gathered at the windows, and Ilsa saw them — the grey people at the edge of the light, faces like cloth gone threadbare, humming. It came thin through the frosted glass, an unfinished loom-song she felt in her throat like a word she could not finish. No one looked up. It was the grey ones' way, to wait at the edge of what the living held.

Greta wound the pattern onto the beam; Ilsa saw the winter whole: the three locked years as one long amber bolt, warm and humming, the color she had carried from the Thread House, the Locked Warp that should have ended, wound and never finishing. She understood a warp of forty threads refusing to end. The year hung on this; the hanging was the village; it was fraying.

"The threading begins at dawn," Maren said. "Spring will be woven over the amber, or the cold room will be opened for good." Greta's hand stilled on the heddle. "Who holds the shuttle?" "Her." Maren's gaze found Ilsa. "She is the weaver's apprentice. She has held the amber. Let her hold the year."

Ilsa's throat closed. The loom stood above her, both cage and loom, the grey people humming at the windows, the beam holding the locked winter like a kept hour. In Greta's face was the whole weight of the village's fear, and a hope unspoken. "Come at first light," Greta said. "The loom knows your hands." Ilsa looked at the amber bolt, said nothing, felt the year settle on her shoulders like a shawl of frost, carried it home through the dark to the grey people's unfinished song.



Chapter 5: The Grey People

The dusk came down over the lane like a strand drawn tight, and Ilsa heard the humming before she saw her — a loose, unfinished sound, the way the grey ones try to remember a song and cannot hold it.

The woman stood where the lane met the shepherd's common, half in the last of the light, half out. Ilsa knew her by her hands: the miller's sister, grey since the second year of the lock, at the well. Her face was threadbare — the features not gone but worn, like cloth washed until the pattern shows through faint, the colors pale at the edges.

"Girl," the woman said, her voice the thread-sound, the hum of wool under tension. "Girl, does the mill still turn?"

The past tense. She spoke of the mill, of herself, as finished, folded, put away. Ilsa felt the cold of it like frost on the inside of a window — not the weather, but the half-light of the half-lived, where everything was remembered and nothing was being lived.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "The wheel turns."

She did not know if it was true. But the lie came out of her warm and wanted, and the woman's threadbare face loosened — a little, a very little, like a knot easing.

"The wheel," the woman repeated, tasting the word. "I was the one who dressed the stones." Past tense. "I was the one who carried the flour up in my shawl." She touched her chest, where the shawl would have been, and found nothing. "I was the miller's sister. I was."

Ilsa wanted to take her hand — she had taken her mother's hands going threadbare in the first cold spring — but she was afraid of finding nothing where the warmth should be, of losing her own fingers in it.

The woman looked past her toward the lamplight in the windows, and began to hum.

It was a loom-song, the one about the flock coming down from the hill; every child in Brekkelund knew it. She sang its first line, the one that winds the memory of the fleece, and it came out true and clear, almost whole.

Then it stopped.

Her mouth stayed open on the missing note, and her face went threadbare again, and she lowered her eyes as if caught at something shameful.

"I was a good singer," she said, in the past tense. "I was."

She drifted toward the edge of the village, where the grey people gather at the Hall windows at dusk, humming their unfinished songs. Ilsa stood in the cold lane and watched her go, the song hanging in the air after her like a loose thread, and understood the lock's true cost: not a frozen season, but a life gone to the past tense, unable to finish its song.



Chapter 6: Anke's Pattern

The drawer under the great loom was the one thing in the Hall that was not locked. Greta had said so once, offhand, the way she said anything she did not want to be asked about. It opened on a smell of lanolin and old smoke, and inside were the pattern books.

Ilsa lifted them out one by one. They were not books but boards, the weaver's drafts, the records of every year Anke had woven, each a small map of a season: forty threads wide and as many long, every warp and weft marked in faded ink. She turned the boards over in the lamplight, and the years came up out of them like faces from a fog. Here was a summer whose green she could nearly feel, the dye so deep the board was warm under her fingers. Here a winter, white and tight, forty threads of cold held fast. She had lived in those years, small and barefoot, and the boards knew it better than she did.

At the bottom of the drawer lay the last book, the Amber Year. It was newer, its ink still black, its warp drawn bold, and it was wrong. Ilsa stared at it a long while before she saw the shape of the wrongness. Every pattern Anke had ever woven had a place for the weaver: a mark where the shuttle turned, the small figure of the hand that held it. This board had none. In the middle of the amber warp, where a woman should have stood, the design simply stopped, a gap shaped like a body, empty.

Ilsa remembered Anke teaching her to spin, her grandmother's hands steady on the distaff, the wheel a soft complaint in the cold room. "Weave the thread you were given, child," Anke said, "never the one you wanted." She had said it the way people say things they mean to be remembered by. Her hands had been warm then, covered in the smell of lanolin, and when she pressed the yarn to Ilsa's cheek she had laughed.

There was no laugh in the board. The gap held the shape of the woman who should fill it, and Ilsa understood, all at once, with a cold that had nothing to do with the year, that Anke was not buried only in the hill. She was in the pattern, holding it, the way a hand holds a door against a wind. The board was not a map of a season. It was a plan of her grandmother's body, threaded and warped on wood, and the empty place where a weaver stood was the only part of it that was free.

The amber thread in Ilsa's pocket hummed, once, warm as a pulse.



Chapter 7: The Cold Spring

The village counted the Locked Warp in lambs born still, in the rowan with buds shut tight as a fist, in the well at the edge that ran grey. Three years now. A warp of forty threads should have ended long ago; instead the amber held, and the village kept what it kept: the last of the hay, winter rations counted out like wool from a dwindling skein, the cold room in the Hall filling slowly with the grey, who were not dead and could not be buried.

Her mother had died in the first cold spring. Ilsa kept her hands, not as the village kept its harvests, but how they went threadbare — skin thinning over the knuckles until lamplight shone through the fingers like light through a worn shawl. In her last week her mother held Ilsa's wrist with a hand half light and said her name twice, as you say a thing to remember it. Ilsa remembered. She remembered lanolin even when there was no wool in the room — there was none; there was nothing left.

The village said the first year was famine, the second forgetting, the third grey. By the third year the well ran grey and no one drank from it, though none could say when the drinking had stopped. The grey people stood at the Hall windows at dusk, humming their unfinished songs, and the great loom stood dark as Anke had left it.

Ilsa sat in the Thread House with the amber spool in her lap. Forty threads in a warp; Anke had woven forty and the pattern swallowed the weaver whole. She turned it over; the amber was warm, not the dry warmth of a kept room but the living warmth of a pulse, of something that remembered. Every other thread in the skein-box was cold, her own birth-skein cold. Only the amber kept its heat, as if still being spun, as if the hand that spun it had never let go.

Outside, frost stood on the inside of the window. It had stood there since the third year, never quite melting, never quite spreading, a held winter in miniature. Ilsa pressed the amber thread to the pane, the way you hold a hand out to see whether the cold is real.

And just there, where the thread touched, the frost let go. A single clear circle melted in the pane, the size of a coin, the size of an eye, through it she saw the rowan, buds shut tight, and for a moment the melting spread, one warm breath reaching out from the locked color, and then stopped, as if the winter had remembered itself.



Chapter 8: Tobin's Shorn Fleece

The fleece came into the Hall on a grey morning, slung over Tobin's shoulder like a shorn season, and Ilsa smelled the flock on him before she saw him: lanolin, and the cold smoke of the shepherd's fire, and underneath, the small warm thing that was his laugh, still beating.

"Hall-fee for the Trust," he said, and set it down with both hands, careful, as if it were the offering. The year's best, and he had brought all of it. The shearing came early in a locked year.

He teased her about the loom, called it her tall suitor, asked whether the great dark frame had courted her yet. He was the only one left who could still make such a joke land. Ilsa laughed, a thin clear sound in the Hall's quiet.

But her hand was already on the fleece, and the fleece was grey.

Not the honest grey of un-washed wool, a cloud's belly — this was the grey of the well at the village edge, the grey that ran through the Locked Warp, the grey she had watched creep across the miller's sister's face. She lifted a strand and it came away in her fingers like something already un-spun, and carried no warmth at all.

"Tobin," she said.

He turned to her in the lamplight, and there it was, the streak she had missed: a run of grey at his temple, thin as a warp thread, going down into the dark of his hair. And his shadow on the wall was thinner than his body, like a cloth cut down to save the pattern.

He caught her looking. His laugh came again, smaller. "It's the cold," he said. "The flock's thinned too. They'll fleece back come summer."

She hid her fear. She put the grey fleece away in the Trust chest, her hands steady because she would not let them shake in front of him. She walked the flock home with him through the grey streets, and he sang half a loom-song — the one that counts a year as a warp, forty threads and a long winter's weft — and stopped.

"I've forgotten the rest," he said, laughing again, too quick.

They stood where the path to the shepherd's common began, and the dusk came down grey on the roofs. He had forgotten the rest of the song. But he remembered her name — said it once, Ilsa, testing, whole — and that was what she carried back through the closing dark.

She turned at the Hall door to watch him go, and at his heels the grey of the path was rising like a tide, licking up around his boots, and he walked on through it, unseeing, still humming the song he could not finish.



Chapter 9: The First Unraveling

The great loom's shed stood open, the warp hanging like a held breath. Ilsa set her foot on the treadle, the shuttle cold in her hands, the Spring colors wound on the bobbins—a green from the Hall's last moss-wool, a blue from the river. The amber lay beneath the pattern, warm against the frame, humming its low half-song, and the whole weight of the village hung from the beam.

Greta had barred the doors. The Elders had gone home. Only the hearth fire, and the two of them, and the thread-sound of the room.

Ilsa took the first pass. She beat the weft into place, the shed closing over the amber like a lid, and somewhere in the village a door stopped latching.

She felt it in her chest first, a small fraying, a thread pulled loose behind her ribs. She paused with her hand on the heddles. The frame creaked once, a note she almost knew.

"You felt it," Greta said. Not a question.

Ilsa wove the second pass, green over amber, and the room's pitch changed—in a house a name rose to a lip and was gone, and a woman looked at her own hand and could not say who had spun it.

The fray pulled deeper. Ilsa's breath went thin, and she understood, with the cold certainty of frost on glass, what the loom was: both cage and loom. What it held, it made. Every pass she wove was a year, and this year was wrong.

She let the shuttle fall.

Through the high window the village showed its edge: a strand of thatch had lifted from a roof like a loose end; the miller's door stood ajar and would not shut; and down the path a woman walked to the Hall trailing her own name like a grey thread.

Greta took the shearing knife from its hook. Its edge caught the lamplight, grey as a shorn fleece, and she worked it in under the first pass—the green, the blue, the single day Ilsa had made—while the amber beneath lay untouched. It had not cut what was alive.

The shed opened. The threads fell back. Far off, the thatch lay down again, the miller's door latched shut, and the woman on the path stopped and went home.

The Hall was quiet. The hearth fire had gone low.

Greta wiped the blade slowly on her skirt and looked at Ilsa with something that was not anger, not grief—the look of a woman who had fed a hand to a loom and watched the price counted out.

"We have one chance to learn to do this right," she said. "And it is you."

Then she pinned her empty sleeve and walked to the hearth, and Ilsa stood alone at the frame, the amber humming beneath her, warm and patient, as if it had all the winters in the world.



Chapter 10: The Warmth

She took the spool to her bed.

The village was shut against the cold; every shutter latched, every door barred, and frost was already seeding the inside of her windowpane, thin ferns of white climbing the glass. Ilsa lay on her side, the spool in both hands, and the wool was warm against her palms.

Not warm as a hearth is warm, or a shawl fresh from the loom. It was warm the way a body is warm in sleep, the way a hand is warm when it has been waiting. The thread breathed. She could feel it under her fingertips — a slow pulse, like the mill-wheel turning under the ice, like the treadle of a loom worked by someone patient elsewhere.

She held it to her ear. It hummed.

A half-song, so faint it might have been the wind in the thatch, the creak of a roof beam. But it was not the wind. It was a loom-song, she was sure of it, the rhythm of a treadle pressed and released, and she almost knew it — the way you almost know a face in a crowd, almost remember a name at the edge of your tongue. The melody turned and would not end, would not resolve, the way the frost would not leave the glass.

It was not a color. The thought came to her lying in the dark, her heart gone strange and quiet in her chest. Amber was not a color at all. It was a kept hour — a room she had never entered, a door held shut from the inside, a hand resting on a warp-thread, patient, warm. It was her grandmother's hand. She knew it the way the sheep know the fold, the way the thread knows the spinner.

She slept with it curled in her fist.

She dreamed of the hill.

Frost flowers opened at the mound's base, white and slow, breathing open and shut like something alive. A door stood in the hill, stone, shut. And inside — she could not see inside, she was only a dreamer, only a granddaughter — a woman sat at a loom whose face she could not see, working the shuttle, hair grey, and the frame above her creaked, creaked, a voice of wood under tension counting something. Counting, and counting.

Ilsa woke.

Frost stood on the inside of her window, a whole garden of it, whiter than before. The spool was warm in her fist, warm as a pulse, warm as a hand. She lay very still and understood, with a certainty that had nothing to do with knowing: the thread was not lost or locked or hiding.

It was leading her somewhere.



Chapter 11: The Hill at Dusk

The Hill stood at the valley's edge like a held breath, ringed with frost in the dusk that never quite became night. Ilsa walked the last of the path alone, the grave-skein heavy in her arms, the fleece she had spun warm with lanolin. The grave-gift was a plain custom: the dead keep their warmth, and the living can find the dead by the thread. She had performed it for the miller's sister before she went grey. Never, though, for her grandmother.

The mound breathed. She felt it in the soles of her boots, the roots of her teeth: a slow in and out, the ground rising a finger's width with each sigh. At its base the frost flowers opened and closed, petals of rime like small lungs in some colder lung. She stopped at arm's length, as before a door you have not decided to knock on.

There was a door in the hill. Stone, shut, grown over with years, so fitted to the turf that the mound wore it like a clasp. No one spoke of the hill since the lock, except to say what was buried there. Ilsa knelt in the frost and laid the skein at the lintel. For a moment nothing happened. Then the thread stirred, not by wind, and ran into the seam of the door-jamb, strand by strand, drawn like a loose end into a weave. The skein emptied slowly, gold going into the stone, and the frost flowers at her knees all closed at once, holding.

She felt her grandmother like a held breath: no face, no voice, only a presence on the far side of a door, patient as three years of cold. Anke. The last of it slipped from her fingers. She stood, her hands smelling of lanolin, the dusk lying over the valley like a warp waiting to be thrown.

Then, from inside the hill, the creak of a loom frame, not the Hall's loom — a deeper wood, a heavier tension, a beam turning in the dark. One creak, then another, counting. The great loom under the hill was working. It had been working all along, through every locked spring, her grandmother's hands moving in the dark of the mound, keeping the winter, holding the door. The thread she had brought was feeding it.

She put her palm flat to the stone door and felt it warm, the way her window's frost had melted the night she touched the amber spool. "I'm coming," she said, though she had not meant to, and her voice sounded very small against the hill. The creaking paused, as if the loom had turned to listen. Then the beam turned again, one notch, and the sound went on without her, patient, counting wool.



Chapter 12: Elders' Edict

The Elders stood in the lamplight of the Hall like three threads drawn taut from the same beam. Elder Maren did not set the shearing knife down. She held it across her chest, and the blade caught the hearth-glow and held it, cold and quiet, and Ilsa could not stop watching it. The knife had never cut a living warp.

"The Winter-Waking will be woven by the first thaw," Maren said, her voice the sound of a shuttle crossing an empty shed. "Or the village accepts the grey. The cold room stays open. We keep our half-loved, and we count what is left."

Ulf nodded, slow as a weight. Sina did not look at Ilsa at all. She looked at the dark loom instead, the unthreaded frame that had stood like a held breath since the amber year, and Ilsa understood the three of them had woven this speech between them, in the cold nights.

"And the amber," Maren said, "is forbidden."

The word fell into the Hall and lay there. Ilsa's hand closed on the spool in her pocket, the warm hum of it against her palm, the pulse of it, the kept hour. She could feel it listening.

"It is to be returned to the Thread House," Maren went on, "and sealed. It is her color. It is what she locked us with. We will not dress the loom in it again."

Ilsa did not plan the words. They came up out of her like a thread pulled from a full bobbin. "It is not a lock. It is a door she is holding." She held the spool up in the lamplight, and the amber turned, warm as a window. "She is still holding it."

The silence that followed was the silence of a loom when the treadle stops mid-beat. Maren's hand tightened on the knife.

Then Greta moved. She had been standing at the hearth, one-handed, her pinned sleeve catching the firelight, and now she stepped between Ilsa and the Elders and stood where a cut would have to pass. She said nothing. She did not need to. The Hall fell silent around the shape of one woman with one hand; no one dared reach past her.

The Edict was passed anyway. Ilsa felt it go through the room like a warp pulled too tight, felt the frame groan under a load it could not carry. Maren sheathed the knife; the bright blade went dark.

Greta only said, "Then we must finish before the thaw."

But the thaw was not coming. The winter was locked, and the amber in Ilsa's pocket knew it, and hummed, and waited.



Chapter 13: The Loom Sings

The Hall at night kept its own weather. Frost stood on the inside of the high windows like a second weave, and the great loom stood dark against the wall, and Ilsa sat at its foot with the lamp low, waiting for the sound that had woken her.

It came at the turn of the night. A creak in the frame, high and small, as if a beam had shifted in its sleep. Another answered, lower, then a third, running together like a treadle pulled once, and the groan of wood under tension went through the dark the way a needle goes through cloth — not tearing, finding.

She knew wood — the small complaining of a house at midnight. This was not that. This was a voice.

Ilsa stood, and the creak came again, resolving the way a song resolves when you find the words under the tune — a rhythm, a name being counted. Not a song, not a complaint — a count, slow as a spindle turning in a bowl of water.

The lamp flame bent toward the loom, though the Hall was windless, and the frost held its breath.

She moved closer until the frame's shadow lay across her like a shawl she had not asked for. The creaks came from warp-beam, heddle, and the posts joined, as if the wood were one throat. She listened until her ears ached, and the count opened like a pattern book, and she saw what it was counting.

Wool. It was counting wool. The fleece of the Wool-Trust, the autumn's giving, the warmth of flock and hearth — strand by strand it counted everything the village had ever promised, and the count was long, and it had gone on for three years in the dark.

Her breath came out in a plume and did not fade.

She knew the word before it spoke it. Anke had told her a story once, by the hearth, of a name older than the village, a name weavers never said aloud, for saying it was an agreement. The creaks gathered themselves the way a weaver gathers the shed before the shuttle flies, and the frame spoke it — not a shout, a breath, the groan of a beam under winter's weight.

Wool. The word lay in the dark between them, and she understood at last what the great loom was — not the thing that held the village, but the thing that held it and would not let it go: a cage and a loom in one frame, the wood that made the cloth and the wood that counted its price.

She backed toward the door and did not take her eyes from the loom, and the counting went on without her, patient as winter, older than the village, sure of its arithmetic.



Chapter 14: Ilsa's First Pass

At dawn she dressed the shuttle with Spring, the green of first moss, birch catkins white, river-blue for the melt, and the Hall held its breath. Greta stood at her shoulder, one hand on the frame; the Elders had come in to watch, old Maren with the shearing knife at his belt. Ilsa's hands were cold. The amber warp lay under her, forty threads of locked winter humming their one low note, and she set her foot to the treadle and made the first pass.

It went in like a key.

For the length of a day the village was spring. A warm wind came down the valley and stayed; the snow on the roofs sighed and ran; the rowan at the yard's edge, three years shut, cracked its buds open, small green fists unclenching. The mill turned, wheel and stone complaining, and she stood at the window with Greta as the grey well at the edge began, slowly, to run clear. Hope moved through the village like a shuttle, and Ilsa felt the warp of it hum under her hands, forty threads, each one a year, agreeing at last to become the next color.

Then the cold snapped back.

It came at dusk, without wind, without warning: frost laid itself on the inside of the Hall windows; the rowan's opened buds seized shut where they stood, hard and green and dead; the mill's wheel stopped mid-turn, its last drop of water hanging in the air like a held note. The well ran grey through the night, a sound like cloth tearing slowly. In the Hall the great loom trembled, frame to frame, and the amber warp buckled under the Spring threads, refusing to be covered, the locked color pushing up through the green like winter through a thaw, and the whole cloth sang its one low note, louder now, as if interrupted.

She understood, standing in the dark Hall with her hand on the trembling frame: you could not weave Spring over a locked winter. The lock had to be opened first. The green was only a color laid on top of a door, and the door had simply pushed it aside.

Greta came in from the cold room, grey at the edges of her face, and looked at the warp. "You felt it," Ilsa said. "You knew it could not take." Greta was silent a long moment. "Anke said so, the night before they buried her." She put her one hand on the frame where Ilsa's lay. "She said the winter had to be opened, not covered. I did not want to believe her." The loom creaked between them, slow and patient, like a voice clearing its throat.

Ilsa looked down at the broken pass, the Spring threads slack where the amber had thrown them off, and understood the shape of what was left: not a weaving, but a door. And she was the one to open it.



Chapter 15: The Thread Pulls

The amber thread pulled taut in the dark of the Hall.

Ilsa was at the hearth, the spool in her hands, when the strand leapt like a fish on a line — a shiver through its whole length, then it went straight toward the door. It had been warm; now it burned. It hummed the half-song, the one that went away at the edges like a face turning.

She did not call out. Greta slept in the weaver's room with her pinned sleeve and her secrets, and the loom stood dark against the wall, and Ilsa knew, the way you know a door has been open a long while, that the thread had waited for this night.

She took it up. It ran out of the spool in a long strand, out of the Hall, over the frozen yard — and the snow received it like a warp receiving a weft. Where it lay, the frost dimmed, a thin dark line of earth showing through, warm and breathing. She walked the thread the way you walk a road, two fingers on it, feeling it hum under her touch.

The village behind her was all grey roofs and closed shutters, the eaves bearded with the three-year frost. No lamp burned at any window; even the smoke rose thin and colourless, as if the chimneys had forgotten what warmth tasted like. She passed the stopped mill and the well running grey; the grey people stood unfinished at the Hall windows behind her, humming, and did not watch her go.

The Hill ahead of her was breathing. She had felt it before, in dreams — the mound drawing in, letting out, a slow chest of earth and root — but she had never walked to it in the body, at dusk. The frost flowers at its base opened and closed as she passed, each one a small held breath. The door was there, stone and shut, the same door she had laid the grave-skein against, the one that had drawn her grandmother's thread into its jamb like a strand pulled into a weave.

The amber thread ran over her wrist, warm, pulling, humming the half-song — it ran into the door and through it, and the stone did not stop it.

The door stood open.

Inside, the hill was dark and full of thread-sound, and at the far end something creaked — a frame under tension, counting. She stood at the threshold, warm thread in hand, the cold village at her back, and understood she had been going to this door all her life, thread by thread — her grandmother on the other side of it, holding the winter, waiting.

She stepped through.



Chapter 16: Under the Hill

The door in the hill stood open, and the frost at its jamb was warm, which was the first wrong thing. Ilsa put her hand to the stone and it gave like cloth under her palm, and the amber thread at her wrist pulled, humming its half-song, and she went in.

Down stone stairs worn to a shine by feet that were not feet, and then the stairs were gone and the earth changed. It was not earth, not any earth she knew: walls of root and warp-thread braided together, dark as the underside of a loom, a ceiling of thatch and bone and fleece stretched overhead like a shed of threads held at tension. The air was full of thread-sound, the hum of wool under strain, low and constant, like a loom left running in another room. It smelled of lanolin and cold, of the Hall's stores after a long winter, and under that, of something older, the smell of a cellar that had never been opened.

The grey people were here. She had thought them in the Hall's cold room, in their houses, at the edge of the village, but they were here, standing in the dark of the passages, shuffling, half-unwoven. Their faces were cloth gone threadbare; their eyes were warp and weft, loose and grey. They hummed their unfinished songs, the loom-songs they could not finish, and the sound of them was the sound of the village fraying. One of them, the miller's sister, or what was left of her, turned toward Ilsa, and the hum stopped, and she said, in the past tense, "The mill turned. I was the one who was glad of it." And then she went on humming.

Ilsa's throat closed. She wanted to gather them, to hand each one back her name, but the amber thread at her wrist was pulling, warm, insistent, and Greta's teaching was a weight in her: the pattern holds what it holds; you do not pull at a warp that is holding you. She walked on.

The thread ran ahead of her, a strand of warm light in the dark, and the passages opened and narrowed and opened again. The grey people followed her at a distance, their shuffling like snow falling, their humming rising and falling. She passed a place where the woven wall was rent, and through the rent she saw the winter, the valley under grey, the locked frost, the buds that would not open, and understood that the hill was not under the village but behind it, or around it, or holding it. The Hill was the frame. The village was the cloth. She was walking inside the weave of her own life.

The amber thread pulled on, and the passage opened into a chamber of light the color of honey, and the humming behind her stopped, and she stood at the threshold of the Great Loom.



Chapter 17: The Loom Chamber

The passage let her out into a chamber of honey. Light hung in it the way dust hangs in a barn, heavy and gold — the first warmth in three years. Ilsa stood at the threshold: the Great Loom filled the room the way a hill fills a valley.

It was twelve times the height of the Hall's loom, its posts trunks, its beams those of a house that never stood above ground. It was alive the way tension is alive: every joint held its breath, and the creak in it was not wood complaining but wood speaking — slow, counting, a language she had begun to learn. She felt the count in her teeth and in her thread.

On the beam hung the Locked Warp: a bolt of amber cloth, three years of winter, warm and humming against the dark. It hummed the half-song she had almost known since she was small. Ilsa's hands went to it before she meant them to; the cloth was warm as a hearth, warm as a pulse, warm as Anke.

The woman sat with her back to Ilsa at the loom's foot, working the shuttle. Her hair fell grey over her shoulders, and her hands moved in a rhythm Ilsa had known all her life — the treadle's rhythm, a song's, the rhythm of hands that once held hers over a spinning wheel. The shuttle went, the warp took the color, and the frame creaked above her, counting.

Ilsa's breath left her. The shuttle was a woman: her grandmother, woven in to hold the winter. She knew the back of that head the way she knew frost, the set of those shoulders the way she knew her own hands.

"Grandmother," she said, and her voice came out small, like a thread pulled from a cloth.

The hands stopped. The shuttle hung between the threads. The frame creaked once, and the count paused — as if the chamber itself had turned to look at her.

Anke turned.

Her face was the pattern's missing figure — the gap shaped like a woman, the empty place in the design. It was the face of the amber year, held in the loom like a moth in amber: threadbare at the edges, worn thin by three years of holding — warm, and hers.

Her mouth shaped Ilsa's name. The sound came out like the first creak of a loom after a long stillness — the first word spoken in that chamber in three years.

"Ilsa."

It was not a question. It was recognition, the way a thread recognizes its spinner. Ilsa stood in the honey light, the warm cloth against her fingers, her grandmother's face out of the pattern, understanding what the spool had been telling her all along: the winter was not a thing done to them. It was a door. Anke was the hand holding it — and had been holding a very long time, and was tired.

The frame creaked above them, counting. Counting. Counting.



Chapter 18: Grey Tobin

Dawn came up grey over the valley, and Ilsa came up out of the hill into it, the amber thread still warm in her pocket, and the village did not look like the village. The frost had lain all night on the roofs like the nap of an old cloth. At the edge of the village, where the common ran into the mist, the grey people stood in a row, and Tobin with them.

She knew him by his shoulders before his face. The same patched coat, the same unlaced boots; but his hair, barley-colored, had gone the color of ash. The streak she had seen at the Hall's lamplight had not stayed a streak. It had won.

His face was threadbare. That was the only word — the way a shawl goes thin at the elbow until the light shows through. His cheekbones showed through like warp, his mouth a loose hem. He was humming — a loom-song, the shearing song, the one he always butchered to make her laugh, in the key of something missing.

"Tobin," she said, and took his hand. His hand was cold and light, like a hand made of cloth. He looked at her, and the looking did not arrive. She had seen the miller's sister look at her that way, but never believed it could come to Tobin — Tobin who laughed, who remembered names.

"I had a friend," he said, in the past tense, which was the tense of the grey. "She smelled of lanolin."

The words went into her chest like a weft through a shed. She had never once in her life stood in the wind off the sheep without smelling of lanolin, and he had forgotten her name and kept the smell. The grey is not death, she understood, holding his cold cloth hand. Death ends. This is being half-loved — remembered only by the smell you left, standing forever in the doorway of your own life, unable to step in.

She did not cry. The grey people were watching, humming, and she would not give the half-light her tears. She led him to the grey-gathering, where the families kept their vigil, and gave him into the keeping of his flock — the ewes pressed to the gate, as if they could smell, through the grey, what had gone out of him. He stood among them, humming the unfinished song.

Then she walked to the Hall, through it, to the room where Greta sat at the dark loom, one hand folded in her lap.

"Teach me everything," Ilsa said.

Greta looked at the hand she had fed to the frame in the year of the lock. Then she looked at Ilsa — the girl who had come up out of the hill at dawn with her grandmother's color in her pocket and her friend's name gone grey.

"I will," said Greta.



Chapter 19: The Debt in Wool

The Hall was cold that night, and Greta sat with her back to the hearth as if the fire had failed her too. She held the story in her hands like a thing carried too long. "Generations ago, in a starving winter, this village was hungry enough to bargain with the Hill. Our ancestors promised a debt in wool. The living warmth of the flock. The warmth of the hearth. Everything a body keeps that firelight cannot name."

Ilsa sat at the loom's foot, the amber spool warm in her fist. She had heard the shape of it before, but never named outright, never given whole.

"The Hill chose to collect in the year of your grandmother's last Winter-Waking." The pinned sleeve where her left hand had been lay grey in the lamplight. "Anke saw what the payment meant. It would have taken the village's warmth all at once — every hearth, every flock, every sleeping child. She refused to pay in lives. So she wove the lock instead."

"To pay the debt slowly," Ilsa said.

"In cold instead of warmth." Greta's voice came rough, like wool too thick to spin. "Thread by thread. Every grey face is a strand of it. The half-woven are the slow price of the holding — their warmth trickling out into the hill."

Ilsa thought of the miller's sister, who spoke of herself in the past tense. Of Tobin's thinner shadow. Of the cold that ate the village's edges like a moth eating a shawl from the inside.

"Amber is locked time," Greta went on. "The winter is a held door. Anke is the one holding it." She reached toward her own wrist, and stopped. "The loom keeps what it is given. It does not give it back for asking."

She unbound the sleeve, and Ilsa saw what lay at the wrist. Not a stump. Not a scar. A thread — a single grey strand, the length of a hand, wound where the flesh should have been, humming like a note held under a finger. The loom had kept the hand, and in the keeping it had become thread.

"This is what is left of it," Greta said. "The rest is in the frame. It comes back when the debt is paid — if it is ever paid." Her eyes had stopped hoping for herself long ago. "I fed it to the frame to buy Anke time."

"Does it keep everything?" Ilsa's voice came small. She was thinking of the gap shaped like a woman in the pattern book.

Greta bound the sleeve again, slowly, one-handed. "Anke gave it everything," she said. "She is still giving it. Every day she holds that door she is a thread being drawn." She looked at Ilsa, almost tender. "Do not give it everything, child. Give it just enough, and come back."

But Ilsa held the amber spool against her heart, pulsing like a kept hour, already thinking of what she might pay instead.



Chapter 20: Anke's Voice

The chamber was quiet under the hill, and Ilsa had come alone, carrying nothing but the amber thread wound around her wrist like a warm animal. The Great Loom stood over her, its beams dark with old oil, and did not move. She sat at its foot and waited.

It came not as sound but as a settling, a place in the air that gave way like a worn step. "You came back." A voice like a dropped shuttle, warm and ironical and tired, the way a thing is tired that has been holding a door for three years. Ilsa could not find its face in the dark; she felt it in the threads of the chamber leaning toward her like grass toward a wind.

"Grandmother."

"I am not a ghost. I am the pattern now, what is left of its weaver. There is a difference, though it is a thin one." A creak that was almost a laugh. "Thin is the word for this year."

Ilsa asked what she had come to ask: how to end it.

"Not cut," Anke said, the word falling into the chamber like a stone into wool. "Never cut. The Locked Warp is the village, every pass of it a life, a door, a winter's keeping. To cut it would unwind them at one stroke, and the grey would not be half but everything. The shearing knife has never cut a living warp. It must not begin with ours."

"Then how?" Ilsa's voice was smaller than she wanted it to be.

"It must be un-woven. And it cannot be un-woven from the outside. And it cannot be re-woven the way it was woven." A long silence, the frame breathing. "You will have to think of a color that is not a season. I could not think of one in time."

Ilsa turned the amber thread on her wrist. It was warm. It hummed the half-song.

"I wove the only color I had," Anke said. "A winter that held. I thought, if I held it long enough, someone would think of another. I have been the shuttle. I have been the held door. It is a good thing to be, for a while." The threads of the chamber loosened, one strand at a time, like a hand letting go. "It is not a thing to be forever."

The warmth came first at Ilsa's wrist, and then it was a hand, Anke's hand, through the weave, cool and warm at once, the hand that had taught her to spin, that had kept the door. It held Ilsa's for a moment that was not measured in years. Then it let go.

"Think of the color, granddaughter. I will keep holding until you do."

The chamber was quiet again, the frame creaking in the old speech, counting, the amber thread warm on Ilsa's wrist like a promise kept.



Chapter 21: The Pattern Breathes

She sat at the Hall's loom long after the hearth had fallen to embers, and the pattern breathed around her.

It had come slowly, as hearing comes to a sleeper: first the thread-sound she had always known, the hum of wool under tension, and then, beneath it, another sound — many sounds — the whole village rising and falling like one chest. She closed her eyes and heard it: every life a strand, every strand humming at its own pitch, the way Anke had taught her to hear the spin of a single thread in a full skein-box.

She listened until she could name them. The miller's sister was a strand gone flat and silent, wound too tight, no song left in it — she had been grey longest, and her humming had stopped; only the shape of her thread remained, like a cord with the twist pulled out. Old Ulf was going: his strand fraying at the edge, the pitch thinning, a looseness creeping in from the ends. And Tobin — she found his thread by its stubbornness, thinner than it had been a month ago, but warm at the core, a small steady gold that would not be pulled out. She held her breath on his name and the strand held back.

The pattern breathed. In, out. Some strands went silent while she listened — she felt each one stop like a door closing far down a hall, and knew that somewhere, at the edge of the lamplight, another face had gone threadbare.

The loom is both cage and loom. Greta had said it, and now Ilsa felt it in her ribs: the same frame that held the village upright was the frame that held it hostage. What it holds, it makes. The strands were not separate things; they were the cloth — the village was a cloth, and she was sitting inside it, feeling every pull of the loom that wore it, the way a hand feels the tension of a warp — too taut, and the cloth tears; too slack, and the pattern sags.

She thought of the grey people drifting at the Hall windows at dusk, half-woven, half-lived, their loose memories trailing behind them. She could hear them now — the unfinished songs, the past tense of their own lives — and the horror of it was not that they were dead. It was that they were interrupted, lives half-lived, in the half-light, waiting for a hand that did not know yet how to find them.

There was no way to reach them from the hearth. No way to hand back a memory from across the cold room, no way to re-wind a thread by looking. She would have to go into the weave.



Chapter 22: Greta's Confession

The confession came in the small hours, when the Hall's hearth had burned to coals and frost climbed the windows like a second loom against the glass. Greta sat with her pinned sleeve in the firelight, not looking at Ilsa when she began.

"In the year of the lock," she said, "the frame began to move against the Hall. The beams leaned. Not the loom's work — the frame that owns the loom. It reached for the warp while Anke was still mid-Spring, wanted the whole cloth off the beam all at once. And I —" Greta held up her left arm, the empty sleeve. "The loom keeps what it is given. I gave it my hand; it stopped reaching; Anke had the night she needed to finish the lock."

"Your hand," Ilsa whispered. "For the winter. For all of this."

"For your grandmother." Greta's voice came from the coals. "I did not know it would take the years too."

The confession went on after that, thread by thread, and Ilsa sat in the cold and listened. Greta had known what the grey costs from the first grey-gathering — the miller's sister, speaking of herself in the past tense, unable to finish a song. She had let the village thin. She had watched Tobin go grey. She had kept Anke's secret, and the keeping had been her own slow unweaving.

"I told myself the pattern would give us time," Greta said. "That you would come to it on your own, the way Anke said you would. So I stood at the loom and let the cold eat the years, one strand at a time, and bought you every one of them. That is the whole of my honesty, Ilsa. The village is grey because I chose it."

The silence after that was the heaviest thing in the room. Ilsa thought of the wool-trust, the warmth the Hall was meant to hold; of her own threadbare fear of standing in a doorway without knowing why. Greta had been standing in that doorway for three years.

"I kept your grandmother's secret," Greta said, "and I lost her to it. I will not lose you."

She reached for the lamplight, and her hand — her one hand — trembled. Then she pushed the grey hair back from her face, and Ilsa saw it.

The streak ran from temple to nape, a single grey thread gone to a rope of them, and it was not new. It had been there longer than the famine, longer than the lock, longer than Ilsa had been alive. Greta had been thinning longest of anyone in the village, hidden in the Hall's dark, in the years she bought, in the lie that it was anyone's cold but her own.

"The loom keeps what it is given," Greta said quietly, "and returns what is owed." She looked at Ilsa at last. "Let us hope it owes the village more than it owes me."



Chapter 23: The Winter's Teeth

The frost moved indoors. It came at night and settled on the hearth's stones, a white beard on the lintel, and in the morning the Hall's lamplight burned slow and fat as if the wicks had thickened with ice. The thread-sound had gone wrong — not the loose hum of the warp breathing, but a whine, high and thin, like a strand drawn too far and held. Ilsa laid her palm on the frame and felt it shiver.

The river had thickened to a grey jelly, moving like something asleep. The village gathered at its banks at dusk, silent; at the edge stood Elder Ulf and Elder Sina. Ilsa saw it before they knew she was watching: the light coming through them at the shoulders, thin and ragged, the grey creeping at their temples like a slow dusk. Two Elders going grey at the borders, the cloth fraying at its edges and taking its keepers with it.

She said nothing. She went into the weave at night instead, and felt the whole pattern like a warp under too much tension — every thread drawn tight, the grey ones thinning, going silent, the frame above her counting. The whine was the village's own voice, straining. She came back to her body with her teeth aching.

The council was called at dawn, grey light falling through the windows onto the great loom standing dark. Elder Maren held the shearing knife, silver still, and laid it flat on his palms as if it weighed more than it did. "The amber must be cut," he said. "Mercy. A clean cut, before the whole village thins away strand by strand." His voice was kind, which made it worse. "We do not cut a living warp," Greta said, one-handed, standing before the loom, her pinned sleeve a flag. "To cut the Locked Warp would unwind the village at one stroke. You would not be saving them. You would be shearing the cloth off its frame whole." Maren's eyes did not leave her. "And what are we doing now, Greta, but waiting to be sheared by degrees?"

Ilsa heard her own voice come out. "Anke wove that lock with her body. She held a door. You would cut the door down because you are cold." The Hall went still. Maren turned to her slowly, and she saw in him a terrified man who believed he was being merciful. "Then one of you show us another way by the next grey-gathering. Or the knife decides." Greta opened her mouth; Ilsa stepped past her into the grey light and said, "I will." "Show us, child." "Inside the weave," Ilsa said. "I will go in and find the way." And beneath her words she felt the whine of the warp steady, the frame listening, and the whole cold valley holding its breath.



Chapter 24: Ilsa in the Weave

The weave was a place of holding. Ilsa stepped through the shed of the loom and came out into a sky that was a loom's shed—great threads overhead, crossed and uncrossed, parting to grey light, closing like breath. Rivers of un-spun wool ran between the warp-strands, slow and white. There was no ground, only a floor of weft, packed and giving, her two feet in it like knots in a cloth.

Her body was a strand here. She had known it at the first step, the way a spool knows its own wind—every part of her running, one long thread, from her fingertips back to the Thread House, to the skein-box, to the little loop of wool spun at her birth and tagged with her name. She could feel it humming, warm and taut, going grey where she stood, the way her mother's hands had gone grey in the first cold spring. She did not look down at them.

The grey people drifted through the half-light like figures the weaver had not finished. They walked the far side of the warp, past and between, loose memories trailing like frayed ends—a name here, left hanging; a door here, unlatched and swinging; a song here, broken off on a breath. One of them passed close enough that she could have touched its sleeve, and what trailed it was a basket of wool, empty, and the grey person did not know what had been in it.

That was the horror, and it came to her slowly, coldly, the way frost builds on a pane: they were not dead. She had thought all her life that the grey was a dying, a slow wearing-out, with death at the end of it, and so some mercy. But here, in the weave, she saw them whole where it mattered least—threads still running, still warm at the core, still humming their unfinished songs. They were interrupted. They were lives half-lived, walking the half-light of the weave, and the light did not fall on them so much as through them, and they could not finish anything, not a song, not a thought, not a life.

A grey woman crossed her path, head down, hands held out before her as if feeling for a wall. Ilsa knew her. The miller's sister, who went grey in the second year, who had stood in the road at dusk and asked whether the mill still turned. Here her thread trailed her like a wake, and out of it hung a loose end, bright and frayed—a memory. Ilsa saw it for what it was before she touched it: the mill, the turning, the grain, the white of flour on her arms, her name in her brother's voice.

The woman was passing. The loose end swung toward Ilsa's hand like a strand pulled by a draft, and Ilsa caught it, closed her fingers on it, felt it go warm and hum. She promised the mill turned. Now she held the turning in her hand.



Chapter 25: The Half-Woven

In the weave, the memory was a small warm thing, and Ilsa held it as she had held a newborn lamb, cupped against her chest, terrified of crushing it. The miller's sister stood before her, half-finished, her face a cloth gone threadbare, and all that was left of her life trailed behind her like the frayed end of a warp — a door, a name, the turning of a stone wheel.

Ilsa did not think. She gathered the loose end of it in her hands — the turning, the grain pouring dark between her fingers, the river that fed the mill — and re-wore the woman strand by strand, as she watched Greta mend a tear in a blanket, weaving back what time had pulled out. The miller's sister's name came back first, and then her face filled in like a field coming green, and she wept, and she was whole.

The cost arrived at the same moment, exact as a cut thread. Somewhere in Ilsa's own head a strand went grey — the sound of her mother's voice, that low hum she had carried since the first cold spring, gone, drawn out of her like a fiber pulled loose. She stood still and felt the empty place where it had been. She had known the sound so long she had thought it was hers forever. It was not even a grief yet; it was a shape of loss, like the gap in Anke's pattern, where a woman should be.

She should have stopped. But behind the miller's sister stood another grey figure, and behind that one another — a whole quiet congregation of the half-woven drifting through the weave's half-light, their loose memories trailing from them like unspun wool. The old shepherd who had forgotten his flock. The girl who had forgotten her sister's name. Ilsa caught the next loose end and re-wore it — the price this time was the smell of rain before it came, which she had loved without knowing she loved it. And another — the way her father laughed, which went grey like the first, drawn out of her.

The loom above her creaked, and she almost did not hear it for the humming of her own work. Her roots were grey now; she felt it in the weight of her hair, each strand she gave thinning her by a thread. But the faces filled in one by one, and the half-woven wept, and were whole, and stood back from her as if they had been given something they had no words for.

And then the frame above her creaked again — not the groan of a bearing beam, but shaped, deliberate, the way Anke's creaks had been shaped in the Hall. Ilsa went still, a loose thread in her hands, a whole woman standing behind her, grey to the roots.

The creak resolved into a sentence, and the sentence was addressed to her.



Chapter 26: The Hill-God's Creak

The creak came out of the grain of the weave itself, and Ilsa found she could read it now, the way she read a pattern — the long groan of a beam settling, the dry whine of the warp drawing tight. It was not the Hall's loom complaining in the cold. It was speech. It had been speech all along, and she had only now grown the ears for it.

The frame above her said: wool.

She stood in the middle of the half-light, her grey root-hair cold against her neck, and listened. The wool of the flock. The warmth of the hearth. The heat of living bodies through the winter. These were promised. Not by Anke. By the long-ago ones, in the starving winter, when the village made its bargain. The frame creaked the promise out of the wood like sap: the living warmth of flock and hearth, to be paid at the Hill's choosing. The choosing had come. It was owed.

It was feeding, the creaks said, on the locked cold. The amber winter was its food and its patience, a meal set at its table, and the slow grey of the village was the way it dined — thread by thread, until nothing living was left to hold.

And if the lock were cut — the creak came lower, a groan out of the deep grain — if the shearing knife went through the amber, it would not free the village. It would take the loom entire, the Hall's loom and the village's warp wound on it, down into the hill. The grey would not be half, but all. The whole cloth, un-woven at one stroke, every strand going dark at once.

Keep the lock, the frame said, and the village endures half-woven forever. Cut the lock, and the village unwinds. Those were the terms. They had always been the terms.

Ilsa stood in the weave, grey at the roots, the hum of the loom in her teeth, and thought of Tobin's thread, warm and stubborn at its core. Half, forever. All, at once. The frame had counted the whole village and found it wanting, and there was no third door in its wood.

But she had learned to read the creaks, and a question formed in her mouth before she could stop it, shaped the way a shuttle shapes the shed — smooth, inevitable. In the creak-language, breath by breath, she asked: what would it take instead?

The frame stilled.

The great beams, which had moved with the slow patience of three years, stopped. The hum went out of the warp. Even the grey people in the passages paused in their shuffling, half-turned, listening to a silence never heard in that chamber before.

As if the question had never been asked it. As if, in all its age, in all its counting, no weaver had ever thought to ask what it might take instead.

Ilsa stood under the stilled frame, her heart a shuttle, and waited.



Chapter 27: A Bargain in Thread

The frame stilled around her answer, and the whole hill held its breath the way a loom holds its shed before the shuttle flies. Then the creak came again — not a sentence, but a word drawn out slow as a winter: everything.

She offered it herself. Her warmth, her years, the whole of her warp, if the village might go free. She said it in the creak-language, rough from her throat, and meant it. She stood in the weave with her arms open, the thread-sound of the hill around her like a held note, and waited to be taken.

The Hill-God tested her.

It drew a thread from her — a year of her life, cool and gold and strange in the frame's hand — and held it up where she could see it. Then, gently, the way a weaver shows a cloth, it showed her what it would take. The whole of her, strand by strand: her name first, then her mother's voice, then Tobin's laugh as a thing she had once owned. She watched herself become grey in the vision, standing at the edge of the village in the snow, humming an unfinished loom-song, half-remembering a friend who had smelled of lanolin and been warm.

She knew that grey. She had promised the miller's sister that the mill still turned.

Ilsa recoiled. The thread of her year slid from the frame's hand and came back to her like a shy thing, winding itself home into her chest. The Hill-God let it go. It did not need to hold her; she had seen what she was worth to it, and she had seen the price.

But the test had taught her something. It took. It could be paid in kind. She had watched it draw a year of her life as easily as a spinner draws a strand from a distaff, and she understood, the way a weaver understands a cloth by touching it, that it had never been paid in color. It was older than weaving. It counted in wool. It had no word for what a color might be worth, because no one had ever shown it one.

The amber thread hummed in her hand, warm as a pulse, the locked color that refused to become the next color.

And Ilsa, standing at the foot of the master frame, looked at the amber thread and saw, for the first time, the hour between day and night — the hour when the light is held, neither won nor lost, warm and slow. Not winter. Not a door shut. A held hour.

She did not know yet what to do with it. But she knew its shape now, and she knew its name at last. Dusk, she thought. And the frame, above her, creaked a long, considering silence.



Chapter 28: The Elder's Knife

The Hall came to her through the warp as a change in the grain. She was in the weave, the whole village hung around her like cloth from the Hall's beam, and she felt the heaviness before she knew its cause — a footstep down the aisle like a shuttle driven too hard, and the cold of the shearing knife, the blade that had never cut a living warp.

Maren. She did not need sight; in the weave his purpose reached her first, a wind over a frozen yard. Mercy, he believed it — a clean cut, one stroke, before the whole village thinned away. She opened her mouth to call out and the sound would not come; her voice was a thread now, and threads do not shout.

Then Greta moved, and the world slackened. Ilsa felt it as a release of tension at the weaver's place, as if someone had taken hold of the pattern and would not let go: the one-handed knot of her, the pinned sleeve like a twist in the warp, her body laid across the amber between the knife and the frame. Greta meant to take the cut; Ilsa knew it the way she knew her own hand.

The knife came down. Through the cloth of the world she felt the edge pass — not into the loom, not into the amber, but through Greta's shadow, which stood behind her like a woman a little out of herself. The blade came out grey. It had cut nothing but the shadow, and the shadow was threadbare now, like the face of the half-lived, and Greta did not fall; the Hall shook instead.

The frame groaned, and the groan ran from the Hill through the floorboards and out along the warp of the farthest fields; Ilsa felt the tear before she saw it. At the edge of the pattern, where the village's last houses lay against the grey, something rent — a loose selvedge, threads lifting from the cloth. Maren's knife had not cut the loom. It had cut something. The farthest fields were going out at the outside of the world, strand over strand, the way a name goes at the edge of a forgotten song.

She was already running. In the weave running was a pouring of herself along her own thread, grey thinning fingertips streaming ahead, the root-grey of her hair catching the half-light like a frayed end. Behind her the frame kept up its groan, and through it she heard, thin and far, Greta's voice — not a scream, a loom-song, unfinished. Ahead of her the rent gapped open, a loose door at the edge of the village, through which the grey was pouring like weather.

The unraveling had begun at the edge. She ran for it, her thread singing out before her, meaning to reach it in time, though she did not know what time remained.



Chapter 29: The Unraveling at the Edge

The rent was ahead of her, and the weave was coming apart at its edge. She ran through the selvedge country, the warp standing in grey rows like winter trees, and every stride she felt the loosening under her feet—threads giving way like thawing ice, one strand, then a handspan, then the whole far border of the cloth falling open.

The farthest houses were going grey all at once. She felt them as she ran: the winter fields unwinding strand by strand, the fences losing their knots, the roofs lifting off into un-spun wool, and the people of the edge—the sheepfold family, the twins from the river bend, the old charcoal burner—standing in the snow like unfinished figures, their faces gone threadbare, their songs stopping mid-word.

It had a sound, the unraveling. Not a scream. The sound a warp makes when it snaps—a long hum going flat, like a song losing its key. Ilsa ran straight into it with her arms out, gathering the loose warp of the edge in her hands like a woman gathering fleece off a hillside, and the thread was cold as the well, cold as three years of locked time.

She re-wore the border strand by strand. The snowfields first, laid back down like weft; then the fences, knotted back into their posts; then the sheepfold roof, shearing the grey out of it with her fingertips, paying in her own thread. Her hair greyed at the roots, strand by strand, to the nape. Her hands thinned at the fingertips, threadbare, the flesh going transparent as a worn shawl. She did not stop. Every fray was a thread pulled in her own chest, and she pulled it taut and wove it back, and the village came back to itself at its edges, field by field, roof by roof, until the rent was closed and the border held.

The grey ones of the edge stood where they had gone, still and humming, and Ilsa moved among them in the half-light, gathering their loose ends one by one and tying them off like a finished cloth, and she walked them back through the weave toward the Hall, toward the cold room and the grey-gathering, where they would be re-woven slowly, memory by memory, through the winter.

When she came back to her body she was on the floor of the Hall, and Greta knelt over her. Ilsa looked down. Her left hand was grey to the wrist, the grey running up under the sleeve like the tide at the edge of the path. She had paid for it gladly. But it was not what she saw. What she saw, humming through the whole mended cloth—warm and stubborn, holding the edge she had just re-woven—was Tobin's thread, still lit at its core. It was his thread that had held the whole time.

She pressed her grey hand to her heart and whispered, in the loom-song's rhythm, "Wait for me."



Chapter 30: Tobin's Thread

The dark of the weave was not empty. Ilsa had thought, before she learned to walk in it, that the space between threads would be a silence, but it was the closest thing to a held breath she had stood inside: the whole village suspended in the tension of the warp, every life a strand, every strand humming where it held and going thin where it frayed. She moved among them, left hand grey to the wrist, and the grey knew her — kin to the grey at her roots, the same slow unraveling, humming back like a greeting.

His thread she knew before she saw it. It ran with the others, grey and thinning, trailing loose ends like a shorn fleece dragged through mud — but at its core a light that had not gone out, a warmth as stubborn as a hearthbank in a cold house. She put her good hand to it and the thread remembered her: the smell of lanolin, the shape of her name held unspoken. Tobin, who had stood at the edge of the village with the grey people and told her, in the past tense, that he had had a friend. The memory was wound at his center, and it was his thread that had held the mended border when the rent came — the whole edge humming warm through the remembering of her. It had not unwound because he had not unwound.

She knelt in the thread-sound, her grey hand open. She could re-weave him. She had re-woven the miller's sister strand by strand, and the price had been a strand drawn out of her — her mother's voice, gone grey, gone for good. She could hand Tobin back his flock and his laugh and his name, the way she had handed the sister back her mill. But the price for him would be the largest thing she had yet paid, and she did not know what it was, and that was the terrible part. Not that she could not afford it. That she could not count it. She might pay in years, in warmth, in the memory of the river or the shape of her grandmother's hands — and she would pay, gladly, and still not know until the strand was drawn what she had given.

His thread leaned toward her hand like a sheep at a familiar gate. The glow at his core did not know her name, but it knew the smell of lanolin, and it waited.

"I will weave you back," she said, in the rhythm of the loom-song, the treadle keeping time in her chest. "Wait for me."

The thread hummed, warm and stubborn, holding its light against the grey of everything else. She wound the loose end of it once around her grey wrist, a keeping, and did not let go.



Chapter 31: The Amber Warms

The amber thread warmed in her grey hand as if it recognized her. Ilsa sat alone at the Hall's dark loom, the spool open across her palm, the forbidden color loose over her fingers, and it was not a color at all — it was a room she had almost forgotten, a hand she had almost forgotten, a kept hour with a lamp lit inside it. Every thread in the Thread House had lain cold against her skin, dust and lanolin and sleep. This one pulsed.

Three years she had believed the amber was a wound. That Anke had cursed the village with it, or failed in her exhaustion, or been cruel. Greta had let her believe it. The Elders believed it, enough to send Maren at the loom with the shearing knife and leave its edge grey. Ilsa had carried that belief to the hill and into the weave, and it had nearly undone her — nearly handed the whole village to the grey rather than sit with what she held now.

It was warm. That was the whole of it. Winter is cold, and this was not winter. It was the hour between day and night, when light is held — neither won nor lost, neither this year's nor next's — a door standing open on its threshold with the wind held back. The locked season had never been a curse. It was love holding a door. Anke had stood at the Great Loom with the Hill-God counting, the village going cold behind her, and she had woven the only color she could think of, a winter that held, because she could not think of the other one. She gave it her body, and held.

Ilsa understood now what the Hill-God had shown her in the weave, the test that had cost her a year of her life and shown her her own grey figure humming in the snow. It takes, and it can be paid in kind. A payment must be a color it can keep. Amber was a color it could keep — but amber was stopping. What the village needed was a color that flowed, that held without freezing, that moved through the kept hour like water through a held cup.

Beneath the amber's hum she felt Tobin's thread, warm and stubborn, waiting for her in the weave, the way it had held the edge of the village when everything else had gone grey. To weave him back she would have to pay in something the hill could keep. She knew now what she had to spin. Gold from the last un-greyed wool, blue from the thread of the river, and her own warmth wound in — her years, her fear of becoming threadbare, her love of Tobin — all spun into a color that held.

It had no name yet. In her head she named it anyway, softly, in the loom-song's rhythm, the way a weaver names a thing before it is made, the way you speak a name to see if the room will hold it. Dusk.



Chapter 32: The Second Unraveling

She waited until the Hall was empty and the hearth burned low, then went to the loom alone. The amber warp hung on the beam like a held breath, three years of winter wound tight, warm where the rest of the frame ran cold. Anke had said un-weave it, never cut it — lift the winter off strand by strand, the way you take a damaged cloth off the frame without tearing what is still whole. So Ilsa lifted, and the whole night held its breath.

Her grey hand found the first strand of the warp, and the thread was warm against her fingers, alive, humming a half-song she almost knew. She worked it loose from the beam — one strand, then the next, each coming free like a door unlatched. For a little while it was almost gentle; the frost thinned on the inside of the windows, and she felt the pattern giving way.

Then the amber fought. The strand she held snapped taut, the warp tightening down the length of the beam, and the pattern snarled — a knot closing like a fist in the middle of the winter. The village's threads strained, and she felt each one in her chest like a pulled thread, as at the first unraveling. The grey began to climb. It rose from her wrist, where it had already settled, up her arm, past the elbow, over the shoulder, the color of cloth gone threadbare, taking her strand by strand as it had taken Tobin, and she could not let go, and the amber would not let go of her.

Her name went out of her, then the sound of the river, then the shape of the Hall's roof. A hand came through the weave — Anke's hand, warm, reaching between warp and weft as if the pattern were the door she had held all along — and it closed on Ilsa's wrist and pulled. The grey tore free of her with a sound like a strand snapping. Ilsa fell back on the cold floor, gasping, her arm grey to the shoulder, teeth chattering, the amber humming on the beam, patient, sealed again.

She lay a long time in the dark, listening to the thread-sound, understanding the lesson paid in her own flesh. The lock could not be un-woven by one pair of hands, from the outside, against the tension of the whole winter. Every strand she lifted pulled the rest tighter. A damaged cloth was not mended by cutting it out, nor by pulling it apart; it was woven over, pass by pass, replacing the color as the shuttle went, and only then the old thread gave way.

The creak came, and then the voice — warm, tired, like a dropped shuttle: "I held the door as long as I could. It is your turn to change the color of the room."



Chapter 33: Anke's Choice

The honey light of the loom chamber did not quite warm. Ilsa stood at the foot of the Great Loom, grey hand pressed to her chest, and Anke was telling her what it would cost, the way a weaver names a warp before she threads it.

'A winter held needs a hand on the shuttle,' Anke said, a dropped shuttle, soft-worn. She did not turn from the beam. 'The color can be changed only from inside, pass by pass, while something holds the tension true. And only the hand that wove the lock can hold it without tearing it. That is the shape of the price, granddaughter. It is me.'

Ilsa said no. She said it twice, then in the creak-language of the frame, and the frame did not answer. 'There is another way. There is always another way, the way you said—'

'There is not. I looked.' Her hands moved on the shuttle, and the weave shivered, three years of winter shifting like a held breath. 'I looked for three years, and then you came down the stairs with a spool of my own wool in your fist, and I knew the looking was done.'

Ilsa argued. She would hold the shuttle herself; she had the dusk thread; the gap shaped like a woman would fit her, and she would hold the tension and never let go. She wept in the honey light, her tears freezing on her cheeks, and Anke listened the way the hill listens, older than the objection.

'You are not the weaver of this warp,' Anke said gently. 'You are the weaver of the next one. I have been a shuttle long enough. Weave, granddaughter.'

Ilsa said, 'You will be un-woven.' The words came out like thread pulled loose.

'Yes.' Anke did not flinch. 'I will be un-woven, thread by thread, into the death I have been holding off for three years. I am not afraid of it. I have been half-alive in the hill, and half is worse than done.' She said it warm, ironical, exhausted. 'Three years I kept the door so you could grow up to find the other color. Now you have found it. Let me set the door down.'

Ilsa wanted to say she was not grown, that she was nineteen and grey at the roots, that she could not weave without her grandmother's hands to catch her. She said none of it. The frost had gone from the inside of her window the night she held the amber; she understood then that her grandmother had been holding the door for her all along.

Anke showed her where the pattern would let her in, the empty figure on the draft, the gap shaped like a woman. Anke's own shape filled it now, faint as frost on glass.

'That gap is mine,' Anke said. 'It will fit you too.'

And Ilsa, holding the dusk spool against her grey heart, said yes.



Chapter 34: The Dusk Thread

In the Thread House, by the last of the lamplight, Ilsa took the amber spool from its shelf. The thread hummed against her grey fingertips, warm as a kept hour, and the room gathered around it like a held breath. She had seen, at last, what she had to spin. The name came to her in her own voice, small and certain: dusk. The season of holding.

The gold came from the Hall's last un-greyed wool, a hank Elder Sina had set aside before she thinned, pale as lamplight, and it ran through Ilsa's hands like something remembering the sheep. The blue came from the thread of the river, unwound from a skein in Greta's chest, the color of the mill's turning water gone still, and when Ilsa wound it around the amber the two lay together the way day lies against night. Then she gave her own warmth. She spun in the year of her years she had offered the Hill-God and taken back, and her fear of becoming threadbare, the terror of standing in a doorway without knowing why, and Tobin's name, and the smell of lanolin, and the shape of her grandmother's hand. She wound them all into the thread, and the thread held them. It did not stop time; it let time move through it, warm and slow, the way a river moves under the ice it has kept all winter. That was the difference between amber and dusk, and she felt it in her palms like a pulse.

The wheel turned. The thread grew. It was the first new color anyone in Brekkelund had woven since the lock, and the loom-songs would not name it. She tried the old names, gold, amber, honey, and each one went grey in her mouth before she could sing it. The songs had no shelf for a color that was not a season. So she named it herself, quietly, to the humming thread: dusk. The hour between day and night. The season of holding.

Greta came to the doorway and stood without speaking, watching Ilsa's hands on the spindle, the grey at her roots, the strand winding gold and blue and brighter than either. When the spool was full, Ilsa held it up into the lamplight, and for one moment every grey thread in the room glowed gold, the hanks, the warp, the thinning skeins, lit from within like a face at a window. Then the light passed, and the room was wool and shadow again, and the thread was only a thread, warm and humming, with time moving through it.

Weave, granddaughter, the hall seemed to say, but it was not the hall. It was her own heartbeat, counting.



Chapter 35: The Great Loom

The dusk spool was warm in her greyed left hand as she went down into the hill, and the hill knew she was coming. The walls of woven earth pressed close, root and warp-thread, and the air was thick with thread-sound — the whole loom singing in its sleep, or waking. She had walked this way alone twice before, following amber. Tonight she carried her own light, the hour between day and night wound tight as a heart, and the passages opened for her as if they had been waiting.

The grey people were gathered in the dark of the passages. She passed among them like a thread passing through a shed, and they turned their threadbare faces toward her and hummed — not the unfinished songs this time, but something underneath them, a drone like the warp before the shuttle flies. Some had been half-woven longest, their faces cloth gone thin, their eyes holding the half-light of the half-lived. They had come to see the color, and the dusk spool in her hand answered them: gold passed through the passages where the grey had been, and was gone.

The chamber opened around her, and the Great Loom was awake.

Twelve times the height of the Hall's loom it stood, dark wood and moving beams, its frame creaking its speech — the Hill-God, older than the seasons, counting. But the counting was slower tonight, wary, as if it knew the hour had come. On the beam hung the Locked Warp, a bolt of amber cloth three years deep, humming under its own tension, warm as a held breath. And at the loom sat Anke, back to her, shuttle in hand, her grey hair falling, her body moving through the pattern she had woven herself into.

The Hill-God is the frame. The village is the cloth. Anke is the shuttle. Ilsa had heard Greta say it in a different order, but standing here she felt it as one thing, a single woven truth, and she stepped past her grandmother to the empty place in the pattern — the gap shaped like a woman — and it fitted her.

It fitted her the way a gap in a shed fits the shuttle. The whole village ran through her then: every thread, every name, the miller's sister whole again, old Ulf humming his song to the end, Tobin's thread warm and stubborn at the core where the grey had not reached, Greta's one hand gripping the edge of the cloth she had held for three years. Ilsa felt her own warp running back to the Thread House, to her birth-skein, and the grey at her roots like weather, and she did not flinch from it. Behind her, Anke waited.

She raised the shuttle.

The chamber stilled. The grey people's hum went thin and stopped. The Locked Warp's song fell to a whisper. And the Great Loom, the frame that was older than weaving, that had never in its age been shown a color, creaked once — not a sentence, not a counting — and fell silent to listen.



Chapter 36: The Color of Holding

She fitted the shuttle into her grey hand and let the stillness hold. The dusk thread on the quill was warm; through it the whole village ran, every thread and name, Tobin's warm core, Greta's one hand, and the frame above her had stilled to listen, and the listening was the hardest thing she had ever been asked to speak against.

"I cannot pay you in cold," she said, and her voice came out in the loom's own tongue, wood under tension, creak for creak, "and I will not pay you in warmth." The grey people in the passages leaned forward out of the dark like cloth in a wind. "The debt was named in wool. Let me tell you what wool is, since you have fed on it three years and never asked."

Wool is not heat alone. It is the gold of lamplight on a child's face in the Thread House. It is the color of the flock coming down the common at dusk, the shape of a warm shawl around the old, the kept hours of a village that remembers its dead by the thread and finds them still warm. The flock's heat is the smallest part of what a fleece carries. The rest is light, the gathered light of every hour the village has woven and kept. That is wool's truest coin. That is what the debt is owed.

"Not cold," she said, "and not warmth given away. Color. Kept and given at once." She raised the dusk spool, gold from the last un-greyed wool, blue from the river, her years wound in, and where its light touched the amber cloth on the beam, the locked winter opened like an eye held shut, not melting, not darkening, only seeing.

The frame creaked. Not a sentence. A question no one had ever asked it in all its long age: what is a color?

She answered him creak for creak. The loom is both cage and loom; what it holds it also makes. Her payment was the same: holding, not locking. A season that held without freezing was not a stopped hour but a kept one, the hour between day and night, and it could feed the frame forever and never end, the way amber fed it, but bright, but moving. "Let me show you," she said.

She held the dusk thread up into the honeyed light of the chamber, and the frame, older than weaving, older than the seasons, older than the word for color, leaned down out of the dark to look at it. In all its age it had never once been shown a color. The great beams of the hill shuddered, and something that was not hunger went through the wood, something that had no name because it had never had cause for one.

"Wool," Ilsa said, and held the thread out like a gift. "The color of holding. Take it, or learn it."



Chapter 37: The Hill-God Listens

The frame argued first, and its argument was the whole winter. The creaks came down the length of the beam like a roof settling, and Ilsa heard in them the old counting: the debt was named before the village had a name, the wool promised in a starving year, palm to palm, and a promise in wool is a promise in the body. The winter was its food. It had fed on the locked cold for three years and would not be emptied of its meal—the creaks said this the way a door says shut, without anger. Feeding was its patience, patience its being. The frame groaned, and the groan was a sentence: the Locked Warp is mine.

Ilsa stood where the gap shaped like a woman had fitted her, the shuttle cold in her grey hand, and answered creak for creak. It was like singing the loom-songs: the rhythm was the treadle, the tone was the frame, and her voice, pressed to the wood, came back as a creak of her own. The debt was a debt in wool, she said. Not in cold. Wool is heat, yes—the flock, the hearth, the shawl against the frost—but wool is color too: gold of lamplight on a child's face, dun of the flock at dusk, the shape of a shawl around the old, the kept hours of a village that remembers. Let it take the color of holding instead, and feed on dusk forever—warm, and slow, and never finished.

The frame stilled.

She felt the stillness as a warp going taut: the whole chamber holding its breath, the thread-sound falling to nothing, the grey people in the dark passages quiet as if the hill itself listened. It had never been paid in color—older than the seasons, older than weaving, no hand had ever raised a color to its beam, only warmth, and cold, and the slow trickle of half-lived years, but never the thing the weavers made. The idea of a season that holds without freezing was new to it, and the new, in the old of the world, is a kind of gift.

Ilsa held her breath in the creak-language, the dusk spool warm in her fist, her grey hand lifted, the whole village running through her like threads through a shed. The stillness lasted long enough for frost to creep into her roots—until she thought the frame had refused and the winter would close over her like a locked door.

Then, slowly, the Great Loom's beams shifted.

Not a refusal. Not a sentence. The old wood moved against itself with a sound like water finding a channel, and the shed of the loom opened—the warp lifting, the space between the threads widening for her shuttle. It had opened its shed to her, and the amber cloth on the beam hummed once, warm, as if it, too, had been listening.



Chapter 38: Ilsa Weaves the Dusk

The treadle rose, and the shuttle flew from Ilsa's grey hand. Anke sat at the loom's edge, a hand of light and skein, guiding the first passes; the warp parted like a held breath and let the dusk in. Gold, the last of the Hall's un-greyed wool; blue, the thread of the river; and at the core her own warmth — a year of her years, her fear of becoming threadbare, her love of Tobin — wound thin and bright as a kept hour. The amber resisted, stiffening under her weft, humming its refusal the way a door will not latch when it does not want to be entered, when it is holding something in. She did not fight it. She wove over it, pass by pass, the way you re-weave a damaged cloth, never cutting, never un-woven.

The gap shaped like a woman fitted her. She felt it settle around her like a shawl cut for someone long dead, and it was warm, and it was hers. The whole village ran through her then — every thread, every name: Tobin's warm core, Greta's one hand, the miller's sister whole at the weave's edge, Ulf and Sina grey but singing. She was not weaving the year; she was weaving them, and they were weaving her, and the treadle beat like a heart that had forgotten how to stop.

In the passages the grey people gathered, half-unwoven, faces turned up in the dark. One by one they began to hum — the loom-song they could not finish — and the hum found her treadle and matched it, the chamber filling with thread-sound, a loom dressed by a thousand hands. Above her the Hill-God's frame creaked, and she braced for a sentence, for the counting of the debt, for the old speech of wood under tension. But the creak was not a sentence. It was a song, the oldest loom-song there is, older than the village, older than the debt — the rhythm of the frame itself, opening its shed to her weft as if it had waited since before weaving.

Anke's hand let go.

Ilsa wove alone. The last pass of the dusk thread went in, gold and blue, and seated itself in the warp like a latch finding its notch. The amber cloth on the beam changed color as it hung — not thread by thread, but all at once, the way dusk comes down a valley: the locked winter softening, flowing, the hour between day and night held open on the beam. And the frost in the chamber, the frost that had stood on every surface for three long years, began to melt, running down the stone in bright seams while the grey people hummed their songs to the end. Ilsa stood at the Great Loom, the shuttle warm in her grey hand, and wept into the warp, and did not stop the loom.

The door of the hill stood open. Somewhere above, spring was waiting to be woven.



Chapter 39: The Winter Loosens

Above ground and below, the winter loosened. In the village the frost let go of the windows pane by pane, each square of glass coming tear-bright, as if it were remembering how to be seen through. The buds on the rowan cracked open and stayed open, small green fists unclenching into the air of a morning that did not try to kill them. Down the valley the river found its voice again, a mutter under ice that thickened and broke and ran, and the thread-sound of it came to Ilsa under the hill like a warp being re-strung.

She stood at the Great Loom, grey at the roots, her left hand grey to the wrist where she had paid for the edge of the village, and felt it all as she felt her own breathing — a letting-out, a held breath finally spent. The hum of the wool under tension had changed key — the whine of a held breath to the song of a thing at rest. The whole village ran through her like threads through a shed: every name, every door, the well at the edge running clear, a child's laugh bright as a thread pulled through grey cloth. The locked time, three years of it, had begun to flow again — slowly, the way a thaw flows, as if it knew it had all the time in the world.

In the Hall, Greta wept into the warp, one hand in the threads, the pinned sleeve hanging — and the weeping was not grief, or not only grief, but the sound of a secret keeper laying a thing down. The frame creaked once above Ilsa, not a sentence, and she knew the Hill-God was letting the beam turn. People stood in the street and looked at the sky as if it were a face they had forgotten how to know — and then remembered. Someone opened a window. The smoke of a hearth went up straight into air that did not freeze it.

At the Great Loom the beam turned, and the Locked Warp began to unroll — amber, dusk, gold, the colors running into one another like hours of light, three years of winter sliding off the beam and into the keeping cloth. Ilsa did not touch it. She only watched it come free, strand by strand, felt the pattern breathing around her like a body setting down a long weight, and where each strand left the beam, some grey thread in the village began, faintly, to hum.

A warm wind came down the valley at dusk — the hour between day and night — and where it passed, the frost flowers on the mound door folded, and the door, which had let her pass before only at the pull of the thread, stood open now in the evening, as if it had always meant to.



Chapter 40: The Grey People Return

The weave was a house to her now, a house she had learned by touch. She walked its passages with a skein of loose memories over her arm, and wherever a grey figure stood half-finished in the warp she knelt, found the frayed end, handed it back. The miller's sister took her name first, and then the mill, the turning, the grain, the low note the stones held under the heart, and her face filled in from the eyes down, a field coming green. She wept, and was whole, and took Ilsa's grey hand in both her hands and said it in the present tense, like a vow she had forgotten she could keep: "I am here."

The row of edge-house people came back the same way, strand by strand, each one a name and a doorway and a morning they had nearly lost. Old Ulf stood a long time with his thread loose in his fingers before he remembered how to hold it; when he did, he went on humming the song he had not finished, and he finished it, to the last note. Sina finished hers in the next passage, and the two came up out of the weave into the lamplight of the Hall together, singing, and the village heard them and wept.

Not all returned. A few of the oldest grey, half-woven longest, stood at the edge of the passages and did not take what she offered. They had grown into the half-light the way a tree grows into a wall, and they looked at their loose threads, smiled, and let them hang. "We were half for so long," one said, in the past tense, "we would not know ourselves whole." Ilsa honored it. She laid her hand on each thread and left it to them, and they drifted out to the edge of the village, humming, like winter's weather kept warm by nothing. The half-lived have a right to their half-life; she let them keep it.

In the Hall the cold room stood open at last, its door swung back on the winter, and the grey-gathering emptied out: the vigils ended, the half-unwoven came into the lamplight, met by their families, and the loom-songs ran to their ends all through the village like a thaw finding every locked channel. Ilsa watched from the Hall's doorway, her grey left hand at her side, her roots grey under her cap, and counted them home. But Tobin was not among the returned. His thread was not in the skein over her arm, and it was not in the loom. It hummed where it was, warm and stubborn, far out in the dark, still waiting, patient as the flock, patient as the man who remembered her when he could not name her.

"Wait for me," she said to the dark, and the hum answered, steady as a held breath.



Chapter 41: Tobin Re-Woven

His thread came to her hand without resistance, grey along its whole length, warm only at the core, where her own name was held tight. She sat on the packed earth of the grey-gathering, Tobin before her, his face threadbare as the moon through wool, humming a song that would not end. Her left hand was grey to the wrist. The thread had waited for her, warm and stubborn, and she had promised, in the rhythm of the treadle, to weave him back.

She began with the flock. She caught the loose end of his memory and handed it back to him: the smell of the sheep, the weight of the shorn fleece across his shoulders, the hill in summer. As it re-wound into him, a thread of her own went grey at the root in payment, a year of her life cool and gold drawn out of her. She gave him the fleece and lost the sound of her mother's voice, which had gone grey in the weave, gone for good, and she gave it gladly, and it was enough.

She gave him the hill, and a strand of her own thinning went silver at her temple. She gave him his laugh, and felt a door close in her that she would not remember opening. She gave him her name, and his thread came warm in her hand, humming through the whole mended cloth of the village like a pulse that had never gone out.

His face filled in the way a field comes green, first the eyes, then the mouth, then the whole of it, strand by strand, until the threadbare was gone and only Tobin remained, looking at her from a great distance that was closing.

"Ilsa," he said, and the word came out whole, a thing with edges. "You smell of lanolin." She laughed or wept; both came out of her as one sound, ragged as an unfinished song. He was whole, and she was a little less, and they were both fine with it. She helped him to his feet, and the grey people at the edge of the gathering looked up as they passed, and a few of them hummed the first line of a loom-song, and for the first time some of them found the second.

They walked the flock home together through the first true spring light, the rowan buds open and staying open, the river finding its voice. Tobin walked ahead, the way he always had, and after a while he began to hum the loom-song he had stopped at half. He sang it through, halting, then sure, all the way to the last line, and when he reached it he turned and sang it at her, his voice clear in the thawing air, and the last word was her name.



Chapter 42: Greta's Missing Hand

The Hall was grey at dawn, and then it was not. Ilsa stood at the hearth banked to ash, feeding the last of the night's wool into the coals, and heard behind her a sound she had not heard in three years: the soft knock of a shuttle laid down, then picked up, laid down again, in no rhythm she knew. She turned.

Greta stood at the great loom, one-handed, the left sleeve still pinned at the shoulder, and the pin was working loose. The grey streak in her hair had gone the color of lamplight. She had both hands.

Ilsa did not move. She watched it happen the way you watch a bud open or a well clear: slowly, and all at once. The air by Greta's left wrist thickened, gathered, a skein of light unspooling from nothing, thread-sound rising like a song remembered, and settled into a hand. Flesh at the wrist, warm, whole. The fingers moved, one at a time, testing the world as a lamb tests the first green, tentatively, then with joy.

Greta made a sound low in her throat. She raised the hand, turned it in the dawn. The lamplight ran over it like water over stone.

The loom keeps what it is given, Ilsa thought, and her voice came out rough. But it returns what is owed.

Greta nodded, not looking away from her own fingers. The debt was paid in color. The hand it held in pledge was released.

Then she wept. Not the weeping of grief, the weeping of a door unlatched. The secret keeper, released from keeping. Ilsa went to her and stood at her side, and did not touch her, because some tears belong only to the one who sheds them, and these were Greta's alone.

When she was done, Greta wiped her face with the back of the new hand, as if it had always been there, and turned to the loom. Weave with me.

They dressed the small loom together, plain wool, un-greyed, from the stores. Greta took the shuttle in her returned hand, and the treadle found its rhythm, and she wove, two-handed, for the first time in three years, the loom-songs coming up out of her whole, to their last lines. Ilsa watched the cloth grow under her hands.

It came off the beam plain wool, and gold at the edges. The edges held light.

Greta held the small cloth up to the dawn, turned it so the gold caught. Her hand, whole, warm, hers, was steady in the light.

Anke would have liked this, she said, quietly.

Outside, the first true spring wind came down the valley, and the frost on the Hall's windows, for the first morning in four years, had let go.



Chapter 43: The Hill at Dawn

Dawn came up the valley grey, as it had for three years, and Ilsa expected frost on her breath. But the frost flowers at the foot of the mound were open, not frozen shut — open like mouths finished with singing — and the door in the hill stood open, the way a held door stands open when the holding hand lets go. She had come here at dusk, when light was held between day and night. This was the other end of that hour. The light was being won.

She went down into the hill. The passages, dark with the shuffling of the half-lived, were light now, empty, swept of grey; the air thick with thread-sound was quiet, the way a loom is quiet after the cloth comes off. In the chamber the Great Loom stood still, its beams at rest, and where the Locked Warp had hung there was only the memory of amber — a sheen, a warmth, the shape of three years' weight lifted. The mountain's frame had given back its cloth.

At Anke's grave in the mound the locked cold was gone. The husk lay free of the winter that had held it: a woman at rest at last, her thread — she could feel it, humming above her in the weave — still moving, still waiting. Not yet released. Ilsa knelt and unwound the grave-skein from her wrist, the last dusk thread wound in, and laid it on the grave. The grave-gift. She watched the thread lie still where three years ago the skein had been drawn into the door-jamb like a strand into a weave.

It was not drawn in. The hill accepted it gently — the way a hand accepts a gift it expected, the way a weaver takes the shuttle always hers. Frost flowers at the grave's edge closed, then opened. She understood. Her grandmother had held the door; the hill had held her; now the hill had let go of the winter and taken the dusk, and both were a little freer.

Dawn over the mound, not dusk; light won, not held. In the weave above her, Anke's thread hummed once, warm, and was still — resting, waiting for the day she would un-weave it, and let her grandmother fall into the true death. Ilsa said goodbye with her own voice, out loud, the way you say a thing so it stays true: "You held the door. I changed the color of the room." And she meant it. The Hill was just a hill again, breathing quietly, frost flowers open at its foot, and the dusk had ended in light.



Chapter 44: The Pattern Settles

The pattern settled like a cloth coming off a beam — not all at once, but by degrees, the way a weave finds its truth only after the last pass is in. Ilsa felt it first in the loom itself. For three years the great frame had stood against the Hall's wall like a held breath, dark, un-threaded, heavy with the fear the village leaned on it. Now it creaked under a living tension. The warp flowed; the treadle moved under her foot as if it remembered the rhythm from before she was born, and the shed opened and closed like a mouth breathing.

She wove the Spring. A true Spring, the first in four years — green from the thawing fields, gold from the lanterns in the Hall, and a thread of dusk running through it all, holding, not locking. The cloth came off the beam green and gold, and where the amber had been there was only the hour between day and night, warm and moving.

All through the village the pattern settled. The well at the edge ran clear, the grey gone from it, and the mill turned on the water without a hitch, its wheel stroking the river like a hand on wool. Doors latched. Names stayed where they were put. At supper no one searched for the word they meant, and the children laughed the way children laughed in old songs.

The loom is both cage and loom, Anke's voice said from somewhere in the weave, and Ilsa understood at last: the cage had become a loom again, a thing that holds by making. The frame that had held the village hostage now held it together, and what it held it made, and what it made it held.

She stood at the beam until the lamplight had burned to embers, her grey hand resting on the finished cloth, the grey at her roots fading as the year greened. She could feel every thread in the village humming at its pitch, the thin ones and the half ones, all holding.

The village woke to a morning with no frost on the inside of any window. Ilsa stood in the Hall doorway and watched them come out of their houses — the miller's sister, whole, carrying her bucket to the clear well; old Ulf and Sina, done with humming, walking arm in arm; Tobin at the common's edge with the flock, singing to the last line. They stood in their doorways the way a cloth stands when it comes off the loom, knowing it is finished, knowing it is whole, and they looked at the sky as if it were a face they had recognized at last.



Chapter 45: The Hall Reopens

The Hall was warm when Ilsa came in out of the dusk, warm as it had not been in four years, and the village was gathered in the lamplight, shoulder to shoulder, whole. Faces that had been threadbare had filled with color. Names went up on the air where there had been only humming. The hearth had been fed all day, and the smell of it — lanolin and smoke, the faint iron of the loom's dark wood — rose like a remembered season. Ilsa stood at the loom's foot, and the frame creaked once, not in speech, as a thing settles into a shape it has grown unused to holding.

The loom-songs began. Old Ulf raised the first, the Waking Song, and voice after voice found it, and the warp of the Hall answered with a hum that went through the wood, through the cloth on the beam, through Ilsa's grey wrist. The songs were sung to their ends this time. No voice stopped mid-line. No one forgot the last verse.

Then the Elders moved. Elder Maren came forward and the village made a way for him, and he knelt at the hearth — the eldest kneeling, the keeper of the knife kneeling — and he carried the shearing knife before him in both hands. Its edge was clean and bright; the grey was gone. He laid it down at the foot of the loom on the stone, where the rite kept it, and spoke the words Ilsa had heard only from Anke's voice in the weave: the knife that cuts the finished cloth from the frame. He did not take it up again.

They were forgiven, and they forgave. Ilsa watched it pass through the village like a shuttle through a shed — Sina's held breath going out, Maren's shoulders letting down, Greta stepping forward to the bench, two-handed, whole. Greta looked at her and nodded once.

The rite continued, changed. Ilsa drew the dusk spool from her sleeve — the strand of holding, warm as a pulse — and wound a thread of it into the year's cloth where the lock had hung. It went in gold and stayed gold, flowing, the hour between day and night held in the weave so the village would remember what a locked winter had cost, and what had paid it.

Then Greta and Ilsa wove the first cloth of the new year together, one hand each, passing the shuttle hand to hand, the treadle finding its rhythm, the pattern breathing out green and gold — and the whole village sang the loom-songs to their ends, and the Hall was a loom again, a thing that held by making.



Chapter 46: Anke Un-Woven

Ilsa worked in the weave the way you pull a thread from a finished cloth: slowly, one strand at a time, from the wrong side, so the pattern does not know it is being let go. Anke's figure stood where it had always stood, the empty chair filled by a woman at a loom, a gap that was Anke's shape and fitted Ilsa too. Pass by pass Ilsa drew the threads out of her: the grey that had held the winter, the amber that had locked it, the three years of holding, the hand that had held the shuttle. Each strand came out warm, and each went, somewhere above, into the grave-skein on the hill.

Anke did not fight her. That was the worst of it, and the best. She sat at her loom in the heart of the pattern and let her granddaughter un-weave her, strand by strand, as a mother unpicks a hem, and once said without turning, You are doing it right. Slow enough. Her voice was the voice of a dropped shuttle: warm, ironical, finished. Ilsa wept and did not stop; her hands kept their work, the left one grey to the wrist, the roots of her hair grey where her own thread had gone into other people.

Grandmother, she said.

Come round where I can see you.

Ilsa walked the shed until she stood before her. Anke was nearly gone. The pattern showed through her like lamplight through a worn shawl, and her face was the face of the empty figure at last. I held the door, Anke said. You changed the color of the room. That is what a granddaughter is for. She smiled the way the amber thread hums, warm, and going. Now let go, Ilsa. Not un-woven. Finished.

Ilsa let go. The last threads of her grandmother ran out of the pattern like a song's last line, and the figure became an empty chair, a remembered shape, a woman at rest, and then only the chair, the whole village humming through it, warm. She gave the last thread herself, two hands teaching a child to spin, and felt it go without grief, the way a strand feels the cloth take it. Ilsa knelt where her grandmother had been and grieved alone, and above her the Great Loom creaked once, not a sentence, an amen.

When she came up out of the hill it was daylight, and the mound was just a mound, ringed with frost even in the thaw, frost flowers blooming pale and open for the last time. The grave-skein lay wound full at the door, and the door did not draw it in; it stood open on the light, the way a hand opens to a gift it expected, and closed behind her, and the hill was only a hill, and it let her go.



Chapter 47: The Village Remembers

The new verse came to them in the lamplight, the first night the frost let go of the windows. Greta found the tune, humming it over the warp, and Ilsa found the words, and the Hall learned to sing the half-woven to their end. There was a verse for the miller's sister, come back whole; for the edge-house people who had stood in the snow like unfinished figures; for old Ulf and Sina; and for those who chose to stay half. It was not a sad song. It was a song that finished.

They sang it at the Keeping, the new custom, whenever a cloth came off the Hall's loom: a thread of amber woven into the selvedge, the locked color kept as the color of holding, so no one would forget what a locked winter cost, or that it was paid. Ilsa stood at the beam and watched the village remember. Elder Maren left his grey-edged knife at the foot of the loom; the well ran clear, the mill turned; the returned grey people held their children like skeins once thought lost. To weave was to make what was remembered real.

And the village remembered Anke. Not as the woman who cursed them — that story the frost kept, and let go of — but as the weaver who held the door with her own body until a better color could be found. They said her name at the Winter-Waking in the same breath as the season; her empty chair stayed at the hearth, and the last pattern book lay open on the drawer, its gap shaped like a woman, filled at last.

At dusk Ilsa walked to the hill's foot and told Tobin the whole story, the way a warp is dressed: the debt in wool, the amber year, the loom that stood dark, the door in the hill, the hour between day and night. She told him about his grey thread and the glow at its core that had held the village's edge while she mended it; about Anke's hand through the weave, warm, letting go. The mound stood quiet at her back, just a mound, frost flowers gone.

And Tobin, who had once looked at her without recognition, who had spoken of himself in the past tense — Tobin remembered it back to her, all of it: the flock, the shorn fleece, the half-song he could not finish, the smell of lanolin. He said her name — Ilsa — the way you say a thread you would follow anywhere.

"That's right," she said, her grey hand finding his. "You remembered it all."

He looked at the hill, then back at her. "Someone had to," he said. "It was your story too."



Chapter 48: The Amber Kept

The amber was not burned. Ilsa had thought, in the nights after the thaw, that the Elders would want it burned — that a locked winter could be unmade by fire the way a bad skein is flung on the hearth. But Elder Maren came to her at the Thread House door with the shearing knife laid flat across both palms, its grey edge clean, and said only: keep it. The village keeps what it was saved by. So she carried the spool down the dust-furred aisle to the skein-box and laid it in the worn hollow beside her own birth-skein, the warm amber beside the pale strand spun the night she was born. A relic, and a lesson, and a covenant in one thread.

The Hall's loom weaves a thread of amber into every cloth now. Greta had said it first, two-handed, the morning her hand came back: the debt is paid in color, not cold, and the village should not forget the price of either. So the weavers dress the warp as they always did — wool of the flock, wool of the Trust — and among them, thin as a kept breath, the locked color runs, warm as a pulse through every blanket and cloak. The old are wrapped in it, the sick lie under it, and the holding remains.

Today Ilsa showed the children. They gathered at the skein-box in their grey-faded aprons, eyes wide as lamplight, and she lifted the spool so each of them might touch it. Warm, they whispered, as if it were a miracle and not the plainest truth of wool. She told them what it was: the hour between day and night, when the light is held without being lost or won. She told them what one woman could think of, in time — a winter that held the door, and a dusk that changed the color of the room. She did not tell them everything. Some things a thread keeps better than a tongue.

Then she wound a strand of it around her own wrist, over the grey that still mapped her left hand, a keeping over the fading. It hummed against her skin, the old half-song, warm, patient. The miller's sister smiled at her from the doorway, whole again, and beyond her the rowan stood in flower.

She wore it to the loom. The warp was dressed, the shuttle waiting, and the amber on her wrist caught the hearthlight as she took the first pass of the evening cloth — a thread of holding going in beside the gold, the winter over, the debt paid in color, not cold.



Chapter 49: The Last Thread

She closed Anke's pattern book and laid it on the drawer's lip, and the gap shaped like a woman was filled at last, warm with her own thread where her grandmother's had run. The final strand of the Locked Warp went into the Keeping cloth strand by strand, and the amber on the beam gave up its last locked hour without a fight, the way a door gives when the hand that held it lets go. Ilsa's own pattern began as she worked, unasked: her mother's voice, lost and given gladly; Tobin whole in the fields; the dusk thread running through everything she touched; and at the roots of her hair the grey fading, like frost off a window when the morning means it.

Greta stood at her shoulder, two-handed now, and Ilsa looked at the mended wrist and thought how strange that a hand could come home after so long. The old weaver's chair stood empty by the hearth, and Greta crossed to it and sat, released, her secret told at last to the whole Hall: the fed hand, the bought years, the slow letting of the village grey. It was no confession now, only a story, and it ended where the village stood: whole.

The Winter-Waking began with the shuttle. Greta took it up from the beam, held it a moment, then crossed the lamplight and put it into Ilsa's hands, before the village, and the loom-songs began, sung to their ends, every one, no grey voice faltering mid-verse. Tobin sang the flock-song from the door, and the miller's sister sang with him, her face full and her name her own. Old Maren knelt at the hearth with the shearing knife laid before the loom, its grey edge gone, returned to the rite it belonged to. The Hall was warm with wool and smoke, and Ilsa felt her thread run back to the Thread House, to the skein-box, to the amber wound around her wrist, warm and humming, a keeping kept close.

She took the first pass of her first year. The treadle found her foot; the shed opened; the warp of her life ran through her — her mother's voice a loss she carried like a thread, Tobin a warm strand that would not thin, the dusk gold and blue and holding, and the grey at her roots going out like a color finished. Never to cut a living warp. Holding, not locking. The shuttle crossed the shed, and the cloth came off the beam the color of the hour between day and night.

Greta's voice rose from the old weaver's chair, whole, and the whole village sang behind it, and Ilsa Wren wove on into the color she had named, as the year came up green.



Chapter 50: Spring, Finally

Green came up the valley like a weft running home. Ilsa saw it from the Hall door the morning the warm rain began, a color that had not been a color in four years, seeping up from the low fields to the shepherd's common. On the roofs the frost let go its last grip and ran in silver drops down the steep grey slates. The rowan by her gate stood in flower, every bud open at once, as if the tree had been holding its breath all winter to see what the thaw would bring.

She worked the great loom all that morning, weaving the summer. The warp flowed under her hands the way water flows, no snarl, no whine, the treadle finding its own rhythm against her feet. Her left hand was grey to the wrist still, and would be for a while yet, but it moved with the shuttle as true as the right, and she did not mind the grey. It was a price, and it had been paid. The cloth came off the beam green and gold, and it hung in the Hall like the year itself.

From the common she could hear Tobin singing, the loom-song all the way through, every verse, to its last line, and the flock answering with the noise of their bells. She stopped to listen, her hands resting on the warp, and the song carried across the warm field like a thread laid straight. Greta sat in the sun by the Hall's door with her two hands full of wool, carding, her grey hair gone gold at the edges, her laughter easy as a wind with nowhere to hurry.

The well at the edge ran clear. The mill turned. The miller's sister stood in her own doorway, whole and remembering, and called the children in from the rain.

At dusk Ilsa was alone in the Hall, the hearth lit against the cooling air, the great loom quiet. She stood at the beam and looked at the cloth, and there, running through the green and the gold, the thread of amber, the locked color, kept, warm as a pulse under her fingertips. It hummed, a half-song she almost knew. Three years she had feared that color. Three years she had thought it was the winter that would not end.

It was not winter. It was Anke's held door, and her own dusk, and the long debt paid in color instead of cold, the village's answer to the Hill, woven into everything they would ever make. She understood at last what her grandmother had woven, and what she had woven herself, and that they were the same act done twice, once in fear and once in love. Holding, never locking. The hour between day and night, warm and slow, given freely.

She turned the beam one notch. The thread went into the cloth, and the door of the Hall stood open on the first true summer evening the village had ever had.
