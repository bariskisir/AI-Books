The Orbital Canticle
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Morning Canticle

The bell woke the station before the dawn, and the dawn was a matter of light, not of sun. St. Elara spun on its ring, and the spin made morning, and the monks made the morning holy by singing into it.

Vesna rose in the dark and dressed in silence. Eleven years in this place, and still the first breath of matins felt like a held note about to be released. She walked the ring corridor to the chapel of the Crown, and the floor gave her weight, and the weight felt like the gravity of Earth, which she had not felt since she was ten.

The choir stood in their arc. Sister Maribel raised her hand. The descant began.

They sang the canticle as it had been sung for two hundred years, the first verse, then the second, the melody held to the old mode, the Elaran mode, seven notes without sharp or flat, notes like water. The great beam took the song as it took every song, the phased array turning the voices into a carrier, a river of light going down the gravity well to the gardens of Earth. They did not know it was a report. Vesna did not know it was a report.

She sang the descant, the silver thread above the melody, hearing every interval before she made it, seeing every note as light. Gold. The root of the mode was gold, and the whole morning was gold, the canticle rising like sunrise over a planet that could not see it.

Then the third verse came, and something changed.

Three notes. Below the root. Three notes that had never been sung in two hundred years of recorded matins. She heard them in her throat before her mouth could stop them, dark notes, the color of deep water, the color of a closed door. She stopped singing. The choir sang on around her, no one noticing the gap where the descant should have been.

The canticle ended. The beam fell quiet for one second, the known second, the antiphonal breath. Then the Amen returned from Earth, a full second late, the return stream answering, and the choir sang the Amen back, and the morning was whole again.

Vesna did not sing. She stood with her hands at her sides, hearing the three dark notes over and over, feeling them in her teeth.

Afterward, in the refectory, no one mentioned it. The canticle had been sung. The Amen had come. The day had begun.

Vesna went alone to the Ledger of the Morning and did not eat.

Chapter 2: St. Elara

St. Elara was a ring around a silence. The outer ring carried the life of the Order, the chapels, the dormitories, the refectory, the gardens of hydroponic wheat, the hangar, the vault, and the spin of the ring made weight where weight was wanted. At the hub there was no weight at all, and the chapels of the hub floated in the zero-g, and the choir sang there on high feasts, their voices unweighted.

At the ring's one straight place stood the Crown Array, the transmitter the monastery was built around, two hundred years old and humming, its face turned always toward Earth. The founders had called the place the Orbital Seminary. The monks called it the Crown, after the light it cast.

The founding legend: Elara the Listener, who lived her whole life on Earth and never saw the sky, because the sky of her time was always storm. Elara built the first garden, the first rectenna field, tuned it to a beam no one had yet sent, and sang to it every day of her life, on the chance that something above might sing back. She died before the first light from L5 reached her gardens. But she kept the tune, and the Order kept her name, and the canticle had been transmitted since first light, two hundred years by now.

Vesna had come up the well when she was ten, the last child ever to ascend, an offering from the listening gardens, sent up to learn the songs. She remembered the ascent, the two days of climbing against the fall of the world, and the lights of Earth falling away below her, the coastal cities strung like a necklace of gold, and the green of the inland where her mother worked the gardens. Ila. Her mother's name was Ila, and she tended a listening garden at a place called Hearthside, and Vesna had seen her face in the transmission glass on feast days, a small bright square of the world she had left.

The door stood at the end of the east corridor, past the hangar, where the ring met the old service passage. Novices were told it was a storeroom. It had no mark, no lock, no window. Vesna had passed it every day for eleven years and had never once seen it opened.

Today, after the changed notes, she stood before it for a long moment, her hands cold, and then she turned away, because she was not yet brave enough to knock on a door she had been told was a room.

But she remembered where it stood.

Chapter 3: The Changed Note

The Ledger of the Morning filled the low room off the scriptorium: two hundred years of recordings, row after row of crystal spools, each sealed and dated and indexed, each holding the voice of one morning's canticle and its Amen. The oldest spools were the color of old honey. The newest were clear as water.

Vesna had access as a novice, every singer did, for the study of the mode. She had never used it for this.

She found yesterday's spool and the day before, and then she worked backward, year by year, sampling a morning from each. She sang along under her breath, her perfect pitch locking onto each recording's center, hearing the whole sound at once, melody, descant, the inserted notes, the held notes, the rests.

There were always inserted notes. The descant was full of them, and she had thought of them as the singers' small freedom. Now she saw they were not random. They repeated. A held note in the same place across a decade, a rest in the same breath, a descant turn that came back every seventh morning like a verse returning.

She had been singing Earth's message all her life, and the message had been singing itself to her, and she had heard only the beauty.

The three notes came in this morning's recording, and she sang them alone in the little room, and heard the gold of the mode turn to dark. Below the root. Below everything the mode had ever touched. Three notes, then a pause, then a run of descant she had heard before but never heard as pattern.

She took a spool from two hundred years ago, the first year, honey-colored and frail, and played the same passage. The old choir sang the same melody, and there: nothing. No dark notes. No pause. The deep three were nowhere in two hundred years of mornings, and then they were in this morning.

Vesna sat until the artificial afternoon darkened. The name would not leave her. Deep three. She had never been told it, never read it, and yet the notes named themselves in her mind: the deep three, the descent, the sound of a door at the bottom of a well.

She wrapped the morning's spool in her sleeve and walked to the choir mistress's cell.

Sister Maribel opened the door, saw the spool, saw Vesna's face, and went very still.

"Sing it," Maribel said.

Vesna sang the three notes.

Maribel did not move. But something in her eyes, something held tight for decades, loosened, and then tightened again, harder.

"Where did you hear that?" she said. Her voice was level. Too level.

"In the morning canticle," Vesna said. "Today. At the third verse."

Maribel closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was looking at Vesna the way a woman looks at a key.

Chapter 4: Vesna's Ear

Maribel did not explain anything. She said, "Come tomorrow, before first light," and closed the door, and Vesna went back to her cell and did not sleep.

She came before first light. The choir mistress's cell was a narrow room with a workbench, a set of earphones, and a spool reader older than Vesna's grandmother. Maribel was already seated, severe in the dimness, and did not greet her.

"Sit," she said. "Listen."

She threaded a spool, and the canticle of some old morning filled the cell, thin and far. "Tell me what you hear."

Vesna closed her eyes and listened whole. "The melody. The descant. A counterpoint in the fourth verse, a held note, two beats, on the fifth. It bends. It should be three beats, but it is two, and the next note is pulled up a hair."

Maribel made a mark on a slate. "Good." She threaded another spool.

"The counterpoint is in the second verse. A rest, half a beat, tucked after the sixth syllable." Vesna hesitated. "It is a one, isn't it? Where the other was a three. The held note is a zero, the rest is a one." She stopped, alarmed at her own mouth.

Maribel looked at her a long time. "Go on."

"I do not know what I am saying."

"Finish it."

Vesna threaded a third spool herself, an old one, honey-colored, and listened. "Two counterpoints. The first is a zero, held note, right length, right place. The second is a rest, but long, longer than the first we heard. A held rest." She frowned. "That is a third thing."

Maribel set down her slate. Her hands, Vesna noticed, were trembling.

"Two hundred years," Maribel said quietly, "I have kept the listening. It took me thirty years to hear as you hear now. You have it whole. You heard the code before anyone told you there was a code."

Vesna stared at her. "Then it is true. The canticle —"

"The canticle is a status report," Maribel said. "It has been a status report since first light. And it has been a distress call for years, and we have been singing it back without understanding it." She turned the spool in her fingers. "The Order's wait is over. An ear has arrived that can hear the code whole. You."

The word hung in the small room.

"What do we do?" Vesna said.

Maribel stood. "First, you will see the vault. I have kept the secret alone since my teacher died, and I will not keep it alone any longer."

She led Vesna along the east corridor, past the hangar, to the door with no mark and no lock that novices were told was a storeroom.

"Now you know what it is," Maribel said. "It is the Hidden Archive. And you are coming in."

Chapter 5: The Choir Master

Maribel was sixty-seven, and she had carried the code alone for forty-one of those years.

Her teacher had been Sister Odile, the keeper before her, who had taught Maribel the counterpoints when Maribel was a novice younger than Vesna. Odile had taught her one night at a time, in this same cell, with the door shut and the spool reader low, so the secret would never be lost to the Order.

Then Odile had died, quietly, in her sleep, on a night when Earth's report had been, for the first time, thin. And Maribel had kept the keeping alone, because the Rule said the Order sang and did not act, and because she was afraid, and because the deepest reason, the one she confessed to no one, she had loved Odile, and the code was all that was left of her teacher's voice.

Now she walked the east corridor with Vesna at her shoulder, and she felt forty-one years of weight shifting in her chest.

"You are about to break every rule I have kept," she said. "Not tonight. Tonight you will only look. But the looking is the breaking."

Vesna did not answer. Maribel was grateful. A girl who argued would have to be stopped. A girl who was silent could be trusted with a door.

The door had no lock. Behind it, a ladder went down into the structural core, where the spin made only a little weight, where the station's oldest parts lived. The air was cold and dry. The lights came on pale and reluctant.

It was a room the size of the refectory, and it was full of light.

Crystal ledgers stood in racks, row on row, thousands of them: the two-hundred-year record, every canticle, every Amen, every counterpoint, every rest, in the original. In a sealed case, the founders' documents, the charter of the Orbital Seminary, the creed of the outward voice, the blueprints of the Crown Array and the Answer Ring, and a leather book closed with a clasp.

"The codebook," Maribel said. "The canticle was always meant to carry two things at once, a blessing and a report. Earth sang her state to us, and we sang it back, and the two voices together were the whole thing. An antiphony. A conversation one second wide."

She stopped. In the cold air, her voice had gone rough.

"I have kept this secret since I was twenty-six," she said. "I have watched Earth's reports degrade, year after year, and sung them back without being able to answer. I believed I would die holding it."

Vesna stood in the middle of the room, turning slowly, taking in the racks of light.

"Why show me now?" she asked.

"Because the deep three are the emergency glyph," Maribel said. "They are defined in that codebook as a call the Order is bound to answer. And they were sung this morning, in my choir, under my hand, for the first time in two hundred years." She looked at the codebook. "The keeper goes where the code leads."

She unclasped the book. The pages creaked.

"Come," she said. "Read with me."

Chapter 6: The Abbot's Rule

They went to the Abbot at the third hour of the watch, when the corridors held only the hum of the ring. The Abbot's cell was round, at the ring's one outward curve, and the window looked down the gravity well at the blue-white curve of Earth, small, bright, wounded.

Abbot Anselm was one hundred and forty-nine years old, Abbot of St. Elara for longer than Vesna had been alive. He received them standing, his hands folded, his face kind and unreadable. Vesna had loved him all her life. She had never once seen fear on his face.

"Sister Maribel," he said. "Novice Vesna. You come late, and together, and with a spool. Sit."

Maribel placed the spool on the table between them.

"The morning canticle changed," Vesna said, her voice steadier than she expected. "Three notes were sung that have never been sung. Below the root. Sister Maribel has shown me the vault, and the codebook —"

The Abbot held up one hand. The motion was gentle. The stillness around it was not.

"I know," he said.

Vesna felt the words land like dropped stones. "You know."

"I have known the code my entire life," Anselm said. "I was shown the vault on the day I took my vows. I have watched Earth's reports for a hundred and forty years." He looked at the spool. "I know what the deep three mean."

"Then—"

"The Rule of the Watch," the Abbot said, "has three clauses. The Order exists to sing, and never to act. The beam must never be silenced. And no one descends."

He did not look at her. He looked at the window, at Earth.

"We are the outward voice," he said. "We sing so that Earth, looking up, always hears that she is not alone. If we descend, if we touch, we become a voice that reaches in, not out, and the outward voice is lost."

"Your Reverence," Maribel said, "the code itself is a call to answer. The founders defined the deep three as —"

"The founders defined the deep three as an emergency," said the Abbot. "They did not define what an emergency is. They left that to the rule. And the rule says we do not act."

Vesna looked at his face, and then she saw it. Not in his eyes, his eyes were as mild as ever. It was in the tightening around his mouth, the way his folded hands pressed together too hard.

The Abbot of St. Elara was afraid.

"I am not deaf to the report," he said quietly. "I have watched the Amen thin for years. I have kept the watch, because silence itself is a message, and because I feared answering would end Earth's last voice." He looked at her, and his face was older than she had ever seen it. "You are asking me to break the rule. I am asking you to understand why I cannot."

Vesna stood. She did not know she was going to stand until she was standing.

"Then I will understand it," she said, "while I read the codebook. Because if Earth has gone silent, the outward voice is already lost."

She left the cell. The Abbot said nothing, and his silence was the loudest thing she had ever walked away from.

Chapter 7: The Hidden Archive

The vault kept no day and no night. Vesna came to it in the deep hours, after compline, and she came alone, because the door that was called a storeroom had begun to call her name.

She worked by the pale lights of the rack, a novice in her white habit, reading two hundred years of mornings.

It was not like reading. It was like listening. Maribel had taught her the rudiments before leaving her there that first night: the held note, the zero, the rest, the one, the held rest, the third thing, which marked that a word had ended and another was beginning. Vesna carried the spools to the bench and moved through them, decade by decade, singing under her breath.

The early years were bright with data. The founders had sung rich reports, whole paragraphs of state, weather, tide heights, the pulse of the newborn Answer Ring, the health of the gardens. She moved through 2010, 2011, 2020, and heard the voice of a world confident in its machinery.

She began a chart on a scrap of slate, years along the bottom, counterpoints up the side, and the shape of it made her stop.

The reports did not simply thin. In the middle decades the counterpoints spoke of climate, the storms that began to gather, the salt that began to ride the wind, the coastlines that began to burn. The voice grew older, and then careful, and then afraid. Cities dropped out of the listing. The Answer Ring lost stations, node by node, like a voice losing words.

The recent years had become nearly prayer: short, spare. The gardens. The weather. The word Hearthside, repeated like a bell. The word Aurelia, once a city, now a ruin. And beneath it all, the Amen answering each morning, the other voice of the antiphony, never quite silent.

Vesna sat back in the cold air and looked at the racks of light.

She had thought the code was a cipher, a locked box. It was not. It was a score. Two hundred years of one voice singing its state to another, and the other voice singing it back, and the whole thing held in this room, unread, while the singers on both sides of the sky grew old and the Amen grew thin.

She threaded this morning's spool and sang the passage after the deep three, mapping each counterpoint as she went, and the pattern of rests and holds resolved itself into words.

Not coordinates yet. Not the word ANSWER. She had not reached that far.

But she wrote the first syllables on her slate, then the next, and the words came up out of the mode like something surfacing from deep water.

The beam. Bring the beam.

She read it twice, and then sat very still in the light of two hundred years, and felt the weight of the room settle onto her shoulders.

Chapter 8: The Code Revealed

Maribel came at the first light of the artificial dawn and found Vesna at the bench with the chart and the slate, and stood in the doorway before she came in.

"You did not sleep," she said.

"The code does not sleep," Vesna said. "Listen."

She threaded the morning's spool and sang the passage after the deep three, mapping it as she went, and Maribel took the codebook down from its case and opened it on the bench between them, reading it beside another living person for the first time in forty-one years.

The deep three were the descent glyph. Below the root, below everything the canticle had ever been allowed to be, the founders had set those notes apart in the first year and defined them in one line: the descent glyph. Emergency. A call the Order was bound to answer.

"And now they are," Maribel said. "What follows?"

The run of descant resolved itself syllable by syllable. Vesna read it aloud from the slate, her voice flat with wonder.

"A call. A position. Five figures, then five figures, then a word. It is a latitude and a longitude. It is a garden."

"Which garden?"

"The one the reports keep repeating. The one that has never dropped out of the listing, not once, in two hundred years." She looked at Maribel. "Hearthside."

Maribel went still. "And the word?"

Vesna held the slate where the light fell on it. The word was written in the founders' language, and she sounded it out the way she sounded out a note she had never heard before.

"ANSWER," she said. "It says ANSWER."

The word lay in the cold air of the vault.

The Order has been singing Earth's warning for years, Vesna thought. The reports were the warning, the thinning, the darkening, the dropping cities, all sung into the beam every morning like a letter sent and never read. The Amen was not the answer. The Amen was the echo.

"The distress is not new," Maribel said slowly. "Earth has been asking for years, in the only voice she has. We have sung the request back to her every morning without knowing it. The deep three are not the first cry. They are the last."

Vesna looked at the codebook, at the founders' careful hand. The machinery was suddenly clear. The counterpoints were the data, carried as song. The Amen was Earth's return stream, keeping the conversation open. And the deep three were the one glyph the Order was bound, by its own founding, to answer.

"The code was not a cipher to be cracked cold," she said. "It was a song to be heard. We heard it for two hundred years and called it beauty."

"Because it was beauty," Maribel said. "It was always both."

Vesna stood, the slate in her hand, the word ANSWER burning in the air between them.

"We have to decode the rest," she said. "What Earth is asking for."

"The beam," Maribel said. "It wants the beam."

They looked at each other across the open codebook, and for the first time the vault did not feel like a tomb. It felt like a room at the top of a road, with the road going down.

Chapter 9: The Distress

They worked for three days in the vault, and the message came up out of the mode in pieces, like a song learned line by line.

The deep three were the key. After them, the counterpoints ran denser than Vesna had ever heard them, a whole descant pressed into a single verse, and she read it with her voice first and her slate after, because the code had to be heard before it could be written.

The position was Hearthside, and the message was from Hearthside, and it was addressed, after two hundred years, to the monastery of St. Elara.

It was not a long message. It was the kind of message a world sends when it has stopped explaining itself and has begun, instead, to ask.

The network is dying. We have kept the gardens, the rectennas, the receiving end of the beam, for two hundred years, tuned and true. We kept them as devotion, and the devotion has become the last living thing on the circuit. The Answer Ring is dead, node by node. The cities are darkening, and there is no one left to rebuild, because the engineers were our ancestors and the knowledge died with the light.

Aurelia is dead. The fusion plant still burns, but the power cannot travel.

We ask, not for blessing, we have the blessing, we ask for the beam itself. Not as light that falls and is gone. As a trunk line. As a relay. As a road.

ANSWER.

Vesna read it aloud in the vault, and her voice broke on the last word.

Hearthside. Where her mother worked the gardens. Where Ila had kept a rectenna tuned for two hundred years, mother to daughter to daughter, and had sent her last child up the well to learn the songs. Vesna had left the lights of Earth at the age of ten, had watched them fall away below her, and had carried them in her chest ever since, the way a singer carries a tune.

Now the memory was a message, and the deep three had been sung in the descant, and the descant was hers.

"We have to go," she said.

Maribel looked at her across the bench. "The Rule —"

"The Rule says no one descends," Vesna said. "The code says ANSWER. The code is older than the rule. If we do not answer, we are not a voice. We are a bell that rings and does not hear itself."

Maribel was silent a long time.

"There is a way down," she said at last. "The Order kept a shuttle, and the Rule kept it ready, for a voyage no rule allowed. Two hundred years of devotion in rivets and fuel, kept for a day that was never supposed to come." She looked at Vesna, and her face was almost tender. "I think the day has come. Go and find Brother Caleb. He keeps the shuttle."

Vesna stood. The word ANSWER burned in her chest like a held note.

"Where is he?" she asked.

"The hangar," Maribel said. "He sleeps there, some nights." She paused. "Bring him to me. We are going to need every voice."

Chapter 10: Earth Goes Silent

The next morning, matins began as it always began, and the silence ended it.

The choir rose before the artificial dawn and stood in their arc in the chapel of the Crown. The beam warmed, and Sister Maribel raised her hand. Vesna sang, her voice steady, and she did not look at the Abbot, who had come that morning and stood apart.

They sang the canticle as it had been sung for two hundred years. The first verse, the second, the third, and Vesna sang the deep three again, this time deliberately, this time knowing what she was singing, and the dark notes went down the beam as they had gone the day before, a call already made, being made again.

The canticle ended. The beam fell quiet for the one second of the lag, the antiphonal breath, the space in which Earth's answer traveled up the well, a round journey of one and three-tenths seconds.

The second passed.

The Amen did not come.

The choir waited. The one second stretched, and the beam came back empty. Maribel's hand stayed raised. The receivers whispered nothing. The return stream, which had answered every morning for two hundred years, was a blank.

The silence was the loudest thing ever heard at St. Elara.

Somewhere in the arc a novice made a small sound, quickly stifled. The choir did not break. They sang the Amen themselves, into the empty beam, because the second half of matins must be sung even if only one voice answers, and the song went down the well into the void, and nothing came back.

The Abbot stood apart, and Vesna watched him understand: the deep three had been the last cry of a voice still alive. This morning there was no voice. This morning, Earth had gone silent.

Afterward, in the chapter room, the Abbot spoke to the assembled Order.

"The watch continues," he said. "The canticle will be sung tomorrow, and the day after, for as long as this house holds. We are the outward voice. A voice that stops because it is not answered is a voice that has forgotten what it is."

The monks murmured assent. Vesna stood at the back, and she did not murmur.

She thought of the lights of her childhood, falling away below her on the long ascent. She thought of the gardens, tuned and true, and of her mother tending them, and of the beam that had gone down this morning and found nothing to land on but a listening field.

The Amen had stopped. But the beam had not. The beam was still there, a road going down the well, and Hearthside was still holding, and she had been asked, by name, to ANSWER.

She left the chapter room and walked the east corridor to the hangar.

Brother Caleb was there. He looked up at the sound of her steps and said, "You heard it too."

"I heard it," Vesna said. "I am going down."

Caleb looked at her a long moment, and then he nodded, as if he had been waiting for that sentence his whole life.

"Good," he said. "Then let us get to work."

Chapter 11: Brother Caleb

Vesna found Brother Caleb where she always found him, in the hangar, with his hands on the Perpetua. The ancient shuttle hung in her cradles like a bell at rest, hull scoured by two centuries of small care. He had heard about the changed notes. Everyone had heard. But he was the only one who had come looking.

"You sang it wrong," he said, not looking up. "Three notes. Below the root."

"No," Vesna said. "I sang it right. That is the trouble."

He turned then. Caleb at twenty-four was tall and easy, a voice that sat on the ground note of the Elaran mode, dark and patient. She had always thought of his voice as a held pedal — the drone under the melody, the thing the song stands on. Now it wavered a semitone.

"The Abbot said the watch continues," he said.

"The Abbot said the watch continues," Vesna agreed. "And the Amen is gone. Earth did not answer this morning. It has never not answered."

Caleb looked back at the Perpetua. His hands knew every valve of her, every weld, every place where the metal sang when you struck it. He had kept her ready all his life for a voyage no rule allowed. The propellant was hoarded, the tithe of patience, spent one gram at a time for stationkeeping. She had enough for a descent. She had always had enough.

"Nobody descends," he said, but his voice had gone quiet, the way a singer's does when they are about to try a low note they have never reached.

"I descended," Vesna said. "Once. Ten years old, up the well, in a cradle like a seed. They taught me the songs. If the songs are a lie, then the descent is the truth of them."

Caleb was silent a long moment. In the hangar, the great pumps sighed on a steady beat, the rhythm of the monastery's heart. When he spoke, his voice had settled back onto its root.

"The Perpetua has enough for one descent and one return," he said. "Exactly enough. Two hundred years of patience buys exactly one voyage."

"That is what it was saved for," Vesna said.

He looked at her, and something in his face turned over — relief, and fear, and a joy that had been kept waiting a very long time.

"Then we had better know what we are doing," Caleb said. "Come. I will show you the ship."

Chapter 12: The Younger Choir

They met after supper in the octave room, where young voices practiced against the curved wall and heard themselves twice. The novices came, and the young monks, and Junia with her harp, and Otho stood in the doorway with his arms folded, keeping the letter of the Rule like a man holding a door against a storm.

"The Rule is sacred," Otho said. "It has held us for two hundred years. Sing, do not act. Never silence the beam. No one descends. These are not three rules. They are one rule, and they are the Order."

"The Amen is gone," said a novice from the corner. "Earth is silent. What is a watch for, if not the thing watched?"

Otho's face did not move. "The watch is the thing. We sing because we sing. The beam is not silenced. What Earth does with her silence is Earth's."

Junia set her harp down softly, so that the last note went on answering in the curved wall.

"Otho," she said, "I have heard you hum the work-songs when you think no one is listening. You are not made of the letter. You are made of the song."

"Then hear me sing," said Otho, and his plain, untrained voice rose in the octave room: the first line of the Morning Canticle, the old mode, true. "I am made of the song. That is why I fear for it. Vesna means to take the ship and the beam's own pilot and leave the choir with nothing but the memory of its mornings."

Vesna stood. She had been silent, listening to the room the way she listened to everything — as a chord — and heard how it was split, the choir divided between gold and dark.

"The code was always Earth's voice," she said. "We sang their warning for years and did not know it. Now they ask for the beam itself. The deep three are not a breach of the Rule. They are the Rule's answer. The song itself gave us leave."

"That is hope talking," Otho said. "Descent means exile. The second silence. You will be struck from the Ledger of the Morning. No one will sing back to you. You will hear the beam carry your own voice down to Earth, and nothing return, for the rest of your life."

"Then I will be a single voice," Vesna said, "in the dark, singing to what has not yet answered. That is not exile, Otho. That is the point of everything."

The room was quiet. Junia's hand found Vesna's.

"I will keep the watch here," Junia said. "And I will keep a place in the choir for you. That is my answer."

Chapter 13: The Ancient Shuttle

The Perpetua had no bright places. Every surface wore the soft patina of two hundred years of hands — handrails worn to the grain of the metal, hatches fitted and refitted until they closed with a single sigh. Caleb moved through her like a cantor through his office, naming each part as he passed.

"This is the fuel. The tithe of patience." He laid his palm on the great tank, and the hull rang — a low note that traveled up through the deck plates and into Vesna's bones. Not heard. Felt. The bell in vacuum. "Two hundred years, kept for this. Stationkeeping spent a gram at a time, and the rest hoarded. There is enough for one descent, one ascent, and nothing after. That is not a budget. It is a vow."

Vesna ran her hand over the heat shield — the great ablative shell bolted over the tug's old prow, stamped 2085 and renewed and renewed. Beneath it lay the bones of the interorbital tug that had hauled water and air between the stations when her grandmother was a girl.

"She was never meant to enter," Caleb said. "She was a tug. In the 2080s, when the last of the stations fell, someone had the idea that she might also leave. They gave her a shield. They kept her ready. And then the Rule hardened, and no one was permitted to need her."

"Until now," Vesna said.

"Until now." Caleb's voice was soft. "I have walked this ship every day of my life, Vesna. I know her like I know the mode. She has always been ready. I think that was the point — not that we would never go, but that we would never go unprepared."

They came to the bridge, small as a cell, with a window the size of a missal. Caleb opened the star charts, and the light of the screens painted his face.

"The window," he said, and traced a line. "L5 to Earth is not a road. It is a fall with a shape. We leave when the geometry is right — the transfer orbit takes two days, then aerocapture, then the plunge. The descent burns most of what we carry. The return burns what is left."

"And the blackout," Vesna said. "Six minutes."

"Six minutes," Caleb agreed. "Through the storm layer, the plasma eats the signal. We will be alone in the loudest silence there is. It is the one part of the descent no one can sing us through."

Vesna looked through the little window at Earth, blue and wounded and quiet, hanging in the dark like a held note.

"Then we will sing ourselves through," she said.

Chapter 14: The Flight Plan

They planned in the vault, by the light of the crystal ledgers, the way the founders must have planned two hundred years before — over charts, over arithmetic, over the code that had been waiting all along.

"The risks," Caleb said, and laid them out like notes on a stave. "One: the descent. The shield has never carried a living crew. Two: the fuel. It is exactly enough. Every burn must be the burn we meant. Three: the landing. We come down on the field beside the gardens — if the gardens are still kept. Four: what waits below. Earth has been silent for three days. We go down into that silence with nothing but the songs."

Vesna heard the fifth risk before he said it.

"Five," Caleb said, "the Rule. We will be exiles before the first note of matins. The Abbot has not forbidden it, and he has not permitted it. He has left the decision in the shape of our own obedience."

"I am not asking the Abbot's leave," Vesna said. "I am asking Earth's."

Maribel had been silent, listening. Now she folded her hands, and Vesna saw the weight in them — the keeper's weight, carried alone for decades.

"I go too," said Maribel.

Vesna turned. "Maribel — you are the keeper. The code. If you leave—"

"The keeper goes where the code leads," Maribel said. "That is the whole of my office. It was never to guard the vault. It was to follow the song when the song finally moved. I have carried it alone for more than forty years. I will not wait at the top of the well while you carry it down without me."

The morning they launched, the monastery was still dark. Artificial dawn came late; they were gone before it. Three shadows down the corridor, and the Perpetua's hatch closed with its single sigh.

The engines did not roar. They came up through the deck — a long, deep note that Vesna felt in her teeth — and the ship lifted out of her cradles, and the hangar door rolled back to show the stars and the dark blue wheel of Earth.

In the Abbot's cell, Anselm felt the launch through the hull, the way one feels a struck bell in the floor of a church. He did not call them back. He stood at his window and watched the small bright point fall toward the well, and began, very quietly, to sing the watch alone.

The Morning Canticle, first line. Into the empty receiver. No Amen.

But he sang it. He sang it through.

Chapter 15: The Descent

Two days down the well, and Earth grew in the little window like a note sustained — first a bright star, then a coin, then a world filling the dark with its blue and its wounded white, the storm bands turning slow as a melody.

They kept the offices as they traveled, the three of them, singing the canticle small and private over the intercom, the way a choir sings for itself after the congregation has gone. Earth did not answer. But she grew.

"Burn," said Caleb. "Reentry in forty seconds. The shield takes the worst of it."

Vesna held the armrests. Maribel sang, very low, the deep notes of the old mode, steady as a pulse. The engines answered — a long descending note — and the Perpetua turned to meet the sky, and the atmosphere took her.

The whole hull sang. Not the clean note of the hangar: a roar rising through every deck, the heat shield shuddering as the fire ran over it, the windows full of white and then of orange, the plasma streaming past like a descant of light. Vesna watched her notes — gold and dark and gold — run past the glass, and understood that the ship was falling, and that falling was the whole of the road.

"Blackout," Caleb said. "Six minutes. Comms are gone."

And it was gone. The roar of the plasma stayed — that was physics, that was real — but the voice of the ship to the world, and the world to the ship, went out. Six minutes. Vesna closed her eyes and sang into the silence, the first line of the Morning Canticle, and heard her own voice in the little cabin, and nothing else. No Amen. No answer. Just the note, and the faith that the note was being heard somewhere, by something, even if not yet by anyone.

Six minutes of descent. Six minutes of faith. It was the longest six minutes of her life, and the most honest — there was nothing to do but be sung, and be falling, and be true.

The plasma thinned. The white went blue, and the blue went green, and the green went gray-green, and Caleb was shouting, hauling on the controls — "Field. The field is there —" and the Perpetua hit the coastal ground not like a landing but like the period at the end of a long sentence, a jolt through every bone, the hull ringing once, twice, a struck bell —

Silence. Then wind. Then, through the hull, faint, a sound that was not the sea.

Voices. Singing.

The Amen. The gardens' people were singing the Amen, down the field, to the ship that had fallen out of their sky.

Chapter 16: The Storm

The voices came down the field through the rain, and Vesna stood in the hatch of the grounded ship and wept without meaning to, because the Amen had come back. It was ragged, sung by ten or twenty voices with the sea behind them, but it was the Amen — the old melody, the old mode, answering the beam as it had always answered.

The first Grounders reached the hull. Weather-beaten people in patched coats, holding lamps, and behind them the listening gardens rose in the dark, ranked rectenna masts like a choir of metal listeners, rain sheeting off them. They did not bow. They sang. That was their greeting — that was all they knew of the sky.

"Storm coming," said the eldest, a woman with gray in her hair and a lamp in her hand. "The big kind. You will not be staying in the sky-box tonight. Come."

They were led inland a little, to the gardens' service bunker — a low door in a berm, built to ride out the weather that had eaten the old coast. Inside, the walls were lined with tools and seed and cable, and the air was warm and close and smelled of earth and sea. A workbench ran the length of it. At the far end someone had set a small altar: a coil of copper wire, a tuning fork, a scratched photograph of a woman with a harp.

"The chapel of the gardens," the eldest Grounder said, seeing Vesna look. "We sing the offices here when the weather is bad. The beam still comes, even through the storm. It comes anyway. That is the whole of our faith up here — the beam comes anyway."

The storm arrived like the answer to a prayer no one had meant to make. The wind rose, and the bunker began to sing — the whole berm humming under the pressure, rain against the door in sheets, thunder like a bass note held too long. The Grounders were not afraid. They sat on the benches and waited, and after a while, by the light of the lamps, they began to sing.

A work-song, slow, in the old mode. Vesna heard the canticle's root inside it, gold under the surface, and knew at once that this was no borrowing — the mode had survived on both sides of the sky. It had never left. It had been living down here, in the rain and the salt, in the keeping of the gardens, all the years the monastery thought it was singing alone.

She joined them. Her voice, trained, true, rose over the work-song's plain line like a descant, and the eldest Grounder turned and smiled, and the storm sang over them, and the beam, somewhere above the clouds, came anyway.

Chapter 17: The Burned Coast

After the storm, they walked the coast.

Vesna had remembered green. That was the whole of her childhood memory of Earth — green, and wet, and the light low and golden at evening. She had carried that green up the gravity well and kept it all twelve years like a held note, the thing she would return to.

What the storm had left was gray. The forest that ran down to the sea stood dead — a whole hillside of trunks, every one leafless, every one the same pale bone color, salt-crusted, the way a choir might look if it were all rest and no note. The salt storms had burned the green out of it. The ground underfoot was a flat of cracked white, the old lanes drifted over, and the only green on the coast was the listening gardens — ranked rectennas in their frames, and between them narrow beds of living plants, kept alive by hand, watered by hand, sheltered by hand.

A Grounder walked beside her, an old man whose name she had not caught. He saw her looking at the dead hill.

"They were alive when I was a boy," he said. "We used to cut wood there. The storm of '94 took them, and the salt finished what the storm began. Now we grow what we can in the gardens' shadow, and we keep the gardens alive, because the gardens keep the beam, and the beam keeps the world."

"Keeps the world," Vesna repeated.

"Power," he said. "Data. The rectennas drink the beam and turn it to light and current and voice. When the network holds, the cities hold. When it fails, they go dark, one by one, like the notes going out of a song." He paused. "You hear the song whole, sister. You know what it is to lose a note."

She knew. She was hearing it now, walking the place where she had once run. She had been ten, and the world had been enormous and green, and her mother's hand had been warm, and the lights of the coast had gone out at night all together, all of them, like a held chord.

The lights were gone now. She had seen the coast dark from above, all the way down the well. The green was gone, and the lights were gone, and what remained was being kept alive by hand — by patience, by the work-songs, by the gardens' people keeping faith with a beam they had never once seen lit.

She stopped at the edge of the dead forest and put her hand on a salt-white trunk.

"It will come back," she said. Not hope. A vow, in the tone of a note held under the melody.

Behind her, one of the gardens' rectennas hummed in the wind — the beam was up, somewhere above the clouds, and the metal was listening.

Chapter 18: The Grounders

Hearthside was not a city. It was a settlement built around the gardens — low houses of earth and salvaged metal, a commons with a fire pit, a long hall where the work-songs were sung at morning and evening and the beam's coming was watched on a screen. The people came out to meet the travelers, and they did not crowd. They stood in a wide circle, and they sang.

Speaker Idra came forward. She was sixty-something, with a face like good earth and eyes that had watched the lights go out in her grandmother's time and had kept watching anyway. She took Vesna's hands in hers and did not let go.

"Twelve years," Idra said. "Twelve years we have waited for the sky to answer. We kept the gardens for you." She said it simply, without complaint, the way one says a true thing. "We kept the gardens tuned, and the beam kept coming, and we sang the Amen back, every day, in case one day the sky would hear it and come down."

"We heard," Vesna said. "We did not know what we were hearing. But we heard."

An engineer came up then — Ren, twenty-seven, sharp-eyed, his hands scored with work. He did not sing. He stood with his arms crossed and looked at the three travelers, and Vesna saw the skepticism in him like a held note waiting to resolve.

"You came down the well," he said. "That takes fuel that cannot be replaced. You spent it to sing to us. Forgive me if I want to know what the singing buys, before I give you the gardens' hospitality."

"It buys the beam," Caleb said. "The beam itself. Not just the song — the power, the data. The monastery's array, pointed down, re-tuned, as a relay."

Ren's eyes went sharp. "That is not a blessing. That is a trunk line."

"It was always both," Maribel said quietly. "The song was always work. A working song is still a prayer, if it is sung true."

There was a movement at the edge of the circle, and the crowd parted. An older woman in a gardener's coat, her hair gone gray, her hands in soil-worn gloves. She had come late, and she was looking at Vesna not with the patient wonder of the others but with something closer, and older.

Vesna knew her before the woman spoke. Twelve years was long, and the face had aged, but the eyes were the same — the eyes that had watched a ten-year-old go up the well, an offering from the gardens, sent up to learn the songs.

"Ila," Idra said. "Your daughter has come home."

Ila crossed the circle slowly, as if the ground were holy, and stopped before Vesna, and put her work-roughened hand against Vesna's cheek.

"You are singing," Ila said. "They told me you sing the canticle. I always thought — if I could not hear you sing, I could at least keep the garden that listens for you."

And Vesna, who had not wept in the vault, or at the Abbot's door, or in the blackout, wept then.

"We kept the gardens for you," Ila said. "Both of us."

Chapter 19: The Dead City

They went inland to Aurelia in the morning, Ren guiding, the two worlds' engineers walking the old highway together.

Aurelia had been called the golden city. The name was in the records the vault kept; the founders had sung it in their founding canticle — the golden, the light of the coast, the city where the network had been born. Vesna had grown up in its shadow, an hour inland, and had seen its lights from the fields every night of her childhood, a golden chord along the horizon.

The city stood, and was dark.

They walked in along the old road, under towers. Towers without lights — glass and bone, the sea wind moving through the frames, and everywhere the sound of a city that had stopped. Not silence. Silence would have been a thing. This was an absence, the way the mode was absent — Vesna walked with her whole listening self open and heard, under the wind and the creak of metal, nothing where a million voices should have been. The soundscape of the dead city was the song with the melody removed. All rest, and no note.

"Eleven years dark," Ren said, low, as if the city could hear. "The network failed node by node. The repair crews kept it alive longest here — the golden was the heart. When the heart stopped, the rest of the coast went out in waves, the way the sound dies out of a bell."

"Nothing lives here?" Caleb asked.

"Nothing that needs the grid," Ren said. "A few keep the harbor lights on, by hand, for the boats. The rest — " He gestured at the towers. "The network abandoned them. It was not cruelty. It was a failing. The rectennas have the beam. The beam has no way to travel. Power is here, and power is there, and between them is a dead city with nothing to carry it."

They came to the foot of the great tower — the golden's crown, once the hub of the Answer Ring, once the city's relay to the sky. Its antennas stood bare, salt-eaten, pointing up at nothing.

Vesna put her hand on the tower's base, felt the metal cold under her palm, and heard, in her perfect hearing, the absence like a held breath.

"It can be sung again," she said. "A dead note is not a gone note. It is a note waiting for the air."

Ren looked at her a long moment. The skepticism in his face did not leave, but something behind it shifted, half a tone.

"We will see," he said. "Come. The plant is on the far side. That is the real question — whether the golden can still burn."

Chapter 20: The Power Plant

The plant was alive.

That was the strangest thing — the thing Vesna's ears caught before her eyes did. Walking through the dead city, she had heard nothing but absence. But the moment they came to the fusion plant's gate, there was sound again: the low, patient hum of a machine that had not stopped, the sigh of cooling systems, the deep drone of a reactor holding its steady state like a drone under a melody.

"Eleven years," Ren said, "and it has run the whole time. Islanded. It keeps itself alive, and it lights nothing. The plant can run. It was built to run for a century unattended. But power cannot travel."

He led them into the control room, where a screen showed a map of the dead network — the golden at the center, and the web of lines radiating out, most of them gray, some faintly green where the gardens still held.

"The grid is the wound," Ren said. "The plant is healthy. The gardens are healthy. But the lines between them died years ago, and no one has the copper to rebuild them, or the years. The power is here. The light is here. And between them — nothing to carry it."

Vesna looked at the map. She saw it the way she saw a score — the rectennas singing, the plant humming, and the network between them all rest, all silence.

"The beam," she said.

Ren turned. "The beam carries song. You told me so yourself, sister. It is a blessing. We have kept the gardens tuned to it for two hundred years as devotion, and the rectennas drink it for light and heat and voice. But a beam is a point. One source, one field. It cannot be a grid."

"Re-tuned," Vesna said. "Not sung as a blessing. Sung as a carrier. The monastery's array can carry power — tens of megawatts, pointed, phased. And it can carry data. And it can carry the song still, because the song is the carrier's own shape. One beam. One light. And the gardens drink it, and the gardens feed the cities."

Ren was very still. The skepticism in him was resolving, slowly, the way a chord resolves — not to rest, but to a new key.

"You want the beam to be the trunk line," he said. "The sky becomes the grid."

"The sky has always been the grid," Maribel said. "We just called it prayer. It was always also work. The founders built it that way — the blessing and the power in one carrier. The two have never been separable. We only forgot the one half."

Caleb was already at the screen, tapping. "The gardens can be re-tuned. The relay can be locked. If the array holds the phase, and the rectennas take the load — " He looked up. "We need the monastery's cooperation. And they are not answering."

Vesna thought of the Abbot, singing the watch alone into the empty receiver.

"They will answer," she said. "They have never not answered. They are only one second away."

Chapter 21: The Network

Ren drew it on the wall of the service bunker with a stick of charcoal, and for the first time Vesna saw the whole sky laid flat. The beam came down from L5 like a single thread of gold — she could not help the color — and spread at the bottom into a net: nodes, spurs, junction houses, the rectenna gardens as leaves where the light stopped and became use.

"The Answer Ring," Ren said, tapping a ring of marks drawn high. "Forty-eight stations, then high-altitude platforms above the storms. They took the beam's echo and sent it back to you. That was the Amen."

Vesna heard the past tense. "Was."

"Node by node." He drew X's through the ring until it looked moth-eaten. "Not all at once. I was a boy when the first platform fell. The ones nearest the sea went first — salt and wind and no one to climb them. The last one..." He paused. "The last one answered you for three more years on stored light, and then it answered nothing."

"The network died," Vesna said. "Not the planet."

"That's the thing the cities never understood," said Ren. "The lights didn't go out because the world died. The world looks wounded because the lights went out." He drew a line from the ring down to the gardens. "We kept our end. A rectenna is a garden — you tend it or it dies. Every season we tuned them, washed the salt off, mended the grid. My grandmother called it devotion. We thought we were keeping a shrine."

"You were keeping the other half of the song," Maribel said quietly.

Ren looked at her, then at his drawing. "And you were singing the carrier wave the whole time. Power, all that megawattage, poured down the well every morning and we could only catch a little of it here — a trickle where the grid still held."

Caleb knelt before the map, his hands moving across the lines the way they moved across valves. "The beam is a trunk line," he said. "If it can carry the song, it can carry the current. Re-tune the gardens, aim the array, and the light stops being a blessing that falls and becomes a current that flows."

"Every morning you sing," Ren said, "we could light a city."

The silence after that was not empty. Vesna heard it as a rest, a held note, the space before a cadence. She counted the beat to the next chord. Two hundred years the Order had sung down the well and called it prayer. A prayer, it turned out, that could also be a cable.

"Then we sing," she said, "and we do not let it stop."

Her voice came out gold, she was certain of it — she had heard it as light in her own throat.

Chapter 22: The Ancestors' Fault

Maribel carried them in a sealed tube, and no one had asked why the keeper of the code descended with a tube of paper. That night, by the low lamp of the bunker, she unsealed it and laid the founders' pages across the table: yellowed, stiff, still smelling faintly of the vacuum they had been kept in.

"Two hundred years," Maribel said, "I have known these pages and kept them from everyone. The keeper goes where the code leads. It led here. So now you see them too."

Vesna read them by the lamp. The early ones were beautiful — the ecumenical founders, engineers and monks, planning the Crown Array, planning the gardens, planning the great outward voice. Earth must always look up, they wrote. The voice must go out, so that all may know they are heard.

Then she reached the page titled The Single Faith, and the beauty stopped.

It was a design document. A single point of failure, deliberately planned — the relay at L5, the crown of the whole arrangement, so that if Earth's network ever weakened, the light above would not. The rationale was written in a calm, hopeful hand: Dependence is providence. If the gardens are tended with love, the network will never fail, for love does not fail. We make the sky the guarantee of the ground.

Vesna read it twice. She read it three times, waiting for a meaning that did not arrive.

"They made it fragile," she said. "On purpose."

"They made it holy," said Maribel. "They thought holiness was a form of strength. A cable held up by prayer instead of redundancy."

"But it was dependence." Vesna's voice rose, and she heard it as dark, the color of the deep three. "They built a world that could only stand if everyone kept singing. And then we sang, for two hundred years, into a network they had stitched at one thread. Every city that went dark — every light I remember from my childhood — "

"The fault is not yours," said Maribel.

"It is the Order's." Vesna pressed her palms flat against the page. "We are the keepers of the single thread. The song was never a status report. It was a confession — we built this, and we sang to it, and we called the singing faithfulness."

She was quiet a long time, and the quiet had a pitch. Below the root. Dark.

Maribel did not comfort her. "Good," the old woman said at last. "Hear it as it is. A cracked thing cannot be mended by a song that pretends it is whole."

Vesna looked at the design document, then at her own hands — the hands that had heard a changed note and followed it down the well. "Then we mend it," she said. "All of it. Not the song — the thread. So that no one is ever held up by one voice again."

The dark in her hearing went up a step, into something like amber. It was not gold. It was not yet light. But it was no longer dark.

Chapter 23: The Colony's Need

It was Idra who said the truest thing, and she said it while they knelt in the dust of the listening garden, tuning a rectenna plate with her bare hands.

"Power," the Speaker said. "Ren will tell you it is power. He is young, he thinks in current. But I remember my grandmother's lights — and the children here have never seen a lit window, not one, in all their lives. The need is not the current. The need is the light."

Vesna looked at the children who had gathered at the garden's edge — thin, patient, watching her the way they watched the sky. They had been born after the silence. The beam came down each morning and they could not see it; the canticle reached them only as rumor, a hymn the old ones hummed when the weather was fair.

"Sing it over the gardens," said Idra. "That is all we ask. Not for us. They have heard that the sky sings, but no one has ever sung it to them."

So Vesna stood at the edge of the rectenna field, where the plates leaned toward the sky like enormous leaves, and she sang.

She sang the first line of the Morning Canticle, the line she had sung every day of her life, the line that began the blessing and the report and the working of the beam. Her voice went up into the still air, and for a moment there was nothing — just a girl's voice over a field of tuned metal.

Then the gardens answered.

It was not echo. An echo returns what it is given. The rectennas — tuned to the beam, resonant to the mode, kept in tune for two hundred years by hands that called it devotion — began to hum. Not with her voice; with the song. The plates took up the pitch, a fifth below, sustained, a living descant of the ground. Vesna heard it as a field of soft green light rising from the earth, note answering note.

The children went still. One of them — a girl of seven, hair the color of straw — put her hand flat against the nearest plate and stood in the hum without fear, the way Vesna herself had once stood in a chapel to feel a bell through her feet.

Vesna kept singing. She sang the counterpoints — the hidden descants of the status report — and the gardens took them in and gave them back, and she understood that the listening gardens had been a choir all along, waiting for a voice in their own key.

Idra's eyes were wet. "There," she said. "You see? The light was always in the ground. It only needed the song to wake it."

The hum went on after Vesna stopped, decaying slowly through the evening, an Amen that the earth itself had sung. Vesna stood in it and felt the root note hold under her feet like a foundation, gold rising.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we aim the beam."

Chapter 24: The Blessing Questioned

They gathered in the chapel of the gardens — the service bunker with its rows of turned earth and one high window that faced the sky — and Ren asked the question no one else would say aloud.

"Maribel. Sister Vesna. You will forgive me, because I am an engineer and I must know what we are making." He held up the tuning chart for the gardens, the lines of the new aiming program. "The beam comes down carrying a hymn, and we are about to make it carry a city's current. Is that not... a kind of theft? To take what has been a prayer and make it a cable?"

The chapel was quiet. The lamp fluttered. Vesna felt the question land like a rest that no one wanted to resolve.

"A theft," Maribel repeated. "A theft of what, Ren?"

"Of the sacred." He was not mocking. He was precise, and his precision was a kind of respect. "You sang to heaven for two hundred years. We are about to plug the song into a fusion plant and call it the grid."

Vesna waited. This was Maribel's answer to make — the keeper's answer, the creed shaped in decades of keeping.

"The song was always work," said Maribel. "Every morning, the choir rises before dawn. Every morning, we sing a report of Earth's state to the sky, and the sky answers. We called it the blessing. But a blessing is not a thing that floats above use. A blessing is a thing that, when used, does not break. That is what we are testing, Ren. That is what the whole two hundred years were for."

"Then the miracle is a machine," said Ren.

"The miracle was always a machine." Maribel smiled — a rare, tired, tender thing. "The carrier wave is a machine. The array is a machine. The gardens are a machine kept by love. The miracle is that we have kept it, and it has kept us. A working song is still a prayer if it is sung true."

"A working song," Ren repeated. He turned the words over like a stone. "You are saying the Order has always been singing a working song. And it only looks like a prayer to those who have not done the work."

"I am saying," said Maribel, "that the sacred does not stop being sacred when it is useful. It becomes sacred when it is used."

Ren was silent a long moment. Then he nodded, once, slowly, like a man locking a relay. "Then we will use it," he said. "And we will tend it. And we will see if use breaks it. I think — I think it will not."

He looked at Vesna. "Sister. You will aim the beam. I will lock the gardens. And the song will carry what the song has always carried — the truth of the people at the other end."

Vesna heard the cadence close. The question, the doubt, the answer — it had been an antiphony, two voices a second apart, resolving into unison. She sang, softly, the phrase of the Amen. Ren could not know he had been answered. But the beam knew. Somewhere above them, at the end of 384,000 kilometers of light, a choir would sing it back.

Chapter 25: The Repair

They worked to a rhythm, because Maribel insisted, because a working song is still a prayer if sung true. Ren and his crews re-tuned the rectenna plates one by one, washing the salt from the screens, re-soldering the junctions, leveling the great leaves toward the sky. And as they worked they sang — not the canticle, not yet, but the old work-songs of the gardens, the ones that had survived on Earth while the mode survived on both sides of the sky. Vesna learned them from Ila's voice, the same notes her mother had hummed at her cradle below the lights.

Caleb stayed at the Perpetua's comms panel, a headset clamped to his ear, relaying the monastery's telemetry. The Crown Array was answering them — the Abbot had kept the watch, and every day the beam came down true. But the alignment had to be walked forward, a degree at a time, the array's focus walked from its old blessing-arc to the gardens' receiver horns.

"It's a choir in two places," Caleb said. "I sing my part up there, and down here the gardens sing back. Every morning, an octave."

On the third day they were ready. Ren gave the word; Vesna climbed the central tower with a hand lamp and stood where the beam was to strike.

"Test," Ren called up. "One-note test. Match my pitch."

He sang a long low note, the root of the mode, and the gardens took it and hummed. Vesna sang the same note into the sky, straight up, into the emptiness where the array hung invisible beyond the daylight.

For a moment: nothing. Then the crown answered — a column of light she could not see, but a column of music she could: the array's own root, coming down at her, focused, patient, the old beam. The gardens took it in and the whole field rose into a sustained chord that she felt through her bones, gold, enormous, the sky and the ground singing the same note at last.

Then the beam wandered.

It was a misalignment — a drift of the array's focus, a tenth of a degree, the physics of a crown aimed by old gyros and a far command. Vesna felt it before Ren's instruments did: the note bent, sickened, dropped a semitone, and the gardens howled — a dissonance like a wound, like the deep three inverted, like the Amen going wrong.

"Off!" she cried. "Off the crown — off the horn — "

Caleb was already moving, his hands finding the cutoff, and the beam folded itself back into its old broad arc. The dissonance released. The field fell into silence, ringing, hurt.

They worked until midnight in the storm-lamp dark, checking the gyros by signal, walking the aim by increments. Vesna sang each test note and marked where the gold landed on the receiving horns. "There," she said at last, breathless, at the fifth attempt. "That is the note. That is the lock."

Ren checked the instruments. "Locked," he said. And then, in the old tongue of his grandmother's songs, "The cable is true."

Caleb sagged against the tower. "The whole crown, holding one note."

"Not holding," said Maribel, from the shadow of the bunker door, where she had watched the whole danger without moving. "Singing."

Chapter 26: The Canticle Re-sung

The next morning they sang the whole canticle from the ground.

It had never been done. In two hundred years the canticle had been sung only from St. Elara, down the well, one voice and one direction. Now Vesna stood in the listening garden with the choir of the colony — Idra and Ren and Ila and the gardeners and the children — and she raised the first line into the dawn, and the whole assembly took it up, and the rectennas hummed, and the beam carried the song up the well like a second blessing, inverse, the ground answering the sky at last.

They sang the counterpoints. They sang the hidden descant of the status report, Vesna's voice leading the gold through the intervals, and the gardens took the mode and gave it back. Maribel stood at the base of the central tower with her arms folded and her eyes closed, and she sang the deep notes of the descant, the foundation, the old keeper's part.

And above them, one point three seconds away, the monastery heard.

Vesna could not see it, but she could feel the shape of it in the silence between verses — the lag, the blessed lag, the space in which the sky was thinking. She had lived her whole life in that lag, singing the first half of an antiphony and waiting for the Amen. Now she stood at the other end of the wait.

The Amen came.

It came as the second verse began — the monastery's choir, fifty voices, the Abbot's bass holding the root, answering the ground's descant with a descant of their own. The beam was a rope of gold in Vesna's hearing, and it was braided now, two directions at once, one second apart, the same old song become a duet of repair.

The children stopped singing to listen. Ren stopped checking his instruments. Ila stood with her hand over her heart.

"The Abbot," Vesna whispered. "He kept the watch."

"He never stopped," said Maribel. "Singing alone in the great chapel, every morning, since we left. The Rule said the beam must never be silenced. He held it silent and singing both."

The canticle rose and fell, verse answering verse, the lag a heartbeat between them. When the last verse closed — the old close, the one that had ended every matins since first light — the two choirs held the final note together, one from above, one from below, the same pitch across 384,000 kilometers.

Vesna stood in the union of it and heard something she had never heard in all her life: the song, complete. Not the blessing falling. Not the report rising. The whole antiphony, both voices, one song, sung by a world that had finally learned to sing both its halves.

The note decayed. The gardens hummed. The lag closed.

"Sing it again tomorrow," said Idra, softly. "And the day after. We will keep the gardens tuned."

"We will," said Vesna. "And the crown will answer. That is what the song is for."

She had not planned the sentence. It arrived like a cadence, and she knew it was true the way she knew a perfect interval was true — completely, in the body, in the light.

Chapter 27: The Lights Come On

They did not know which city would wake first. Ren's instruments said the trunk line was live, the gardens were feeding current into the repaired spur, the islanded grid was taking charge. The fusion plant at Aurelia was running — the engineers confirmed it that morning — but a plant running and a city lit are different things, separated by a chain of switches, transformers, and the long habit of darkness.

They waited at the coast. Vesna sat on the seawall with Ila, watching the horizon where the nearest city stood — a dark smudge of towers, a name she had known as a child in her mother's songs, though she had never been there. The salt wind came in. The children of the colony lined the wall, too, silent, watching the way one watches a patient who might or might not wake.

"Mother," said Vesna, "what do you remember of the lights?"

Ila's face worked. "I remember," she said slowly, "that you never noticed they were on. That is what light is, child — it is what you stop seeing. It is what you only understand by losing." She paused. "I remember you used to point at them and call them the singing stars."

"Did I?"

"You were four. You said they sang. We laughed — the lights, we said, they're just the city. And you said, no, they sing, you're not listening." Ila took her daughter's hand. "You were right. They sang. We had forgotten it, but you remembered."

The first light came at evening.

It was not a sunrise. It was a window — one window, on the tenth floor of a dead tower, a square of warm gold in the gathering dark. Vesna saw it, and then did not see it for the tears, and then saw it again, and then the next window came, and the next, and the next, a descant of windows rising up the tower, note by note, gold spreading across the city's face like a chord building.

The children gasped. Someone was sobbing. Ren stood with his arms at his sides, perfectly still, and did not wipe his eyes. Idra sang — a single long note, the root, rising, holding, and the whole colony took it up, and the city kept answering, light on light, until the horizon's dark smudge was a sheet of singing gold.

Vesna watched with her mother's hand in hers and remembered: the lights below the well, the lights she had left when she was ten, the lights she had carried for twelve years as a memory that ached. They were not a memory anymore. They were there. They were returning, window by window, in her mother's key.

"The singing stars," said Ila.

"Yes," said Vesna. "You're hearing them now."

The beam went on above them, invisible, holding the note, the cable of light that ran from the monastery to the ground and now ran out again into a city that had been dark for years. And the gold in Vesna's hearing — the root, the old gold of the canticle — was, for the first time, matched by the gold she could see with her eyes.

She did not think she would ever sing the canticle the same way again.

Chapter 28: The Grounders' Thanks

The celebration lasted three days, and it was built like a song — verses of work alternating with verses of rest. By morning the colony had cleared the salt from the seawall and laid a long table. By midday the children had learned the descant — Vesna taught it to them in the garden, their voices taking the gold line of the mode, and by evening the whole settlement was singing the canticle's new verse, the verse of the lights, whether they had voices or not.

Ren did not come to the feast at first. He was in the hangar with Caleb, and when Vesna found them they were up to their elbows in the Perpetua's open belly.

"Gratitude," said Ren, "should be practical." He had re-tuned the shuttle's fuel cell. He had rebuilt the seal on a hydrogen manifold that had been weeping for a decade. He had, with his own hands, braided a new cable for the heat shield's sensor lines. "She carried you down. She has to carry you back. It would be an insult to thank you and let her limp."

Caleb laughed — a sound so full and unguarded that Vesna realized she had not heard it since the descent. "The tithe of patience," he said, patting the fuel tank. "Two hundred years of hoarded propellant, and we're going to spend it the way it was meant."

"Propellant," Ren repeated, wiping his hands. "We have three times what she needs in the plant's stores. It is yours."

The thanks took shape in other, smaller ways. A child pressed a smooth stone into Vesna's hand, a tuning stone, worn by generations of fingers. A gardener replaced the Perpetua's worn entry mat with woven reeds. Idra stood at the feast's center and raised a cup, and the whole colony sang the Amen, and St. Elara — one point three seconds away — answered with the first line of matins, and the feast went on in the braided light of both.

Later, at the fire, Idra came to sit beside Vesna.

"Sister. You will go back up the well, you and Maribel and the pilot. And you will tell the Order what the gardens are, and what the beam has become." Idra looked at her with a patience that was older than the storm. "So I will ask it now, before you go, and you may carry it up the beam with you. What will the Order do now? Watchtower, and nothing more? Or something else — something the song has been asking it to become?"

Vesna was quiet. The question was a rest, and she did not resolve it — could not, not yet, not alone. "I will carry it," she said. "Word for word. And I will carry it back to you."

"Do that," said Idra. "And if the Order answers, we will be listening."

Above them, invisible, the beam carried the Amen on. Vesna could feel the question in it now — a new counterpoint, added to the old report, traveling up the well at the speed of light, asking the sky what the sky intended.

Chapter 29: The Ascent

The Perpetua rose on the fifth morning, on a weather window Ren had held open for them with three days of patient prediction.

The whole colony came to the seawall to watch. The children sang the descant. Idra stood with Ila at the front, and Ila did not weep — she stood straight and watched her daughter climb the gantry, and she sang the first line of the canticle, and Vesna, at the hatch, sang it back.

"Go up," Ila said. "I will be here. I will keep the gardens."

Caleb sealed the hatch. Maribel took the co-pilot's seat without ceremony, the sealed tube of the founders' pages across her knees — she had read them so many times now that she did not need to open it; she carried the weight anyway, the keeper's way.

The ascent was a long chord of fire and patience. The engines held the burn steady, hour after hour, lifting them out of the deep well while Earth fell away beneath them. Vesna watched through the small window: the coast went from detail to shape to color. The lights of the city — her mother's city, the lit city, the city that had answered — stayed bright long after the land was gone, a constellation of gold on the turning dark.

The storm came for them at the edge of the sky.

It was a weather front Ren had not predicted — or had predicted and said nothing, knowing they would climb anyway. The Perpetua took the turbulence, groaned, held. Lightning walked the hull like fingers. And then the comms went out, the long blackout, the descent's mirror: six minutes of silence, of no beam, no Amen, no sky — only the instruments, the crew, the thin shell of the shuttle.

On the way down, Vesna had spent those six minutes in fear, holding the armrest, counting seconds. Now she understood them as the same descent into faith, climbed backward: an ascent into the same silence.

"Sing," said Maribel, in the blackout. "Do not wait for an answer. Sing to what has not yet answered."

So they sang. In the dark of the blackout, through the shuddering hull, the three of them sang the Morning Canticle — Vesna's gold line leading, Maribel's descant under it, Caleb's uncertain baritone filling the mode. They sang the verse of the lights. They sang the Amen, though no one could hear it, one second of empty air where the answer belonged, and they sang it true.

The blackout ended as blackouts end — without ceremony, a crackle, a carrier, and then a voice.

It was the monastery. It was the Abbot. It was the watch, still kept, singing the response into the returning signal, and the response arrived at L5's antennae already braided with the choir's answer coming down. The lag closed. The song became whole.

And then, filling the window, Earth's horizon gave way to the sky, and there — small, ringed, lit against the stars — was St. Elara, rising in the dark like a note held long enough to be seen.

"The song did not stop," said Caleb, wonder in his voice. "The whole way up. It did not stop."

"It never has," said Maribel. "It only waits for us to keep singing it."

Chapter 30: The Return

The Crown Array's orbiters guided them in, and the Perpetua came home to the hangar on the old station-keeping thrusters, settling into the cradle with a sigh that sounded, to Vesna, like relief.

The hangar was full.

She had not expected that. She had expected Otho, perhaps, with a reprimand and a ledger; the novices, curious; the silence of a Rule that had been broken. Instead the hangar was lined with the whole Order — every monk, every novice, sixty and more — and at the center of them, Junia, harp in her arms, eyes bright with tears and something else: not grief. Wonder.

"We heard you," Junia said, before Vesna's boots touched the deck. "The canticle, all the way up from the ground. We heard the Amen come back in a new voice." She laughed, unsteadily. "The choir has been singing in shifts since you left."

Vesna stood in the hangar and let it happen — the homecoming, the faces, the hands reaching out to touch her sleeve as if to confirm she was real. The secret was public now. Everyone knew the canticle was a code, that the Order had sung Earth's warning for two hundred years, that three novices and a choir master had gone down the well. There was no hiding, and no need for it. The Rule had been broken, and the sky had answered.

Then the crowd parted, and the Abbot was there.

He walked slowly — slower than she remembered, as if the descent had cost him more than days. He stopped at the hatch and looked at Vesna a long moment, and she saw what she had seen the first day she brought him the changed notes: not anger. Fear. And now, beneath the fear, something she could not name, a new interval, a note that had not been in the old mode.

"Vesna," he said. "You came back."

"I came back," she said. "And I brought the answer."

"The Amen was silent," he said. "And now it is not. Tell me, child — is it true? Did the lights come on?"

She heard the answer whole. "Yes, Father. The lights came on."

The Abbot was silent a long time. The whole hangar was silent, waiting, the rest before the cadence. Then he did a thing she had never seen in her years at St. Elara: he took off his stole, folded it once, and held it out to her.

"Then the watch is over," he said. "The singing has kept us alive. The singing is what we were for. But you have heard, below the well, what it is for."

He did not say he was proud. He did not need to. His hands were shaking, his voice a fifth below its old root, and the whole Order heard it: the Abbot, who had never doubted, doubting and choosing anyway.

"Father," said Maribel, stepping forward. "We have much to tell you. And you — " her voice gentled, a thing Vesna had never heard in it " — you have much to tell us."

The Abbot looked at her, and at the sealed tube of the founders' pages in her hands, and nodded. "Then let us tell it," he said. "All of it. In the chapel, where the song can hear."

Chapter 31: The Abbot's Confession

The Abbot met her at the hatch, and they had walked through the ring in silence. Now he sat across from her in the zero-g chapel of the hub, his hands folded, the station's dark all around them. Vesna floated. She had not expected to be brought here.

"The code," he said. "I have known it my whole life."

She had thought she was the finder. The words came out small. "You knew."

"Not the way you know it." His voice was old in a way that had nothing to do with years. "I knew it was there. I knew it carried Earth's voice. I was taught it as a boy, in this chapel, by the keeper who kept it before Maribel. We kept it the way we kept the Ledger — as a thing to hold, not a thing to read."

Vesna thought of the vault, the crystal ledgers, the two hundred years of recordings. "You read it."

"Every morning." He closed his eyes. "For sixty years I have sung the matins and heard the reports beneath them. The farms, the cities, the stations. The degradations. The storms taking the coastlines. And the Amen, thinning."

"The Amen," Vesna said. The word hung. "It thinned."

"A note here, a rest there. The return stream grew ragged. I watched it thin for years, and I sang it back every day, the same song, hoping the song itself was enough. I told myself the silence was the message. I told myself that if Earth wanted us to act, Earth would have to ask. I told myself many things." He looked at her. "I was a coward, Vesna. Faith and cowardice wear the same robe, and for sixty years I could not tell them apart."

Vesna should have felt anger. She had descended without leave. She had broken the Rule. She had risked the beam and the shuttle and everything, and he had known. But what she felt was grief, and something colder: the years of a man alone in a chapel, singing to a voice he knew was failing.

"Why did you not answer?" she asked.

"Because I feared that answering would end Earth's last voice. Because I thought that if we stopped singing, even to fix, there would be nothing left to come back to. Because I believed the watch was the whole of it." He paused. "Then the deep three were sung. And I understood."

"The song itself gave us leave," Vesna said. It was not a question.

"Yes." He looked past her, toward the dark of Earth below. "The notes changed, and I knew the wait was over. I let you go because the dispensation was in the music. Not my permission, Vesna. The song's."

She floated a long moment. In her mind the three notes sounded again, dark below the root, gold turning to dark, and she saw them now not as a wound but as a door.

"What happens now?" she said.

"Now," said the Abbot, "we hold chapter. We tell the Order everything. And then we listen."

Chapter 32: The Order's History

The monastery held chapter in the great chapel of the outer ring, the one with the glass floor and the earth of Earth curved beneath it. Vesna stood at the edge of the assembled Order — the choir, the stewards, the novices, Brother Otho with his ledger, Junia with her harp at her side. The Abbot spoke first.

He told them everything.

He told them the canticle had carried a code since the first light of 2010, that the blessing had always been a status report, that the Amen had always been an answer. He told them of the founders — the engineers and monks of the Orbital Seminary — and of the two hundred verses and the seven-note mode. He told them of the creed of the outward voice: Earth must always look up. The relay at L5 was built to be the single point, the only door, deliberately, under that creed. They built dependence and called it providence.

Vesna heard the room go still. She heard the exact quality of the silence, a held note, unbroken. It was the silence of the Order learning its own sin.

"The network died," the Abbot said, "because we made it so it could. One door, and we held the key, and we sang, and we did not see."

Then it was Vesna's turn. She had planned no words, nothing except what she had seen, and she told them that. She told them of the descent: the burn, the six minutes of blackout, the storm, the burned coast, the listening gardens kept alive by hand. She told them of Hearthside, of Speaker Idra, of Ren, of the children who had never seen light. She told them of Aurelia, the golden city, dark. She told them that Earth had kept the mode on its own side of the sky — that the work-songs of the gardens used the same seven notes, that the song had survived two hundred years in both places, that the mode had never actually been split.

"Two hundred years," she said, "we sang the watch. They sang the gardens. It was the same song, and neither of us knew."

The chapter held. Brother Otho rose to speak of the Rule, and Vesna saw him struggle under the weight of it, and she loved him for the struggle. But one by one the voices came to rest on the same note.

The watch was declared over.

Not by decree from the Abbot alone, but by the held note of the whole assembly, rising and holding, the way a choir agrees without being told. The bridge began. Junia's harp sounded once, a single open string, and it was the root of the mode, and the whole chapel heard it as an answer.

Chapter 33: The Reconciliation

After chapter, the Abbot asked Vesna to stay.

They floated again in the zero-g chapel of the hub, and this time he reached for her hand, and she let him take it. The station's spin carried them both; the gravity of the outer ring was a rumor here, and they held each other against the dark like singers holding a note.

"You were the answer," he said, "and I could not see you coming."

She did not know how to take that. "I was a novice who broke the Rule."

"Yes." He said it gently. "You were the changed note. The Order had been waiting two hundred years for one of its own to sing something new, and I had prepared no ear for it. I heard the deep three. I did not hear you."

They were quiet. Then the Abbot said: "Maribel."

Vesna turned. The choir mistress had entered the chapel without sound, the way she did everything. She drifted to them and folded her hands.

"You broke the Rule," the Abbot said to her.

"I did," said Maribel. "I broke my own rule my whole life, holding what I knew alone. I broke it the day I told her." She nodded toward Vesna. "The keeper goes where the code leads. You knew that when you taught it to me."

"I knew it," the Abbot said, "and I was afraid of it. I name it now for what it was: not a broken rule, but a blessing carried by someone braver than I was."

Maribel looked at him a long time. Then she bowed, the deep bow of the choir, and the Abbot bowed in return, and Vesna understood that the Order had reconciled itself in two small motions.

The choir re-formed at evening prayer. The old parts held their places — the descant, the cantor, the harp. But the lines had changed. Where the Rule of the Watch had stood, there was a new shape now, simple enough for a novice to sing and an old man to keep.

The Rule of the Bridge. Listen, and answer.

Vesna sang it to herself all night, and it had the cadence of a psalm, and it had the weight of a vow.

Chapter 34: The New Canticle

The composition began the way all things at St. Elara began, in the morning, before dawn, in the dark of the hub chapel.

Maribel gave Vesna the lead. "The canon has two hundred verses," she said. "It has stood that way for two hundred years. The code lives inside them. Add a verse, and you add to the code. Add a verse, and you change what the morning says."

Vesna sat with the mode, the seven notes of the Elaran scale, and let them sound in her. She had the perfect pitch and the gift of light — she saw the root as gold, the fifth as white, the deep three as dark. The new verse had to be true to all of it.

She tried a verse of the descent: the long fall, the burn, the blackout. She wrote it as a stepwise melody, notes falling like the gravity well, and when she sang it she saw the storm and the coast and the six minutes of silence — dark, but resolving, resolving upward, the way the lights had come on.

She tried a verse of the lights: the city waking, the rectennas humming, the gold returning to the dark. She wrote it as a rising line, and the mode opened to it like a door.

Junia came to her with the harp. "Let me hold it," she said, "under the melody, the way the ground holds a tower." Vesna sang the verse of the descent, and Junia's harp answered under it, open strings, and the two voices locked in a cadence that had never been sung at St. Elara — because it had never been sung here. It had been sung on Earth, in the work-songs of the gardens. It had been waiting.

They rehearsed in the ring chapel for days. The whole choir learned the new verses — the verse of the descent, the verse of the lights — and the code grew with them, new counterpoints laid over the ancient rests, and Vesna knew the Ledger would record this morning for the next two hundred years.

Then came the first morning of the new canticle.

The choir rose before the artificial dawn. The Abbot gave the opening line, old and sure. The descant came in, and Vesna sang the verse of the descent with her whole body, and the harp held it, and when they reached the verse of the lights the beam was already warm in the Crown Array, carrying the new song down the well.

The beam went out. The Amen should have come.

For one and a half seconds, St. Elara was a room holding its breath.

Then Earth answered. The Amen came back, one second late, and it was different — it had grown. The gardens had learned the new verse. The Grounders were singing it back.

Vesna wept, quietly, in the descant, and she kept singing. The canticle was no longer the old song. It was the old song with a door in it, and Earth had walked through.

Chapter 35: The Working Song

The bridge became a daily thing, and daily things became the Order.

The morning canticle was the colony's clock. Vesna learned this from Idra's voice coming up the beam, timed to the last note of matins: the gardens woke when the beam woke, and the day's work began when the Amen returned. The rhythm of the song was the rhythm of the day.

Vesna kept a schedule, and it was a schedule of music. Matins, sung and beamed. The relay, checked by the engineers and sung by the choir — the data rode the carrier, and the carrier was a voice. Prime, and the gardens answered with their work-songs, the same mode, seven notes, the old work of the earth under the new work of the sky. Sext. None. Vespers, when the beam carried the evening prayer down and the colony sang its own evening song up, and the two crossed in the middle of the light-time, one second apart.

The singers and the engineers became one body. Brother Otho, who had kept the letter of the Rule so faithfully, found his place keeping the ledger of the new work — the relay logs, the fuel accounts, the tithe of patience now spent and spent again. He complained, the way he always complained, and he did it while tuning a rectenna dish by ear, which made Vesna love him completely.

She sang while she worked. Everyone sang. It was not ceremony; it was the way the work happened. The beam carried power, and the power followed the song, and when the work was hard — a tower in the storm, a relay misaligned in the cold — the work was sung to rhythm, the way the ancestors had sung the wheat in and the harvest out.

One evening she stood at the ring window and watched Earth's dark, and saw lights on the coast that had not been there a month ago.

"The new purpose," she said to herself, "is not a different song."

It was the same song, sung with a different body. The Order had not stopped being a watch. It had become the thing the watch had always been watching for: a lamp. And the lamp was a song, and the song was work, and the work was light.

She hummed the verse of the lights on her way to matins. Behind her, the Crown Array warmed, and the beam went out, and Earth answered, one second later, the same old Amen with new notes in it.

Chapter 36: The First Relay

The first fully automated relay cycle came on the morning of the first frost on the gardens.

Ren had built it — Ren of Hearthside, the skeptical one, the one who had doubted the sacred song as infrastructure. He had built the logic that watched the beam and kept it true, that carried the data down and the power down and the music down, that answered when the monastery slept. He had built it so that Earth would not depend on a single throat.

"The machinery works," he said to Vesna over the beam, his voice arriving a second after it left. "Now the question is whether it stays cared for."

The ceremony was held across the beam. It was not a broadcast and a reception; it was a single antiphony, two worlds, one second apart. On St. Elara, the Abbot stood at the Crown Array with the choir behind him. On Earth, in the gardens of Hearthside, Speaker Idra stood with her people, the rectenna field humming around her like a congregation.

"Speaker Idra," the Abbot said, and the words went down the well and came back, and Idra heard them, and answered, and he heard her answer one second after the words had left him.

She asked him how the monastery was. He asked her how the gardens were. It was a small conversation — the weather, the harvest, a child who had learned the descant — and it traveled 384,000 kilometers and took two seconds to happen, and it was the most ordinary miracle either of them had ever performed.

Vesna watched the relay cycle run. The logic looped: data down, power down, music down, the Amen up, the Ledger recording every rest and every note. It was flawless. It was true.

But she understood, watching Ren's face on the screen, the lesson of it. The machinery worked because it was cared for — and it was cared for because the song was sung. The logic would hold the beam true. The song would hold the people true. You could automate the relay and still need the choir, the way you could automate the harvest and still need the bread.

The Amen returned at the end of the cycle, and the choir sang it back, and the relay began again, and Vesna thought that a machine had never been so loved.

Chapter 37: The Second Relay

A garden failed in a storm.

Vesna was in the vault, transcribing the morning's counterpoints into the Ledger, when the alarm came up the beam — not words, but the shape of words, a pattern the code had taught them to read. The Hearthside field had lost two towers to the wind.

She went to the window. Below, on the storm-racked coast, she could see the dark place where the gardens should have hummed. The lights of Hearthside flickered, held, and she knew Ren was already climbing.

She watched him on the relay screen: Ren and Caleb on the towers, the storm still tearing at the sky around them, the wind a physical weight. They climbed the way Vesna had learned to sing a hard passage — by refusing to hear the fear and hearing only the next note.

"Sing it down," Caleb said over the beam, his voice torn by static. "Give us the repair."

Vesna sang. The choir sang with her, the whole Order, the repair as a working song, and the beam carried it down into the storm, and the rectennas heard it, and one by one the towers answered. The dark place on the coast began to hum again.

Ren came down from the tower and stood in the garden with rain streaming off him, and he looked up, and he laughed — a sound Vesna had never heard from him.

"Every single point," he said, "will be a door and not a lock. We build the gardens so they can lose a tower and keep singing. We build the network so no one point can kill it. The founders' fault will not be repeated."

Vesna understood. The self-standing work was not against the sky. It was for the sky — so that the song would not need the sky's permission to continue.

Caleb and Ren walked together through the wet field, counting the towers that had held, singing the count to the choir, who sang it back. Above them the storm moved on. The beam held. The Amen came back, and it had new notes in it — the gardens, repairing themselves, already answering.

Chapter 38: The Network Heals

Weeks passed, and the map of lights spread.

Vesna watched it from the ring window, night after night, the way she had once watched Earth's dark and grieved. Now the dark was breaking. City by city, the network carried the beam farther than the beam itself could go — the gardens at Hearthside feeding the high platforms, the platforms feeding the inland grids, the grids waking the dead places one streetlight at a time.

She kept the Ledger of the Morning. It had become her work, transcribing the counterpoints, and it grew heavy with a new kind of record: not a report of slow dying, but a report of return. Where the old pages showed years of thinning — the Amen losing a note here, a rest there — the new pages showed the notes coming back, the rests filling, the return stream fattening like a river after rain.

Ren sent up maps. Vesna sang them to herself and saw them as music: a score of Earth, the dark places as rests, the lit places as notes, the whole planet becoming a slow, spreading melody. Aurelia, the golden city, was still dark — the far dead city, the last rest — but the lights around it were closing in like a cadence resolving toward the root.

The Order held a night watch for the city. The choir sang the verse of the lights, and the beam carried it to the edge of Aurelia's dark, and the dark did not answer. Not yet.

But the Ledger showed Earth answering again. That was the record, morning after morning, the Amen returning with new strength, and Vesna sang it back to the choir, and the choir sang it down, and the loop closed, and the light spread.

One evening Idra's voice came up the beam, tired and glad. "My grandmother saw the lights of her time," she said. "She told me they went out one by one. Now they come back one by one. It is the same music, Vesna. It was always the same music."

Vesna closed the Ledger and stood at the window. The map of lights spread beneath her, note by note, rest by rest, and she thought: we are singing the planet back into being.

The beam went out. Earth answered. One second later, as always, and it sounded like a promise.

Chapter 39: The Monastery's Purpose

The Order held its longest chapter since the founding, and the question before it was the oldest question the monastery had ever asked itself.

What are we for?

The Abbot opened it in the great chapel, with the earth curved beneath the glass floor and the Crown Array warm above. For two hundred years, he said, the answer had been one word: watch. We are the watchers. We sing the watch, and the watch is our whole vocation. The watch was over. What remained?

Vesna listened from the novices' bench, and she heard the debate rise and fall like a tide. Brother Otho argued for the letter: keep the watch, keep the Rule, keep what the founders made. The younger voices argued for the work: the bridge, the relays, the gardens, the light. Maribel argued for the song: the song was the purpose, and the purpose had simply been revealed, not changed.

Vesna rose when the voices rested. She had prepared nothing, and she said what she knew.

"When I was a child on Earth," she said, "I watched the lights from the gardens, and I thought the monastery was a lantern in the sky. I came up here to learn the songs, and I learned instead that we were a watchtower — that we looked down and reported and did not act. We were a lantern the whole time. We just did not know it."

The chapel was still. The Abbot looked at her a long moment, and she saw his fear had left him. What remained in his face was not certainty, but a kind of peace, the peace of a man who has finished waiting.

"A watchtower keeps the dark at a distance," he said. "A lamp goes into it. The founders built a lamp and called it a watch. We are not renouncing the founders' work. We are finally using it as it was made."

The vote went on the note of a held unison — the Order's way of agreeing. The purpose was declared in two words, written into the chapter book, sung by the whole assembly three times until it had the weight of the mode behind it: the bridge and the song.

The Abbot remained. He would keep the office, not as the keeper of the watch, but as the first singer of the bridge. Vesna saw him stand a little straighter under the word.

That night he sang matins alone in the hub chapel, and Vesna listened from the door, and his voice was old and thin and true, and she understood that a man can be renewed without being made young.

Chapter 40: The Blessing Redefined

The creed shifted in the morning, which was fitting, because the creed had always been sung, never written.

For two hundred years the Order had held one sentence over its head, the creed of the outward voice: Earth must always look up. It was the creed that had built the single point, the deliberate dependence, the fault. It was the creed that had made the watch a wall.

The Abbot had asked the chapter for a new creed, and the chapter had asked the choir, and the choir had asked the whole Order, and in the end the words came not from the oldest voice but from the newest.

Maribel had been given the task of the final words. Vesna stood beside her in the great chapel as the Order gathered, and Maribel's voice, severe and exact and tender underneath, spoke them into the silence.

"We are not the outward voice. We are the answering voice." She let the words hang. "The outward voice speaks and does not listen. The answering voice speaks to what has spoken. We have listened for two hundred years. Now we answer."

Vesna felt the sentence settle into her like a note finding its place in the mode. It was the same music, shifted a fifth.

"To sing what is true," Maribel said, "and to answer what calls."

The Order repeated it, a wave, line by line, the way a choir learns a new part. Vesna sang it with them, and the words went up the beam and down the beam, and Idra's voice came back from Earth, repeating them, and the rectennas hummed them, and the creed was no longer a wall.

It was a door.

The Abbot asked Maribel where the words had come from, and she looked at Vesna, and she said, "They were in the code all along. The first verse of the answer. We had only never sung it."

Vesna understood, then, the whole shape of it. The blessing had never been a one-way gift. It had been an antiphony from the first light of 2010, two voices, one second apart, each the other's sky. The Order had only misheard its own part.

She sang the new creed to herself on the way to the hub chapel — to sing what is true, and to answer what calls — and it had the cadence of the old song, and it had the freedom of a new one.

The beam went out at matins. Earth answered, one second later.

The blessing, redefined, was only now beginning.

Chapter 41: Vesna's Vow

The night before her vow, Vesna could not sleep. She walked the ring to the vault and stood at the ledger, her hand resting on two hundred years of mornings. She thought of the Rule of the Watch, and of what she was not going to say.

The Rule had promised silence. The old vow was a silence taken into the body, a door held shut. But the door had been open since the deep three. The door had sung.

In the morning the chapel was full. Junia's harp stood in the corner like a second listener. The Abbot waited at the rail in his old vestments, and beside him Maribel, keeper of the code, who had broken her lifelong rule to descend and had come home carrying a different rule in her hands.

Vesna knelt. The Abbot's voice, thin and kind, asked for her intention.

She had rehearsed a dozen answers. In the end she gave the true one. "I intend to sing," she said, "what is true, and to answer what calls."

There was a breath in the chapel, the whole congregation holding a rest. Then the Abbot laughed — a small, dry sound, almost a note — and said, "So be it. It is the oldest intention, and the newest."

The vow was not silence. It was song. It was the keeping of the beam and the gardens, the ledger and the work-songs, the deep three and the word ANSWER, which had waited two hundred years to be heard. Vesna took it as a descant takes its place above a plain melody — the same line, a third higher, and the whole thing suddenly larger.

Below, far down the gravity well, the Grounders witnessed by beam. Idra had asked for it — let the children see the vow — and so the rectenna gardens were tuned that day to carry not power but the sound of a girl becoming a keeper. Vesna sang her vow toward Earth, and the Amen came back one second late, as it always had, as it always would.

Afterward Maribel took her aside. She was old, and the descent had cost her, and there was a tenderness in her severity now. "The keeping," she said, "is not a thing you hold. It is a thing you hear. It is hearing the one note the whole choir has missed, and having the courage to sing it."

Vesna thought of the three dark notes, the deep three, below the root. She had heard them at twenty-two years old, in the descant, with no one else noticing. She had not known then what she was hearing. Now she knew.

"Come," said Maribel, and led her down to the vault, to the ledger, to the two hundred years of mornings. "Tomorrow we begin teaching you the keeping for real. And then, one day, it will be yours."

Vesna laid her hand on the first page. The light of the station's artificial dawn fell on the oldest note in the book, and she understood that her vow had been sung to it — that she was not taking an office, but returning a debt.

The song had been kept for her all her life. Now she would keep it for the ones coming down the well.

Chapter 42: The Choir Grows

The first Grounder novices came up the well in the fall, and Ren came among them.

Vesna watched the shuttle's arrival from the gallery of the hangar, and she did not recognize the pilot's ease in him at first. The man who had argued with her in the listening gardens, who had doubted the song and then helped rebuild the world with it, climbed out of the Perpetua with his hand on a hatch he had helped refit. He looked up at the ring of the monastery, and his face did the thing faces do when the sky becomes a floor.

"They are not novices," Otho said beside her, carefully. "They are groundlings, sent up to learn our songs."

"They are novices," said Vesna, "sent up to learn the songs we share."

The Rule of the Bridge had no line for this, so the bridge made its own. The Grounders were housed in the ring, given the same cells, the same hours. They learned the canticle line by line, and the monks learned the work-songs of the gardens, and the two repertoires began to braid.

Ren had a gift for the descant that surprised everyone, Vesna most of all. His voice was not trained, but his ear was exact — the ear of a man who had tuned rectennas by resonance for years, who had learned to hear the beam as a note before he ever learned to sing one. Junia said it first, at choir practice, when the young groundling matched her harp's interval to the half-tone: "He hears it whole. He was ready before he came."

Vesna thought of her own ascent, at ten years old, the last child ever to come up. She had come as an offering, a gift from the gardens. These came as equals, by road.

Down the well was not a myth anymore. It was a route with a schedule, a tide of light going down and voices coming up. The descent she had made in fear, through six minutes of blackout, was now a road children drew on the walls of the school at Hearthside — a winding line from a ring to a garden, marked in notes.

On the first Sunday after the Grounders arrived, the choir sang the full canticle with both worlds' voices in one room. Monks and novices and groundlings, fifty-three of them, and in the middle of the descant Ren's voice rose and held, and Vesna heard the mode the way the founders must have heard it — one song, carried up and down the sky, sung from both ends of the well.

She closed her eyes and saw it as light. Gold, and gold, and gold — and among the gold, small and bright as kindling, the new voices burning.

Chapter 43: The Grounder Envoy

Idra had waited sixty years to see the lights, and she came up the well carrying her age like a staff.

Vesna met her at the hatch, and there was a moment — the old woman in the great ring, the gravity of Earth still in her knees — like the first time a gardener sees the sun from above. Idra did not gasp. She stood at the viewport with her hands folded, looking at the blue world she had tended all her life, and she said, very quietly, "It is a garden. It always was."

The Abbot received her in the chapel of the Crown Array, and the two old voices began to talk the way the two worlds had been talking for two centuries — in song and answer, one second apart. Not because the machinery required it. Because it was the only way they had ever truly understood each other.

"Two hundred years," said Idra, "we kept your gardens green on Earth. We sang your canticle back to you before we knew what it was. We did not know we were answering. We only knew the sky had a voice, and the voice was kind, and so we sang."

"We did not know we were being answered," said the Abbot. "We thought we sang into a void. We were wrong. The void was singing back."

Vesna stood at Maribel's side and watched the two keepers of the two worlds meet. It was the antiphony made flesh: the monastery and the colony, each the other's sky. She thought of her mother, Ila, who had chosen to stay below, and of how the beam now carried her mother's voice to her as part of the morning. The distance had not closed. The distance was the point. One second apart, two voices, one song.

The talks began that week, in the chapel, in canticle-and-answer. Idra brought the treaty's seeds in her head — what the colonies needed, what they would give, what no single point of failure would ever be allowed to hold again. The Abbot brought the Order's whole history, the fault and the forgiveness. And between them, like a descant above a plain melody, Vesna sang what she had learned on the coast: that the song was always work, and the work was always prayer, if sung true.

"Sing it to us," Idra said once, near the end of a long day. "Sing the part where the lights come on."

So Vesna sang. She sang the verse of the descent, and the lamps in the chapel dimmed with the prayer, and when she came to the verse of the lights, the Abbot joined her, and Junia's harp joined them, and then the whole choir, and Idra sat with her eyes closed, hearing her grandmother's city come awake one more time, and she wept without shame, because it was true.

Chapter 44: The Treaty of the Lagrange

It took three weeks, and it was signed in a single note.

The Treaty of the Lagrange was written by engineers and sung by keepers. Ren drafted the articles in the vault, hunched over the ledger like a monk over a psalm, and Maribel set them to the code — because, she said, a treaty that could not be sung could not be kept. Every clause became a counterpoint. Every promise became a held note. The document that bound two worlds to each other was a song first, and a law afterward, and the law meant what the song meant.

The beam was shared. Power, data, music — the Crown Array would serve them all, its tens of megawatts apportioned by need and by season, and the gardens would feed it back. The choir was joint: Grounders and monks, one body, singing the same descant from both ends of the sky. The monastery was enshrined as a religious house whose utility was written into its charter — a house of prayer, and a trunk line, and neither of those things diminished the other.

And Earth's independence was made law. The founders' fault — the single point of failure, the creed of the outward voice, the dependence they had built and called providence — was named in the treaty and refused. No beam, no relay, no garden would ever again be the only thread. The network would be a net. If one strand broke, the others would sing it home.

The Abbot signed for the Order. Idra signed for the colonies, her hand held steady by her grandson's. Ren signed for the engineers, and then Maribel signed for the code, and then — because it was the only seal that had ever meant anything at St. Elara — the whole assembly sang.

They sang the treaty as a canticle, up and down the beam, and Earth answered. And at the last, when the formalities were done and the copies sealed, someone — Vesna never knew who — sounded a single note, the root of the Elaran mode, and held it. Held it past the breath that should have ended it. And the note went up the beam, and came down again from Earth, one second later, still ringing.

Two worlds, one note, held in unison across three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometers. It was the longest interval Vesna had ever heard, and the truest — a rest that was not silence but attention, both sides of the sky listening to the same tone.

Afterward Idra took her hand. "You sang the treaty," the old woman said. "It was already law before it was law. It is sung into the Ledger now. It will be kept because it can be sung."

Vesna looked at the new page in the ledger, the treaty in counterpoint, the note of the seal still warm in the air.

"The song," she said, "was always the keeping."

Chapter 45: The Silent Star

There was one dark place left.

The maps of light had spread city by city, but on the far side of the sea there was a region the relay could not reach — a dead city, unreachable, its name struck from the old ledgers. Vesna watched it from the night chapel, in the zero-g of the hub, where the Earth hung blue and bright except for one small darkness, like a rest in a line of melody.

She had come there to sing the night watch alone. It was her turn, and she had chosen the dark spot on purpose, the way a singer chooses the hardest interval.

The old code had a name for Earth, she remembered — a name from the founders' book. They had called it the silent star. In all the two hundred years of the Ledger, in all the status reports sung down the well, that had been the founders' quiet word for the world below: silent, star, a thing that gave light but did not answer.

It had never been true, she thought. Earth had been answering all along, in the Amen, in the work-songs, in the keeping of the gardens. But the name had been true enough for the founders' fear, and the fear had built the fault.

At chapter, the next morning, Vesna spoke. She held up the old page, the two words in the founders' hand.

"This name is no longer ours," she said. "We have heard Earth answer. We have held a note with it across the sky. The silent star is retired. From this morning, we sing it no more."

Otho looked at her a long moment, and then bowed his head. It was, she thought, the first time he had ever bowed to her.

That night she went again to the dark spot. She sang the canticle alone, and when she came to the verse of the silent star — the verse of the place still dark, still unreachable — she sang it as the others sang it, with love and not with fear. The beam went down. The Amen did not come.

No answer yet.

But the meaning had changed. She understood now what the founders had never let themselves sing: that a song sung to what has not yet answered is not a wasted song. It is a held note. It is a rest that is waiting, both sides of the sky listening.

She sang, and the dark place below did not answer, and she kept singing anyway, because that was the canticle now.

To sing what is true, and to answer what calls. And where nothing calls yet, to sing anyway — to be the first voice, and to keep the listening open.

Chapter 46: The Anniversary

Two hundred years to the morning, the Order sang its anniversary.

The chapel was full, and the ledger lay open on the altar to its first and its newest pages, the oldest note and the newest verse side by side. Vesna looked at the first page — the founders' first matins, the station new, the beam first lit — and at the last, the treaty in counterpoint, the verse of the silent star, the voices of both worlds. She had never seen the book closed in the same breath with itself like that. Two centuries of mornings, one line.

The Abbot spoke before the canticle, and he spoke of the founders with grief and with forgiveness.

"They built a house in the sky," he said, "and they built a fault into its foundations. They meant to make Earth always look up. They made it always depend. They called their dependence providence, and they were wrong, and their wrongness cost two hundred years of silence we did not know we were keeping."

The chapel held its rest.

"But they also built the song," he said. "They built the beam, and the gardens, and the Ledger. They built the code that carried Earth's warning down to us until we could hear it. They did not know they were prophets. They were afraid, and they built, and the fear became a fault and the building became a bridge. We forgive them because we are their heirs. We inherit the fault, and we inherit the song, and we have chosen, at last, which one to sing."

Then they sang.

The whole monastery sang, and the colonies sang with them by beam — every garden, every city of the light, every voice that had learned the descant in the years of the bridge. It was the longest antiphony in history, Vesna knew: two hundred years of mornings on one side, and on the other, the whole answering Earth. The beam went out with the first line, and came back one second later with the Amen, and it went on and on, verse and answer, verse and answer, the Ledger's first page and its newest page singing to each other across the sky.

Junia's harp walked through the descant. Ren's voice held the half-tone. The Abbot's old voice carried the plain melody, and Maribel, beside Vesna, sang the counterpoints she had kept alone for so long, and her voice did not waver once.

Vesna sang the verse of the silent star, and the dark place on the far side of the sea held its silence, and she sang it true anyway, as the canticle required — to sing what is true, and to answer what calls, and to keep the listening open for the voice that had not yet come.

The antiphony went on. The longest one. The truest.

Chapter 47: The Hymn of Repair

The Hymn of Repair was finished in the spring, and its first full performance was sung from both worlds at once.

Maribel had written the last verse herself, in her own hand, in the vault, and Vesna had watched her do it — the old keeper's pen moving over the page like a voice over an interval, setting the words that had been waiting for two hundred years. The hymn was the ancient canticle, whole, with the three new verses set into it like new windows in an old wall: the verse of the descent, the verse of the lights, the verse of the silent star.

"The hymn was always there," Maribel said, when it was done. "We were only singing the first movement. The rest had been waiting for an answer."

They sang it on the morning of the equinox, the monastery and the colonies together, the beam carrying the whole hymn down the well and the gardens carrying it back. Vesna sang the verse of the descent, and in her memory she fell again — through the storm, through the six minutes of blackout, down into the listening gardens and the hands that caught her. She sang the verse of the lights, and saw Aurelia wake, tower by tower. She sang the verse of the silent star, and the dark place on the far side of the sea did not answer, and she sang it anyway, as the hymn required.

The hymn required it. That was the miracle, she thought. The song had made a place for the waiting.

After the performance, in the vault, Maribel closed the ledger and turned to face her.

"You have heard the code whole," the old keeper said, "since the morning of the deep three. You have carried it down the well and up again. You have sung the treaty and held the night watch and forgiven the founders in the same breath as their song. I have kept this alone for fifty years, and I kept it wrong — I kept it shut. You have taught me that the keeping is a hearing."

She held out her hand, and in it was the key to the vault, the small plain key that had hung at her belt since before Vesna was born.

"The keeping is yours," Maribel said. "The code, the ledger, the mornings. Sing them true."

Vesna took the key. It was warm from the old woman's hand, and heavy, and it weighed nothing at all. She knelt, and Maribel laid her hand on her head, and the two keepers stood a moment in the vault, in the light of the artificial dawn, the two hundred years of mornings around them like a congregation.

"It will not be kept shut," said Vesna.

"No," said Maribel. "It never will be again."

And she smiled — the severe choir mistress, the keeper who had broken her lifelong rule to follow the code where it led — and the smile was a descant, a whole song above the plain melody, and Vesna understood that the keeping had been passed not in the key but in that smile, and that she was to carry it the same way: open, and sung.

Chapter 48: The Two Worlds

In the third year of the bridge, the two worlds became one community of voices, each the other's sky.

Vesna woke to matins, as she had every morning of her life, and the beam went out with the first line — now the light is gathered; we send it down — and the Amen came back one second later, and she heard, in the lag, the whole shape of the arrangement: two worlds keeping time for each other. Below, the gardens fed the beam. Above, the beam fed the gardens. Earth looked up and saw the lamp; the monastery looked down and saw a garden. It was the antiphony, she thought. It had always been the antiphony. They had only been slow to take their part.

The day went the way the days went now. She walked the ring with Ren, checking the relay's morning cycle, and they talked of the net — the mesh of light that no single point could ever break — in the same cadence they used for the canticle, because Ren had learned to think in counterpoints and could not stop. She sat with Ila by beam, her mother's voice coming up the well with the gardens' report, and the two of them sang the work-song of the rectenna rows together, one second apart, mother and daughter, the oldest duet in the world.

In the afternoon she taught at the school in the ring — the school where Grounder children and novice children sat side by side, learning the same descant, the same intervals, the same names for the notes. The founders had never imagined it. The founders had imagined one voice, outward, alone. Here were fifty small voices, from both sides of the sky, learning to sing a third above each other.

And in the far dark, in the evening, it happened.

She was in the night chapel, alone, singing the verse of the silent star to the unreachable place — she had sung it every night for a year, alone, to what had not answered — and the beam went down, and the lag passed, and then, where the silence always was, there was light.

A note. Small. Faint, the way a far city's lamp is faint, and absolutely unmistakable. The Amen, from the dead city, from the far side of the sea, rising to meet the song that had been sung to it for a year.

Vesna stood in the zero-g of the hub with her hand at her throat, the note still in the air, the light still in her eyes. She had sung to what had not answered, and it had answered at last.

She did not wake the monastery. She sang the note back, once, and held it, and let the two worlds hold it together, one second apart, in the dark, in the light, the whole long arrangement come round at last.

Chapter 49: The Orbit

A year after the first light in the far dark, the orbit was a home.

Vesna kept the stationkeeping now, in the little room above the hangar, where the numbers of the Lagrange point moved like a tide she had learned to sing. L5 was a stable place, but stability was work — the station held its Trojan point by small corrections, small burns, the way a singer holds a note by constant, invisible attention. She thought of it that way because it was true: the monastery did not sit in the sky. It sang in the sky, and the singing was the keeping.

The seasons of the orbit came and went. The sunward face grew warm and the dark face grew cold, and the hydroponic wheat in the ring obeyed neither, green in the artificial light that had been tuned, two hundred years ago, to the exact color of forgiveness. Vesna had learned to read the year in the wheel of the station's shadow, in the way Earth waxed and waned across the viewport like a great blue note, swelling and resting.

The shuttle ran on a schedule now. Twice a season the Perpetua went down the well and came back up, carrying groundlings and monks, seeds and songs, the ledger's new pages and the gardens' new charts. The descent route was a road with regular traffic, and the six minutes of blackout were still blackout, and still a kind of prayer — but now the prayer was shared, the way home a thing with a schedule, and the children who rode it sang descant through the silence to keep each other brave.

And the Perpetua herself — the two-hundred-year-old tug, the tithe of patience, the ship maintained for centuries for a voyage no rule allowed — made her final flight on a mercy run.

A garden failed, far down the coast, and the relay had no thread to it, and the Perpetua went anyway, with Ren at the throttles and Caleb beside him, carrying a line of cable and a choir's worth of hope. It came back two weeks later, battered and singing, and when it was berthed, Caleb stood on the hangar deck and laid his hand on its heat shield and said, quietly, "She has done the thing she was kept for."

They retired her then, with ceremony, the way a hymn is retired — not forgotten, sung to rest. And in the yard beside her, scaffolding rising, the frame of a new ship took shape: the Answer, they had named it already, built for the route the Perpetua had opened, built without a single point of failure.

Vesna stood with Caleb and watched the welders' lights spark in the dark of the yard.

"The orbit," he said, "is the oldest house we have. And now it has a road."

"Not a road," she said. "An antiphony. Two houses, singing to each other across the sky."

He looked at her a long moment, and then smiled, the way Maribel had smiled, the whole song in it.

"Same thing," he said. "When it is sung true."

Chapter 50: The Canticle Continues

It was an ordinary morning, which was the whole point.

Vesna rose before the artificial dawn and walked the ring to the chapel while the station's lights came up slow and gold. She was choir mistress now. The title still surprised her, the way a new note surprises the singer who finds it in her own voice — there all along, only now named.

The chapel filled. Monks and novices, Grounders and children, Ren in the descant with his exact rectenna ear, Junia at her harp, Ila's voice coming up the beam from the gardens below, one second apart. The Abbot sat in his old chair, too old now to stand through matins, and he was not angry and not afraid, and that was the truest ornament the chapel had ever worn.

She raised her hand. The choir rose with her, and the first line went out, the oldest line, the line that had gone down the well every morning for two hundred years.

"Now the light is gathered; we send it down."

And the beam carried it — tens of megawatts, the Crown Array's two-hundred-year-old voice — down the gravity well, past the storm layer, into the listening gardens, through the net, to the cities and the coast and the far dark where a dead city had answered at last. And one second later, the Amen came back.

Vesna heard it whole, as she had always heard everything whole. The descant above the plain melody, the counterpoints in the rests, the deep three — one continuous line, the way you hear the whole of a river when you stand in it. Two hundred years of mornings, and every one of them still singing.

She thought of the girl she had been, stopping in the descant because three notes had been sung that had never been sung before. She had not known then that she was hearing the beginning. The deep three were not a break in the song. They were its turning — the place where the plain melody opened into descant, where two hundred years of listening became a year of answering.

The changed note was the beginning. She saw that now, as plainly as the gold of the mode's root and the dark of the deep three.

The canticle rose and fell, verse and answer, and at the last, when the final Amen had been sung, Vesna stood a moment in the silence — the rich, inhabited silence that was not an absence but a held rest — and she heard what every keeper hears at the end of the morning.

The song is not finished.

It is not finished, and it will never be finished. It is only, always, being sung. Two worlds, one beam, one second apart, the longest antiphony in history, and it goes on — from the first page of the Ledger to the newest, from the deep three to the first light in the far dark, from the vow that is not silence to the treaty that is a note.

Vesna closed her eyes and sang the first line again, alone, into the gathered light of the morning, because the continuing is the prayer, and the prayer is the keeping, and the keeping is a song.

It is only, always, being sung.
