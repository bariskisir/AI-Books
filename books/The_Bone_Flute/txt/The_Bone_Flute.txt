The Bone Flute
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Bone Flute

The rain had followed Halden north for three days, and the widow's house smelled of salt and candles and the thing on the table. The drowned boy was sixteen, or had been; the sea had made a stranger of him. His father stood by the door with his cap in his hands and asked the question every village asks the bone man, in the same careful voice.

"My boy. He never got his last word."

Halden looked at the wrapped shape and did what he always did. He took the flute from his sleeve. It was the forearm bone of his sister Maren, pale as winter, and when he raised it the room went quiet the way a held note goes quiet. He did not ask for payment first. The trade taught him that the dead keep their own accounts.

He breathed into the bone. The first note was not a note at all but a breath that had been waiting sixteen years.

The song came then, and the room dissolved into it. He saw the rope coiled on the deck. He saw the wave rise like a wall of glass, green and patient, and he saw the lie the boy had told his father that morning — that he could swim, that the rope was only for the net, that the sea was not hungry. The melody did not accuse. It simply showed the truth the way a window shows the weather. The boy's last moment was not the water but the lie, and the lie was made of love, which made it worse.

When the song ended, the boy was silent forever. That was the rule, the cost, the mercy. The father had dropped to his knees, and the mother was crying into her apron, and the boy's sister looked at Halden as though he had pulled her brother up out of the sea long enough to say goodbye.

The father pressed a loaf of bread into his hands, warm, and then, shyly, kissed the bone. Halden felt the kiss travel up his arm like a small hot ember, and for a moment he was not the bone man of the rhyme but a boy again, twelve years old, breathing his first note into his dead sister's arm.

He wrapped the flute and stepped out into the rain. The town was smaller than his loneliness. Then he saw the horse.

A white horse, held by a man in a herald's livery, standing in the rain without hurry. The man's eyes found him across the square, and Halden felt the cold of the night before a playing settle between his ribs.

"Bone flutist," the herald said, and it was not a question. "King Renn requires you in the capital. You leave at dawn."

Halden looked down at the loaf in his hand, still warm, and at the flute in his sleeve, still carrying the kiss. Somewhere behind him a child's voice, muffled by the rain, was singing the rhyme about the bone man who steals the last words of the dead.

He did not say yes. He did not say no. He simply stopped being able to feel the warmth of the loaf, and that was answer enough.

Chapter 2: The Trade

Halden's father had been a carpenter, and carpenters understand that a thing can be made whole out of what was broken. When Maren died of the fever, when Halden was twelve and she fourteen and the winter the worst the salt town had seen, his father did not weep at the grave. He went into his workshop for three days, and when he came out he was carrying the flute.

He had cut the forearm bone of his daughter, hollowed it, drilled it, smoothed it until it shone like old ivory. He put it in Halden's hands. "She never got to finish. Now she can."

Halden had been afraid of the bone. But it was warm from his father's hands, and it was Maren, and Maren had never once made him afraid. He raised it to his lips the way a boy raises a reed whistle and breathed — one breath, the first real breath he had taken since she died — and the bone answered.

The note came out soft and wrong, and then the bone did something a bone should not be able to do. It shaped his breath into a word. Not a melody, but his name — Hal-den — sung in Maren's voice, the way she had called him across the yard at dusk.

He dropped the flute. His father caught it and gave it back. "The dead speak once, and they cannot lie. That is the whole trade, and you will spend your life deciding whether it is a gift or a punishment. It is both."

The town children had a rhyme, a skipping song: Bone man, bone man, hold your breath, steal the words of the dead, and give them back in a song, and if you don't, they'll sing along. He had never understood the last line. He understood it now, standing in the rain while a herald's horse blew steam into the cold, waiting for his answer.

The rest of the trade he learned slowly, by breath, by error. One playing per dead, ever. The dead cannot lie; the music is confession. A playing costs him a day's strength, a scrap of memory — the name of a street, the tune of a lullaby — and he gave them gladly. The dead had given him their last words; he could give them his days.

The herald cleared his throat. "The King requires you in the capital. It is not a request, flutist."

"Why does a king require a bone man?"

"Because," the herald said, and his voice lost its official edge, "a prince has died, and the king wants to hear what he left unsaid."

The rain came down. Halden thought of the drowned boy, of the lie made of love, of the wave that had come for him green and patient. He thought of Maren, of his name sung across a dusk yard, and he understood that the trade had been waiting for this summons his whole life.

"Then we go at dawn," he said. "But I ride behind no man's horse, and I play no one until I have seen the dead myself. That is the trade."

The herald bowed. It was a small bow, the kind given to a man who might be a knife.

Chapter 3: The Ossuary

The capital of Valdris did not greet Halden with walls or crowds. It greeted him with the Ossuary, which stood in the center of the city the way a heart stands in the center of a chest.

The hall was enormous, and cold, and it smelled of bone the way a forest smells of rain — not the death of it, but the keeping of it. Shelves rose from floor to shadow, and on every shelf the honored dead were laid, each bone a sealed door, each door holding a person who had finished speaking. Between the shelves moved the keepers, the two silent brothers, gray as the bone they tended, who had not spoken in forty years and prepared the dead for playings the way a smith prepares iron.

They did not ask Halden's name. They led him past the great shelves and stopped before a shelf low near the floor, where a countess lay whose name the city had already forgotten. She had died, the brothers' hands told him, of a quiet complaint, and no one had asked to hear her, and so she had waited fifty years in her box of bone for the last word no one wanted.

Halden played her because she had waited. He breathed into the flute, and the countess's song came out small and bright: a garden, a stone bench, a quarrel with a gardener over a rose trellis that had grown wild, the gardener's face — kind, stubborn, beloved — and the apology she had never made because she died before the roses did. Small truths, the song said, are as holy as great ones. A garden is a kingdom with a fence.

When the song ended, the keepers watched him the way men watch a storm they have agreed not to fear. One took a slate from his robe and wrote, in letters careful as stitches:

The court does not summon flutists for small truths.

Halden looked at the words, then up at the shelves, at the sealed doors, at the city of the silent waiting for a breath. "Then what does the court summon flutists for?"

The keeper wrote again: For the truths that are too large to be carried. For the names that have been waiting.

Somewhere above them, on the highest shelf, a prince's bones were being prepared. Halden did not know it yet. He only knew that the Ossuary was not a place of the dead — it was a place of the unfinished, and he had walked into it carrying a flute and a breath, and the keepers looked at him as though he were a key.

Something was beginning, and he felt it the way a musician feels a change of key — in the spine, before the ear. He had not yet met the king. He had not yet seen the prince. But the Ossuary had told him, in its silent keeper's way, that the small truths were done, and the large ones had begun.

Chapter 4: The King's Summons

The throne room of Valdris was smaller than Halden had expected, and colder, and the man on the throne was grayer than either. King Renn had been handsome once, but grief had eaten the youth from his face the way salt eats the paint from a hull. His crown sat on his head like a thing he no longer remembered how to take off.

The courtiers stood in their ranks like notes on a staff. A chair stood empty near the throne — smaller than the king's, carved with a prince's crest — and Halden noticed that the king did not look at it, and also could not stop looking at it.

"Bone flutist," the king said. His voice was a held note, thin, almost gone. "Do you know why I have summoned you?"

"To play the dead, Your Majesty."

"To play my son." He said it plainly, the way a man says a thing he has said to himself a thousand times. "Prince Aldric. Murdered a week ago at the border pass. The one playing — there is only one, is there not? — belongs to him."

Halden felt the weight of it settle. "One playing, ever. After it, the dead person is silent forever."

"Yes." The king leaned forward, and for a moment the hollow man was something else — a father, desperate, holding a door shut against the wind. "Then he must speak once. He must tell the kingdom what happened in that pass. I want the truth, flutist, the whole song, the way he lived it and the way he died it, before the memorial."

The king's hand trembled on the arm of the throne. He was not afraid of the dead, Halden understood; he was afraid of what the dead might say.

"The fee," Halden said, because the trade required it.

The king blinked. "Gold. A place at court, if you want it. Whatever the trade asks." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes, older than the grief. "Or gratitude. The gratitude of a king is not a small thing, flutist. It is the one currency that never runs out."

Gratitude, Halden thought. But he did not say it. He was thinking of another face, of a festival three years gone, of a prince who had sat with the feared bone man when no one else would, who had asked about Maren not with fear but with kindness, and had said the dead should be allowed their last word.

Prince Aldric had been the only person in Valdris who had ever treated the trade as a mercy.

Halden bowed. "I will play him, Your Majesty. Not for gold, and not for gratitude. For that."

The king did not ask what "that" meant. Perhaps the answer was written in the empty chair, in the trembling hand.

Outside, the first snow of the year had begun to fall, soft and slow, withholding what it knew. Halden touched the flute in his sleeve and felt, or imagined, Maren's bone answering him — a question forming, a name gathering itself, the way a held note gathers the silence that will follow it.

He was twenty-nine years old, and he was about to become the most dangerous honest man in Valdris.

Chapter 5: The Prince

Three years before the summons, the harvest festival had come to the capital, and Halden had gone to it because a bone man cannot refuse the living forever.

He stood at the edge of the fair with a cup of mulled wine, watching the living pretend the dead did not exist, when the crowd parted like a held breath and the prince walked up to him. He knew the face from the coins. Aldric was young then, bright with the ease of a man who has never needed to be careful, and he walked straight through the cold ring of space that always surrounded the bone man.

"You play the dead," Aldric said. It was not a question, and not a taunt.

Halden braced for the fear, the quick step backward, the sign against the evil eye.

Instead the prince sat down on the bench beside him, close enough to smell the woodsmoke, and asked: "Who is your flute?"

"Maren," Halden said, and his voice cracked on the name. "My sister. She died of the fever when I was twelve."

"The fever." Aldric nodded, and there was no pity in it, only attention, the attention of a man giving a whole song to one note. "And you carry her so she can finish speaking."

"Yes."

"Do you ever wonder," the prince said, "whether the dead want to be avenged?"

Halden looked at the bone that had been Maren's arm. "No. I wonder whether they want to be told. I think they need the living to tell the truth, and that is all. The rest is ours."

Aldric smiled then, a small and serious smile. "Then the dead should be allowed their last word. Every time. Even the ones the living would rather not hear. Especially those."

Halden had loved him for it. It was not a large thing, a prince sitting with a bone man at a festival — but in a kingdom where the living crossed the street to avoid him, it was a whole sea of a thing, and he carried it for three years like a coin in his boot.

The official account of the murder reached him in the capital, and it read like a censored song. Prince Aldric had been sent north to quell the quiet unrest along the border. His party had been set upon in the pass by brigands — common thieves, nameless, faceless — in a snowstorm. The body had been found with no marks of violence, which the report called a mercy and the court called, in whispers, a mystery. The brigands had not been caught. They never were.

The court's rumors were louder than the report. The prince had been loved; the prince had been the future, and someone — the whispers made a shape of it, a shadow with shoulders — had wanted the future closed.

Halden read the reports and heard, underneath them, the bass line his trade had taught him to hear: the song of the dead is the truth, and the song of the living is whatever they can make you believe.

The memorial was in eight days. He had come to play a prince he had loved, and the truth, he was beginning to understand, was coming to him.

Chapter 6: The Murder

The night before the first playing, Princess Lira came to Halden in the antechamber the keepers had given him, the way her brother had come to him at the festival — quietly, without fear, as though the feared bone man were the only honest thing in the palace.

She was younger than Aldric had been, and sharper, with her father's gray eyes and none of his hollow grief. She set a lamp on the table between them. "I want you to know what they are not telling you, before you play my brother's bones."

Halden listened. Aldric had been sent to the border pass to quiet unrest that had been quiet for years — quiet enough, Lira said, that a man with eyes could see the posting was not about the north. His guard had been changed at the last moment, the loyal men replaced with riders from another company, men who came from the south, went back south, and were not found afterward. The snow had come early, a storm that closed the pass, and the party had been caught in it as sheep caught in a fold.

The body had no marks of violence. "As though the snow itself had done it," Lira said. "As though snow were the only witness, and snow does not testify."

Then she told him about the letter. Aldric wrote every week — small letters, full of gossip and weather. The last one had been different: the north was not the problem; an uncle's shadow was lengthening over the border; he was afraid in a way he could not put into a letter; and if anything happened to him, Lira should remember that the pass had been his own choosing only in the telling.

"The letter was stolen from his rooms the day after his death. The lock was not broken. Someone with a key took it. I am telling you this because you are about to play my brother, and I need you to know that the name is coming. The song will say it. I am only afraid of what you will do when it does."

"I think my father sent Aldric north, and that my uncle wanted him dead, and that both can be true at once," Lira said, "and that the court has spent a week pretending only one of them can be."

The name. He felt it forming in the flute, in the breath, in the bone that had once been his sister's arm. Somewhere in the prince's last moments there was a face, a shadow, a signed order, a name.

At the door she paused. "He spoke of you after the festival. He said the bone man was the kindest man in Valdris, and that the living had it backwards — it is not the dead who should fear you, but the lies."

She left. The lamp burned. Halden sat alone in the cold, the flute in his hands, and heard, very faintly, the sound the bone made when he breathed — not a note yet, but a door beginning to open.

The snow fell beyond the window, withholding everything.

Chapter 7: The First Playing

The Ossuary at night was the inside of the earth, where the candles burned without draft and the dead lay in their bones like seeds in frozen ground.

The keepers had prepared Prince Aldric: the bones laid out on the white slab, the keepers waiting at the doors like two silent sentences. Halden had asked to play alone, before the king's men came; now he was alone with the prince who had once asked who his flute was.

He raised the bone. He breathed.

The song began in the pass — the cold of it before the wind, the high border cold where snow is not weather but geography. He felt the riders moving through it, and the change of the guard under the song like a change of key, the loyal men gone, the fold made.

Then the snow came, and the hoofbeats.

Under the hoofbeats, other sounds — men arriving, men departing, men who had come a long way to be at this pass on this night. And under those, like a bass line in the lowest register, a command. A voice, not heard so much as felt, giving an order in the snow.

The melody rose. Halden saw the face.

It was a shadow, but shadows have shoulders, and these were the shoulders of Duke Moris — the king's brother, the beloved general with the grievance worn for thirty years. The killing happened in a single breath, no marks of violence, because the prince had not been stabbed or struck — he had been walked into the storm, left to the winter, the pass closing over him like a grave that could not be opened.

And beneath all of it, the bass line sounded again, and the music leaned toward a name.

Halden felt it coming — the word forming in the flute the way water forms in the dark before a spring — and he was shaking. The song had almost said it; the two names that would break the kingdom.

He stopped.

He brought the flute down and stood shaking, the song unfinished around him like a held breath snatched back. In the doorway stood three of the king's men, and their captain looked at him with a face that had heard only wind.

"The playing, flutist. Is it done?"

Halden looked at the bones of the prince he had loved. The song was not done. The name was not said. But the flute had shown him the shape of it — the shadow with shoulders, the bass line of a command, the two names braided together — and he knew, with the cold certainty of a man who has just seen the truth and let it go, that it would be said. Not tonight. But before the prince was buried, he would tell the whole of it.

"Not yet," Halden said. "The dead are patient. I am not ready."

But the king's men were gone, and the Ossuary was silent, and the bone in his hands was warm with the song it had almost sung — and Halden lay awake that night hearing it, the unfinished melody, the name gathering itself like snow, waiting for the dawn.

Chapter 8: The Name

At dawn Halden came back to the Ossuary alone, and the keepers let him pass without a word, as though they knew a song interrupted must be finished.

The bones lay as he had left them, in the shape of a man, the skull facing the door. Halden stood before the slab and did not raise the flute at once. He stood in the cold and let himself be afraid, because the trade had taught him that fear is a note, and a note that is not played becomes a lie.

He raised the bone. He breathed.

The pass came back — the snow, the fold, the changed guard, the shadow with shoulders. This time he let the melody run. He followed it the way a man follows a river, on through the killing and the closing of the snow, and then it turned.

It named the killer. Duke Moris — the king's brother, the shadow with shoulders — the music said his name the way a bell says the hour. Halden felt it land in the air, a stone dropped into still water.

And then the flute turned again, and played a phrase that could not be.

It was Moris's name again, and under it, braided beneath it, another name. The name of the author. The man who had sent his son to a place he knew his brother hunted. The hand that had signed the posting, changed the guard, bought the peace with a son's life.

The name of King Renn.

Halden's breath stopped. The flute kept singing — the dead could not lie, the music was confession, the prince's last song was the whole truth, the two murderers, one of blood and one of will — and Halden stood in the middle of it like a man in a falling house, unable to stop hearing what he had feared since the herald found him in the rain.

The dead cannot lie. The dead cannot lie. The dead cannot lie.

The song ended. The Ossuary was silent. Halden lowered the flute and stood shaking, the name hanging in the cold air like smoke, the whole weight of Valdris — its throne, its crown, its king — resting on the shoulders of a bone man who had only wanted to give the dead their last words.

He burned to tell. He burned to run. He imagined the song said aloud before the court. He imagined running north, beyond the pass, letting someone else carry it.

The door of the Ossuary opened.

A chamberlain in the king's livery stood in the gray light, his face as blank as a censored score. "Bone flutist. The king wishes to hear the playing. Privately. Now."

Halden looked down at the flute in his hands — Maren's bone, warm with the truth it had just spoken — and understood that he had stopped being a player of the dead, and become something else.

The something else had a name, and the name was his to carry or to bury.

He put the flute to his lips, tested one note against the silence — the real note, the one that would name a king — and followed the chamberlain out of the Ossuary, into the snow, into the audience that would decide everything.

Chapter 9: The Shadow

The king received Halden in a private chamber with a single window, a fire, a desk, and the prince's empty chair — kept where the king could see it and not see it at once.

King Renn stood by the fire with his back to the door. He spoke to the flames. "Play it. The whole song. I must know who killed my son."

Halden had prepared a thousand words — a warning, a way out, a lie that would save them both. But the trade had taught him there is only ever one honest note in the room, and the king had asked for it.

He raised the flute. He breathed.

The pass came, the snow, the fold, the changed guard, the shadow with shoulders — Duke Moris, the name ringing like an hour — and then the bass line, braided, impossible: the author, the king himself, the hand that signed the posting, the man who sent his son to the place he knew his brother hunted.

Halden played the whole of it. He did not spare the king one note.

When the song ended, the fire had burned to embers and the king had not moved. His hands were on the mantel, shaking the way a held note shakes just before it breaks.

Then the king turned, and Halden saw his face.

It was not the face of a man hearing news. It was the face of a man hearing a confession he had been carrying for a week — grief that split him open, but under it, colder, fear. The king had known. The posting was the killing. He had signed the order knowing what his brother was, and the flute had just told the court's deepest secret to the only man who could bury it — or burn it.

The king said nothing. He dismissed Halden with a gesture so small it was almost nothing — a hand, lifted, let fall, as though he could no longer hold the world up.

Halden did not move. "Your Majesty —"

"Go." The word was quiet, and it was not a request. "You have given me my son's last words."

The audience was over. Halden found himself in the corridor, the door closed behind him, and the truth settling in him like cold.

The king had known. The king had signed. He had been haunted since — that was what the flute had caught, the grief of a man who bought peace with a son's life and found it cost more than the war. And now the king knew that Halden knew, and the kingdom's most dangerous secret had become Halden's, and a secret is a weight that only grows the longer it is carried.

He walked the snow-lit corridors and felt the court around him like a cage of omissions — everyone playing a censored song, everyone carrying a name they would not sing. He was the only one who could no longer be silent, and he had just told the deepest secret in Valdris to the man who might decide a bone man who knew too much was a truth the realm could not afford.

The shadow at the king's back had a name. And the shadow was not only the king's brother.

Chapter 10: The Duke

That evening a summons came to Halden's room, carried by a man in the duke's livery — an invitation to the townhouse of Duke Moris, flawlessly courteous.

Halden went because a bone man does not refuse a duke, and to see the shadow with shoulders in the light.

The townhouse was warm — its first lie — windows shut, fires lit, sealed against the cold like a held breath. Moris received him in a study lined with maps and swords, broad and charming, his smile arriving first.

"Flutist. You played my nephew. The court is still weeping — the good kind, the kind that ends with the telling." He poured wine. "I wanted to thank you. And to ask a favor."

Halden took the cup but did not drink. "Your Grace."

"The playing. I missed it — I was at the border, settling the unrest the prince was sent to quiet, a task I inherited." The lie was smooth, a polished floor. "I would like to hear it again. For the record. The brigands, the storm, the simple accident of it. I will pay you a fortune, flutist. Enough to buy a small kingdom of your own, or a large silence."

Halden looked at him across the study. The charm was real, the way a blade is real. The grievance was real too — thirty years of it, a man passed over at the succession, a crown gone to an elder brother. He understood Moris completely, and the understanding made him afraid — the worst lies are almost true.

"Your Grace," Halden said, "the dead speak once. The prince has spoken. He will not speak again."

"Then you are the only one who heard the whole song." Moris set his cup down, but something beneath the charm went still. "That makes you either very valuable or very inconvenient. I am offering you the first of those. I would advise against making me choose the second."

Halden stood. The flute was in his sleeve, and Maren's bone was cold, the weight of the name he carried pressing against his ribs like a second breath.

"The prince's song," Halden said, "was not about brigands. You know that. I know that. And the dead cannot lie, Your Grace — which means the only lies left in this room are spoken by the living."

He left the townhouse with the fortune unclaimed and the certainty that he had just made himself the second of Moris's two things.

Behind him, in the sealed warm room, the duke traced the road to the border pass on the map, the way a man traces a path he has walked before and will walk again.

Somewhere above the townhouse the snow was still falling, soft and withholding. And in the palace, in a small room, a king sat alone with the empty chair of his son, the name the flute had sung, and the decision he had not yet made.

Halden, walking the cold streets of Valdris, heard none of it. He only felt the name — Moris's name, Renn's name, braided like bass and melody — and he understood the memorial was six days away, and that he was going to have to choose, and that every choice in the kingdom ran through a bone man's hands.

Chapter 11: The King's Guilt

The chamber they gave him overlooked the inner court, and all night the snow fell on the stones — layering, quiet, the withholding of truth made visible. Halden sat at the window with the flute in his lap and did not play it. The bone was warm. It had told the court's deepest secret to the man who held it, and the man had dismissed him without a word, and now the room held nothing but his own breathing and the snow's white arithmetic.

He put the truth together the way a song is assembled, note by note. The posting north had not been routine. Aldric had been sent to quell the quiet north in the very season Duke Moris's riders were known to hunt there — the border pass where a prince could die of a common thief, where the snow would bury the signature. Renn had known. Not the blade — the king had not ordered the killing — but the place, the season, the brother. A temporary peace, bought with a son's life. The flute had caught the bass line under the melody: the hand that signed, the will that posted. The king had heard his own name in the song, and his face had broken — grief first, and then something colder, which was fear. A man afraid of a truth he had known all along.

Halden understood, then, the shape of the cage. The court was not a place of liars by nature; it was a place of griefs, each walled behind a careful silence. The queen who must not see her husband's hands shake. The councillor who hummed to cover a dropped vote. The king who wore guilt like a winter coat and called it statecraft. Everyone in it played a censored song of their own life, and the flute was the only instrument in the whole city that could not be muted.

He was reaching for the window latch — a walk, the cold, anything — when a knock came, soft, a held note released. The queen. A small woman in grey, she entered without ceremony and did not look at the flute. She looked at him the way one looks at a messenger who has come from a long and dreadful road.

"My husband has not slept since the playing," she said. "Neither has his brother. You have made a very quiet noise, and the whole palace is listening to it."

Halden said nothing. The dead cannot lie, and he was learning that neither could the living, when the price was right.

The queen sat on the edge of the bed, hands folded. "They will ask you to play the accepted death. The brigands. The nameless thief." She said it gently, the way one delivers bad news to a child. "I have come to tell you that you may, and no one will think less of you. Grief is a song the living are allowed to sing wrong."

"But the dead," Halden said, "are not."

Her silence was a held note that did not resolve. When she left, the snow had stopped, and the room was colder than the night.

Chapter 12: The Order

The chamberlain came at noon, a man of such polished courtesy that his words arrived already forgiven. The memorial would be held at the turning of the week, and Halden would play. The court had decided on the accepted account — a common thief, a knife, a fall in the snow; no names, no border, no uncle. It was, the chamberlain said, a mercy to the family and a mercy to the realm, and a man of sense would understand that some truths were too heavy for a kingdom to carry at once.

"For your service," the chamberlain went on, "the king grants you gold, a place at court, the title of Musician of the Crown." He paused, and his smile did not change — only the temperature of it. "And your family in the salt town will want for nothing. We know where they live. We have always known where the herald found you."

Halden heard the threat as a bass line under a pleasant tune. Soft and exact. His mother's name, spoken kindly, was a door being held ajar.

"The truth," the chamberlain said, "would tear the kingdom. The lie will hold it together. That is not wickedness, flutist. That is architecture."

Halden looked at the flute on the table, and the flute looked back at him with Maren's silence. One playing per dead. Aldric would have one song, ever, and the whole court wished to spend it on a lie — to bury the prince's true last word in a thief's grave. The dead cannot lie, but the living, it seemed, could rehearse for them.

He did not refuse. He did not accept. He asked for the night, and the chamberlain bowed as though a great generosity had been granted, and left the gold on the table where it gleamed like a small, obscene moon.

Alone, Halden took up the flute. Its breath was cold against his palm. He raised it to his lips and played one note — the opening of the true song, the border pass, the snow — and let it die in the air.

He could still hear it. That was the terrible thing. The truth was in the room with him, waiting to be sung or sealed. The gold on the table was the price of sealing it. The gold, and the salt town, and the name of the author, and Maren's bone singing a lie forever.

He did not sleep. He sat with the note ringing in his skull, and by morning he had not decided — and that, he understood, was its own kind of answer, the way a rest in a score is still part of the song.

Chapter 13: The Censorship

The court composer was a good man, and that was the worst of it. His name was Addo, and he had written the coronation hymn, the war requiem, the lullaby the old queen had hummed to the present one. He greeted Halden in the music room with tea and manuscript paper, as if they were colleagues on a pleasant commission.

"We must make it beautiful," Addo said. "A grief the whole realm can share. Grief unites, flutist. Names divide."

They composed the false song together, note by note. A nameless thief on the border road, a knife, a fall, the snow closing over like a white hand. Addo gave the thief a motive — desperation, a starving family — and a face no one would grieve. He wrote a melody of noble sorrow, the kind that lets a court weep and then go to supper. By any craft it was a good song.

Halden watched the truth being rewritten, and it was like watching a wound heal the wrong way. The border pass became a road. The changed guard became a routine detail. Moris's shoulders, the shadow under the melody, became nothing at all — a space where the music simply failed to go, a silence shaped like a man.

At one point Halden played a single phrase of the real song — the true opening, the wind off the pass — and held it, waiting. Addo turned, tapping his pen. "That's nice," he said. "A touch of the wild. We'll use something like it." And he wrote it down, flattened, a folk ornament, the edge filed away.

He did not hear it. That was the horror. A good man, with a good ear, and the truth passed through him like wind through an open door, and he wrote the lie down instead.

That night Halden played the false score on the flute, alone, to hear what his instrument would do with it. The notes came out perfect — and dead. The bone did not argue. It did not even refuse. It played the lie, note for note, with Maren's breath, and the silence afterward was the coldest thing he had ever heard.

The false flute would be the first lie his instrument had ever told. And Maren, wherever she was, would be inside it, singing it forever.

Chapter 14: The Choice

The night before the memorial, Halden did not sleep. He sat in the cold chamber with the two songs in his head — the true one, sharp as a wound, and the false one, smooth as a stone worn by water — and weighed them the way the living weigh everything: in fear.

The truth. He could play it in the great hall, before the king and the duke and the assembled court, and the flute would name them both, killer and author, and the truth would tear the kingdom open like a winter river. He had seen what a name does to a room. His mother and father in the salt town would be given to the king's mercy — and the king, who had already killed a son with a signature, would know exactly what to do with the family of the man who unmade him. And Halden himself would be dead before the bells stopped ringing. The truth was a beautiful, fatal song.

The lie. He could play the accepted death, the common thief, the knife, the fall. The realm would hold together. The king would keep his crown. Moris would keep his grievance and his smile. And Halden would take the gold, the title, the place at court, and become the kingdom's greatest liar — the only flutist whose instrument had ever sung false. Maren's bone would sing it forever. She would be trapped inside a false song, her last true word buried under a thief's grave, and every time he breathed into the flute he would breathe the lie deeper.

He tried to pray. There was no god for flutists. The dead had their keepers and their shelves; the living had their altars and their wars; a man with a bone in his hands had no one to bargain with but the dead, and the dead were silent tonight.

He considered running. The guards were kind and everywhere. Every door he might take led to a corridor with a lamp and a spear. The city knew his face now, and the catch would cost him worse than the choice.

He asked the flute what to do. He held it to his lips and breathed, and there was no song in it — only the memory of songs, only Maren's patient silence. The bone had already said everything it was carved to say. It was waiting for him to speak.

The candle burned down. Sleep took him without his permission, the way cold takes a man on a road, and the last thing he knew was the two songs in his head, the true one and the false one, turning against each other like two rivers — and then the dream came.

Chapter 15: The Sister's Voice

She came to him in the thin hour before the dawn bell, and she was fourteen again, the age she had been when the fever took her — whole, two arms, the right one soft and living where the flute was hard and carved. She stood at the foot of his cot and looked at him the way she had looked at him in the salt town, when he was small.

"Halden," she said. Her voice was not music and not breath; it was the shape both made when they were the same thing. "You are carrying a name."

He sat up. His hands remembered the flute before his mind did. "The king's. And the duke's. The flute sang them both."

"I know," she said. "I was there. I am always there." She sat on the edge of the cot, and the cot did not shift, because she had no weight except the weight of what was true. "The dead do not need the living to avenge them, Halden. We do not ask for blood. We died; blood does not undo it. What we need — the only thing we need — is for the living to tell the truth."

"About us," he said.

"About everything," she said. "A truth is a name, and a name is a song. When you sing it, the dead are at peace, because they have been heard. When you bury it, you bury us with it."

He wanted to argue — to tell her about the gold, the threat, the cold arithmetic of a kingdom held by an omission. But she was his sister, and the dead cannot lie.

"If you play the lie," she said, "my bone will sing it forever. I will be inside a false song, every note a wall, and I will not be able to find you." Her hand, the whole one, found his wrist. "If you play the truth, I am at peace. And so, in time, are you."

"The king will kill me. He will kill our parents."

"He might," Maren said. "That is the cost, and it is real, and I will not pretend it is not." She kissed his brow, and the kiss was cold and light as the first breath of winter. "But a lie is a living death, Halden. Worse than any death the king can give you. A false song would hold us both, forever."

She stood, and the grey light came through her as if she were made of it. At the door she turned, and for a moment she was not the girl of fourteen but the grown woman she would have been, whole and at rest.

"Carry the name," she said, "or bury it. But carry it like a man who knows what a name is."

He woke with the real song's melody in his teeth — the border pass, the snow, the two names woven through it — and it had never sounded so clear. The memorial bell had begun.

Chapter 16: The Memorial

The great hall of Valdris was a candle in the shape of a room. A thousand flames, tiered in iron, drew the vaulted roof up into a warm dark, and the assembled court stood along the walls in blacks and greys, a congregation of held breaths. On the dais, on a bier of white oak, lay the bones of Prince Aldric — dressed and arranged, the skull's carved smile turned toward the doors. The honored dead, given the place of honor, waiting for their one song.

The king sat on the throne, grey and small, his hands gripping the arms as if the chair were a ship in a storm. Duke Moris stood at his right hand, broad and warm, grief worn like a favorite coat, nodding to this lord and that. Princess Lira stood at his left, her face quiet and exact, and beside her the queen, veiled, her hands a white knot at her breast. The court composer stood at the edge of the dais with his score, ready, smiling at nothing.

Halden walked to the center of the hall, and the crowd opened for him the way water opens for a stone. He wore the grey robe of his trade. In his left hand he carried the false score — the accepted death, the common thief, the knife, the fall — and in his teeth, under his breath, he carried the true song.

The king looked at him and nodded. Begin.

Halden raised the flute. The room held its breath — he felt it, one vast drawn note, the entire hall become a single phrase waiting to resolve. He looked at the bones of the prince who had once sat beside the feared bone man at a festival, when no one else would, and had asked about Maren, and had said that the dead should be allowed their last word.

The chamberlain was watching him with the gold already counted in his eyes. The king was watching him with something that wanted to be hope. Moris was watching him with a smile that had been wearing the same note for thirty years. And Lira was watching him with the truth already in her mouth, waiting for him to sing it first.

Halden raised the flute higher, fitted it to his lips, and did not play the censored song.

The room's held breath went on holding. The candle flames stood still. The false score lay in his left hand, beautiful and seamless and dead — and Halden stood in the center of the great hall with the true song gathering in his chest like snow, and every eye in the room was on him, and he had not yet chosen how to begin.

But he had chosen how not to.

Chapter 17: The Audience

The moment stretched like a held note, and Halden, standing in the center of it, finally looked at the audience.

The nobles wore their masks, and their masks wore them. Here a dowager counting the candles, reckoning the cost; there a young lord whose grief was a pose rehearsed in a mirror; here a merchant-lord whose eyes went to the throne the way a hawk's go to a field. Each had come to be seen grieving, and each had come to hear a name — hoping it would be someone else's.

The guards stood at the doors, patient, armored, paid not to hear. The prince's empty chair stood at the edge of the dais, kept for his soul, a seat no one would fill, a silence with a shape.

Halden read each face the way he read a dead man's song — for what it wanted. The king wanted silence: his hands on the throne's arms were the hands of a man begging the music not to arrive. Duke Moris wanted an alibi: beneath the grief a costume, a watchfulness, a man listening to the notes form, ready to smile through whatever they named. Princess Lira wanted the truth: her eyes held his and did not look away, and in her he saw what he had seen in the prince that day at the festival — the willingness to hear a whole song.

And the queen, veiled, her hands knotted in her lap — Halden saw his mother's fear in the queen's hands. The same grip, the same whitened knuckles, the same prayer to a god who does not answer the grieving. His mother was in the salt town, and these hands were in the palace, and they were the same hands.

He looked down at the false score in his left hand. The accepted death. The common thief. The knife. The fall. The beautiful, seamless lie.

He raised the flute and blew the first note of the true song — low, tentative, a test — and held it against the score's opening phrase. They did not match. They had never matched. One of them was a wall, and one of them was a door.

Halden let the note die and put the score down on the stones at his feet.

The hall shifted. Someone's breath. A rustle of sleeves. The king leaned forward a finger's width. Moris's smile grew careful. And Lira, very slightly, nodded.

The bone flute was at Halden's lips, and the true song was rising in his chest like a held breath finally allowed to move.

Chapter 18: The Playing

Halden played.

The first notes were the border pass in winter — wind over a razor of stone, the cold that gets into a man's teeth and stays. The hall heard the road, the emptiness, the snow falling on a place no one should have sent a prince. Then the melody turned, and the changed guard came through — men exchanged at the last moment, faces new to each other, a door left open in the shape of a schedule.

The duke's riders came next, a drumline under the wind, hooves on the frozen road, and Halden felt the hall go still as every person there heard the truth they had always suspected: not brigands. Never brigands. Riders who knew the road and the night and the hour.

And then the face. The music shaped it the way a sculptor shapes clay — broad shoulders, a smile that had never once met its own eyes, a hand lifted in a gesture that might have been a greeting and was instead a killing. Moris's face, in the moment of the killing, rendered note for note in the voice of the dead prince, who could not lie.

A sound went through the hall like a wound. Someone cried out. A chair scraped. Moris himself — Halden did not look at him, but he heard the silence where a protest should have been, the frozen breath of a man hearing his own song.

But the flute was not done. Under the melody, under the wind and the snow and the face, a bass line had been moving all along — patient, deliberate, the sound of a hand signing. The posting. The order that sent Aldric to the quiet north in the very season the riders hunted there. The will behind the will. The author.

The flute named him. Note by note, the way a name is spelled in a song, the flute played the king — Renn, the father, the signer, the man who had bought a peace with his son's life and called it statecraft. The dead cannot lie. The flute could not soften it. The bass line resolved into the king's name like a stone dropping into still water, and the water closed over it, and the whole hall heard the ring.

The music ended.

No one moved. The candles burned. The king sat on his throne, and his name hung in the air above the hall like smoke, like a verdict, like a bell that had been rung and could not be unrung. Moris stood at his right hand, red rising up his throat, his mouth open on a word that would not come. Lira stood at his left, and her face was not triumph — it was the face of a woman who had known, and who had just heard it sung aloud.

Halden lowered the flute. His hands were shaking. He had played the whole story — the killer and the author, the blood and the will — and the hall had heard what it had always known.

For one long moment, nobody breathed.

Chapter 19: The Truth

The silence after the last note was a thing with weight. It settled on the hall like the snow that had buried the pass — except this snow fell upward, out of the king's name, and it did not bury anything. It revealed.

Halden stood in the center and watched a name do what names do. The king's face had emptied — not of color, but of everything that had been holding him upright. The guilt the flute had caught in the private chamber, the guilt he had hidden behind grief and statecraft and the careful architecture of an omission, was being sung aloud before his court, and there was no throne tall enough to stand above it. He looked not like a king caught in a crime but like a man hollowed out, a drum whose skin had been cut, and the hall could see through him to the shape of what he had done.

"Treason." The word came from the right, red and raw. Duke Moris was rising, his broad face gone from warmth to fury in the time it takes a note to decay, and he was pointing — at the flute, at Halden, at the air where his own name had sung itself. "Treason, this is treason, a dead boy's grudge and a bone man's spite —"

He did not say he had not done it. The hall noticed. Halden noticed. The dead cannot lie, and the living, when they shout loud enough, think the lie will pass for truth — but the shout had a hole in it, the exact shape of a name.

Princess Lira stepped between them. She was not large; she was not loud; she was quiet and exact, and her voice, when it came, cut the room like a knife through a held note. "The flute does not lie, uncle. You know this. Everyone in this hall knows this." She did not look at Moris. She looked at the court, at the dividing nobles, at the empty chair that had been Aldric's. "We have all just heard what we have all known."

The nobles were dividing by blood. Here the old houses, loyal to the throne, drawing together like iron filings toward a magnet; there the young lords and the soldiers, the duke's men, gathering at the edges, hands not yet on hilts but remembering where the hilts were. A dowager was weeping into her sleeves. The merchant-lord's hawk eyes had gone to the doors.

And Halden stood in the middle with the flute, and he understood, with a cold clarity like a bell note, that he was suddenly the most dangerous man in Valdris. The guards could cut him down. The duke could have him killed before the bells. But he was the only honest instrument in the room — the one thing the whole cage of the court could not tune, could not mute, could not make sing the censored song.

The lie had been unmade in public. It lay at his feet with the false score, and the truth stood over it, breathing.

Chapter 20: The Court Shattered

It broke the way ice breaks on a river in spring — not at one point but everywhere at once, a hundred cracks finding each other.

The duke's men moved first. They had been standing at the edges of the hall, arms crossed, faces unreadable, and they moved now as if the gesture had been waiting in them like a held note, released at a signal Halden never saw. The king's guard answered, blades leaving scabbards in a single long whisper of steel, and the two bodies of armed men met in the aisle like two rivers meeting, and the candles guttered in the wind of them.

The queen screamed. It was not a courtly sound; it was the sound a mother makes, and it came from her like a held note finally released, and it cut through the scrape of boots and the shout of orders and found Halden in the center and pinned him.

Someone knocked the prince's empty chair. It went over with a crack like a bone breaking, the seat kept for a soul, and no one righted it.

Accusations were hurled like stones — "traitor," "usurper," "the bone man did this" — and nobody knew, in the noise, which stones were aimed at the truth and which at the mouths that had sung it. Moris was shouting at the guards, his broad face gone scarlet, and then he was moving, and then the crowd closed over him and Halden could not see whether he had been seized or had taken the moment for the kitchens — only that the spot where the duke had stood was empty and the shouting there had changed its pitch.

The king did not move. That was the terrible center of it. On his throne, with the court breaking around him like a storm around a stone, Renn sat and did not defend himself. He had been told, in front of everyone, the truth he had always known, and he had finally stopped pretending he did not know it. He looked at the riot of his court the way a man looks at weather. His hands, at last, were still.

Halden began to move. He did not run; running drew eyes. He moved the way a tune moves through a hall — by being expected, by being part of the noise, by sliding into the gaps. The crowd was a river now, and he was a piece of it, grey robe, bone flute, empty hands. No one was watching the center anymore; the center had moved to the doors, where the duke's men and the king's guard had become a single indistinguishable violence.

He reached the far aisle. He slipped behind the pillars, past a weeping dowager, past a guard whose eyes were on the dais and not on him. No hand touched his sleeve. No voice called his name.

The cold hit him at the door, and behind him the great hall of Valdris was roaring like a furnace, and Halden carried the song out into the dark — the whole truth of it, safe in his chest like a coal.

The cage, at last, was open. He walked, and did not look back.

Chapter 21: The Abdication

The chamber had been swept of candles when they came to it, and the dark sat in the corners like a held breath. King Renn sat behind the table where he had signed a hundred orders, and he looked smaller than the chair, smaller than his own hands, which lay flat on the wood as if the table were the last shore of something.

Lira stood at the door with her back to the lintel, arms folded, the quiet-exact way she held everything. Halden stood where they had told him to stand, with the flute in its sleeve against his ribs, and he could feel the hall's music still on him — the name, the smoke, the way a room changes when a truth has been sung in it.

The king spoke first. "I will not wear it." He did not mean the crown. He meant the name, or the chair, or the whole weather of the day. "I signed the posting. I knew where Moris rode. I sent my son to a place I knew his uncle hunted, and I called it peace, and I have worn that lie the way other men wear armor — for years, and it never once stopped a blade."

He told them he had abdicated the crown a hundred times already, every night, in the small dark room of his conscience, and the crown had always been there in the morning. "Now you have sung it off me," he said to Halden. "It is the first kindness anyone has done me since I buried him."

He gave the regency to Lira — not as a man choosing a successor but as a man setting down a weight he had carried past its purpose. Lira did not bow. She stood and received it with her chin, the way she received everything, and asked in her quiet way, "And the name?"

"The name is sung," Renn said. "It cannot be unsung. It is the only true thing in this room."

He asked Halden one thing: that the flute never be played for him. "I do not want a last word. I have had all my words. I want to end the way a song ends — when the music chooses to stop, not when someone plays it to death."

The herald cried it from the steps at midnight, and the bells of Valdris took the news and rang it through the city like a slow tide. The people gathered in the cold square, and someone at the back said Halden's name with hatred, and the name traveled from mouth to mouth and changed in the traveling.

Halden heard his own name become a thing with edges. It was the sound of a door he had opened and could not close. He held the flute against his ribs and felt the morning coming, and knew the name was his now — carried, and heavy, and true.

Chapter 22: The Trial

The trial was held in the great hall, in the same air where the truth had been sung, and the empty chair of the prince was gone, and in its place stood the prisoner's rail.

Moris came in guarded, and the room watched him the way a string watches a bow — the whole hall tuned to him, to the width of his shoulders, to the easy charm of his walk. He had been caught at the palace border the night of the memorial, three guards down, snow on his boots, and he had laughed when they took him. The court had not yet seen him break.

Halden testified first. He stood where he had stood to play, and the hall leaned in to hear the flute, and there was only his voice — flat, mortal, the way a melody sounds when the instrument is taken away. The flute could not sing the murder again. One playing per dead. The memorial had used it, and Aldric was silent forever, and so the truth had to come out of Halden's mouth the way it comes out of everyone's: poorly, one word at a time, with all the weight of a man who was the only one who had heard the whole song.

Moris's counsel rose. The defense was beautiful — the council heard how a grieving brother had been framed by a frightened king, how the flutist's music was hearsay sung by a dead boy's own bone, how no signature had been found and no order shown. Halden watched the council's faces soften, one by one, the way snow softens a roof, and he understood that Moris was not defending himself. He was conducting.

Then Lira stood.

She asked one question, in her quiet-exact way, and the hall went very still. "The night of the snow," she said, "the night the guard was changed, the night my brother's letter was taken from his rooms — where were you?"

Moris smiled. He had an answer, smooth as a polished note. "I was at the border gate," he said, "making sure the posting went through." And he paused, and the pause was a note too long, and every musician in the room heard it.

It was a liar's truth — true in every word and false in the whole, the way a censored song is false. The council, who had spent a week in the company of Halden's honesty, heard the difference the way you hear a wrong note in a piece you know by heart.

The council condemned him. The room did not erupt; it settled, the way water settles after a stone. Moris was led out, and at the door he turned and looked at Halden, and his look was not hatred. It was almost curiosity — the regard of a man who had never once been asked to tell the truth, meeting a man who could not tell a lie.

The gavel fell. The name was carried.

Chapter 23: The Duke's Doom

The sentence was old, older than the crown, older than the bells: for an oathbreaker, the northern gate. Not the axe, not the rope, not the block — the gate, the wall, the snow beyond. The doom was not death by a hand but death by a season. Valdris gave its oathbreakers to the winter, and the winter kept them, and the blood stayed off the stones, and the city could say, in the years after, that it had not killed him.

They led Moris out at the cold hour before dawn. Halden went because they asked him to — the keeper's note said only that the condemned had named him, and the law of the doom was that the condemned might choose their witnesses.

The northern gate opened on a wall of white. The snow beyond was not falling; it was simply there, a depth, an achieved silence, and the wind that came through the gate was the first wind Halden had ever heard that had no melody in it at all.

Moris stood at the threshold, unbound, his hands free and his coat open, and he looked at Halden with that same curiosity from the trial.

"Flutist," he said. "Play me."

Halden's hand went to his sleeve. "I cannot."

"One playing," Moris said. "I have never had a last word. I have had thirty years of the wrong word, and I would like to hear what mine sounds like, sung by a dead girl's bone."

"I cannot," Halden said, and it cost him more than a playing would have. "The flute is bound to one dead. She is not you. I have no other music for you."

Moris stood a long moment in the open gate, and the wind moved past him, and then he laughed — not the easy laugh of the townhouse, but a lower thing, a note with the truth briefly in it. "Then the dead have better taste than the living," he said, "because she chose you, and you would not choose me."

He walked into the snow. He did not turn. The gate closed behind him, and the sound of it was the sound of a song finished, and Halden stood in the cold and watched the place where a man had been.

He felt nothing he expected. Not triumph — the name was sung, the trial was done, the crown was off the king's head. Not pity — Moris had chosen his song thirty years ago and walked into it every day since. What he felt was the shape of a man who had picked the wrong melody and played it long past the point where stopping was possible.

The snow stayed where it was. Halden turned from the gate, and the city behind him was quiet, and the name he carried was lighter now, and heavier in a different way. Somewhere out in the white, a man was walking, and the winter would finish the song Halden's flute had refused to begin.

Chapter 24: The Flight

The horse was waiting in the lower stable, a grey mare with a white blaze, saddled and breathing fog into the dawn. Halden found her the way Lira's note said he would, and under the saddle was a bag of bread, a skin of water, and a road north.

He did not need the note to know why he was leaving. The city had woken divided, and division sharpens knives. Moris's retainers wanted his tongue — a flutist's tongue, cut out, so the only witness to the whole song could never sing it again. The old king's men, the ones who had built their lives on the censored version, wanted the song buried deeper than bone. And half the court, the half that had loved the lie, wanted the truth unsung, unremembered, unwitnessed — which meant they wanted the only witness gone.

Lira met him in the stable dark, a shawl over her shoulders, the regent's weight already settling on her like a coat not yet broken in. She did not argue with him about staying. She did not pretend to protect him from what the city had become.

"The truth is a guest in this city," she said. "It needs to live somewhere else for a while. You have carried it well. Now carry it somewhere it can rest."

She pressed a purse into his hand, and the purse was heavy with gold, and Halden understood that this was not a gift but a commission: the regent of Valdris was paying him to be the truth's keeper until the city could hold it without breaking.

He rode out of the lower gate as the bells began. The bells of Valdris rang his name — the way bells ring a wedding, the way they ring a fire — half hero, half outlaw, and the people who heard it did not know which half to fear.

He did not look back at the city. He looked forward at the road, and the road was white, and the mare's breath hung in the air behind them like a held note, and the name rang on in the cold behind him, growing fainter with each stride, until it was only the echo of an echo, and then only the sound of his own heartbeat keeping time with the hooves.

He was a flutist with a flute that had told the truth, and he was running. Somewhere ahead, snow. Somewhere behind, a city learning to ring a new name. And in his sleeve, Maren's bone, warm against his ribs, patient as the dead.

Chapter 25: The Hunt

The riders behind him never came close enough to see, and that was their error — a hunter who cannot be seen is not hunting, he is merely following. Halden learned the distance of them the way a musician learns a room: by the resonance, by the way the road changed when they were on it.

He rode by night and hid by day, and the hiding was the worst of it, the long cold stillness in a wood or a barn while the mare stood steaming in the straw and the name rang in his head like a bell that would not be silenced. He could not play. A playing costs a day's strength and a scrap of memory, and he would need both, and besides, a song is loud, and the riders were listening.

On the third day he came to a village where the mill had stopped, and the miller was dead in his own doorway, and the miller's daughter was weeping over him in the snow. Halden looked at the dead man and heard the one playing in his mind, the way the trade always announced itself — a note waiting to be breathed.

He did not mean to play. He was hungry, and the village was afraid, and fear and hunger are the same instrument. He asked the daughter if she wanted her father's last word. She stared at him. The villagers stared. A bone man, on the north road, in winter — the children drew back, and one of them began the rhyme under his breath and was hushed.

He played the miller.

The song came out of the bone flute and into the cold, and it was a small song, a working song, the rhythm of grain and grind. And then it turned. The miller's last truth was not about the wheat or the weather. It was about the man who had come in the night, a man the miller had trusted, a partner, an apprentice — the one who had taken the strongbox and left the miller in his own doorway with the snow coming down.

The village heard it. The village forgot Halden in its grief — the way a whole room forgets the instrument when the song breaks it open. They wept, and they turned on the apprentice, and they did not see the bone man mount his grey mare and ride on.

Each playing bought him a day. Each playing left a trail of wonder and fear behind him, a wake of small truths sung into the cold, and the riders on the road behind him read the wake the way you read a melody — note by note, closer each time.

He was feeding on the dead to stay alive, and the dead were feeding him their last words, and somewhere between the mill and the next village he understood that the trade had never been about him at all. It was about the last breath. It was always the last breath.

Chapter 26: The Border

The pass was not a gate. It was a wound in the mountain, a notch where the range had been cut, and the road through it was the road Aldric had died on, and Halden felt it before he saw it — the way you feel a note before it is sounded, the air already shaped for it.

The storm came down as he reached the mouth of the pass. The snow arrived not as weather but as an achieved condition, a white that erased the difference between sky and ground, and the mare stopped, and Halden understood that they would not pass through by sight.

He did not plan it. He had not played for the pass. But the flute rose to his lips the way it always rose — as if it had been waiting — and he breathed, and the flute played the pass's memory of itself.

It was not testimony. It was the place remembering. Out of the wind came the shape of that night a week before the world changed: the changed guard, the hoofbeats, the snow. And then, rising under the storm like a melody that had been waiting in the mountain all along, the prince's last song — not the version the memorial had sung, but the pass's own. Aldric, alone, facing the riders, and in his last moment the song did not rage; it simply named the faces, and one of the faces was his uncle's, and then the snow took the music down.

Halden stood in the pass with the song in his teeth and the hunters somewhere behind him on the road, and he understood that the mountain had been keeping the prince's last word for him — a second hearing, a grace the trade should not allow.

The hunters' horns came up the pass, thin in the storm, and Halden turned the mare off the road and into the ravine the locals called the Widow's Stairs — a stair of ledges and wind-scoured rock that climbed the pass's shoulder and came down the far side, a way the riders would not know.

The mare took the stairs like a creature bred for them, and the storm howled, and the horns faded, and then the pass was behind them, and the ground opened southward into a lower country, river-dark and faintly green under the snow.

Halden pulled the mare up on the far side of the boundary. Valdris was behind him — the city, the crown, the name, the bells. He raised the flute, and he played one long note, and he let it go into the wind, and the note carried back over the pass like a thread, the only message he could leave the country that had made him: I am here, I am alive, the truth is still in my keeping.

The note hung in the cold and then was gone. He turned the mare south, into the lowlands, and did not look back at the border again.

Chapter 27: The Exile

The lowlands were a different country, and the difference was in the air. No bells. No walls. The rivers ran open between small farms, and the dead of the lowlands had their own songs, smaller and slower, and no one on the river roads knew the bone man of Valdris, and Halden let the name go the way you let go of a note you have held too long — with a certain relief, and a certain ache where it had been.

He sold music for bread. He did not say what he was. To the farmers he was a traveling flutist, a thin man with a grey mare and a wind-chapped face, and they paid him in eggs and apples and, once, a pair of boots that almost fit. He played their dead when they asked, and the lowland dead were gentle — the drowned, the fevered, the old who had gone in their sleep — and their last songs were small and true and cost him a day's strength each, and the days added up, and the strength came back slower each time.

The loss of everything — name, city, family, the whole weather of his old life — sat in him the way cold sits in a room that has been left too long: not loud, not dramatic, just there, in the corners, in the breath.

He came to a crossroads village in a thaw, and a beggar woman was sitting in the mud with a child on her lap, and the child was dead, and the woman was past weeping, and Halden asked her, in the trade's old question, if she wanted the child's last word.

The woman looked at him with the particular emptiness of someone who has already grieved. "What last word?" she said. "He had no words. He was three."

Halden did not know what to play for a child who had never had a song of his own. He raised the flute, and the flute answered for him — a small phrase, a few notes, the shape of a child's running, the feel of a small weight carried. It was not testimony. It was the nearest thing the trade had to a lullaby.

The woman wept, and her weeping was the first sound she had made in days, and in that moment Halden was not the kingdom's truth-teller, not the name the bells had rung, not the outlaw of the north road. He was just a man with a flute, and the flute had done what flutes are for.

He rode on in the evening. The cold sat in him, and the ache of the lost name, and under it all, faint as a melody played far off, something that was almost like the beginning of a new song.

Chapter 28: The Flute's Song

The change came without warning, on a night when the inn was too cold to sleep in and Halden sat by the river with the flute in his hands, playing to the dark because playing was the only prayer he knew.

The flute played a song he had not chosen.

It was not testimony. It was not a dead person's last truth, rising up out of the bone to be confessed. It was a lullaby — low, patient, the kind of tune a mother hums without knowing she is humming — and Halden stopped, and the music went on without him, as if the flute had a mouth of its own.

He half-remembered it from childhood. From the salt town, from the kitchen with the stove, from a voice he had not heard since he was twelve. Maren, singing. Not the fourteen-year-old of his dreams, not the bone in his sleeve, but the living girl, before the fever, humming over the bread dough in a house that still had a whole family in it.

"Is that you?" he whispered to the flute.

The lullaby ended. The flute was silent, the way it was silent when she was listening.

He did not understand at first. The trade had only ever had one song — testimony, confession, the last truth of the dead. But the dead of the lowlands were gentler, and their last songs were smaller, and he had been playing them all winter without noticing that the size of the truth was shrinking.

A lost key. An unkissed cheek. A pie in the oven, promised and never eaten. An old woman's last song was not a judgment; it was a recipe, the particular way she had folded the dough, and the daughter who heard it wept and burned nothing and said "that was her, that was exactly her" with a wonder Halden had never once heard in the great hall of the ossuary.

He learned, sitting by the lowland rivers, that truth has many sizes, and most of them are small.

That night, for the first time since Maren died, he played the lullaby all the way through, and the flute did not fight him, and he understood that the bone had been waiting all these years not to be a testament but to be held — to be played the way it had been when it was still a hand, still a voice, still his sister.

The song was not testimony. It was memory, given breath. And for one held note, in the cold by the river, the two of them — the flutist and the bone — were not the trade. They were a brother and a sister, and the song was the one they had always had, and it was enough.

Chapter 29: The New Tune

It began at a wedding, because weddings are where a traveling flutist is always welcome, and because the bride's father asked him to play something for the living — "we have no dead here today, flutist, can you manage?" — and Halden found he could.

He had never played for the living. The flute had only ever confessed. It had spoken for the dead, sung their last truths, carried their names. It had never once been asked to dance.

He put his lips to it, not sure what would come out, and what came out was a reel — bright, quick, a tune that had no truth in it but the truth of movement, of feet finding the beat, of a whole room briefly not grieving. The bride laughed. The groom laughed. The children, who had been frightened of the bone man at first, circled him like a weather, drawn in.

He played for the fair the next week. A jig, then a slow air, then something the crowd did not have a name for, a tune that rose and turned and came back to itself the way a river comes back to a bend it loves. The living paid him in laughter. It was the first currency he had been paid in that did not taste like grief.

He told himself this was hiding. The music was a mask; the tunes were how he disappeared into an ordinary crowd; every reel was a small lie he told the world about who he was.

The flute knew better. The flute, which had been his sister, which had learned the lullaby, which had spent seventeen years carrying the last words of the dead — the flute was healing, and it let him feel it, note by note, a warmth coming back into the bone like a hand returning to a cold room.

The rumor came up the river from the south, carried by a merchant who had heard it in the capital markets: the regency of Valdris had posted a bounty.

Not for his death. The merchant was clear about that, half amused. "It's a reward," he said, "for your safe return. For the man who brings the flutist home unharmed. The regent's own seal, they say. A fortune in gold, and the name cleared in the square."

Halden's hands went still on the flute.

The hunters had wanted his tongue. This wanted him home. It was a summons wearing the clothes of a pardon, a net made of kindness and gold and the weight of a name he had been trying to lay down — and it frightened him more than the riders on the north road ever had, because the riders he could outrun, and this he could not outrun without also outrunning the truth.

He played the reel again, and the fair turned around him, and somewhere south a bell was waiting to ring his name. He told himself he was hiding. The flute, in his sleeve, knew the difference between hiding and being found.

Chapter 30: The Village

The river village of Kestrel Ford sat in a bend of the water where the ford gave it its name, and the winter came down on it gently, the way winter comes down on places that have learned to hold it. Halden arrived with the first hard snow, a flutist with a grey mare and no name worth keeping, and the village gave him a room over the smithy because the smith's wife had lost a brother to the fever and the flute had sung him for her, small and true, and she did not ask who Halden was.

The room was small and warm. The smith's hammer below was a kind of timekeeping — a bass line the village kept under everything, patient, unchanging. Halden slept the first full night he had slept since the memorial, without dreaming of the pass, and woke to the smell of the forge and the ordinary noise of a place going about its ordinary day.

He played at the fair. The tune had a name now, the one the crowd had learned to call him by — not Halden, not the bone man, but something smaller, a word that meant "the flutist with the grey mare" — and the name did not ring like a bell, it simply sat in the air the way a name should sit, lightly.

He taught the innkeeper's son the pipe. The boy was ten and clumsy and brilliant at it, and Halden found that teaching was a kind of playing, a way of giving the music to someone else so it could be alive in more than one mouth. The boy learned a scale, then a reel, then the beginning of the lullaby, and the innkeeper wept the first time he heard it, and Halden did not tell him what it was or who had first sung it.

For the first time since the memorial — since the name had been sung, since the bells, since the flight, since the north road and the border and the long cold — Halden went a whole day without thinking of the name.

He did not notice at first. The day was ordinary: bread, the forge, a lesson on the pipe, the river running dark under its skin of ice. It was only in the evening, sitting by the smithy fire with the flute warm in his hands, that he realized the name had not visited him once, and the discovery sat in him like a held note finally released.

The village was ordinary. He had been starving for ordinary — for a day that meant nothing, for a fire that was just a fire, for a tune that did not have to be true.

He played the pipe softly, the boy's scale, up and down, and the flute answered him, and the sound of it over the river was the sound of a man learning, slowly, what it meant to be alive among the living.

The winter held. The name stayed away. And in the room over the smithy, warm and small, Halden began, for the first time in his life, to be a man with a flute instead of a man the dead spoke through.

Chapter 31: The Farmer's Daughter

The father came to Halden at the smithy door, cap in his hands, snow on his shoulders. He did not ask whether the bone man was welcome. He asked whether the bone man would play his wife.

"The one playing," Halden said.

"The one playing," the father agreed. "She was not finished."

The cottage was low and warm, the hearth banked. On the table lay the mother's arm bone, clean and white, and beside it a slate and a stick of chalk, and standing against the far wall a girl of seventeen with eyes that watched the door. Halden knew the look. He had worn it himself, once, for years.

He lifted the bone. It was warm from its place by the fire — someone had kept it warm for her, which was not the custom and was, he thought, the truest prayer he had ever seen. The father's hands shook as he set out bread and salt. The girl did not move.

The flute was Maren's bone, Maren's breath; when he played, the mother came out of her own bone like steam off the river. The song was a small truth. An unfinished quilt, half a star left for the needle. A daughter's real name, spoken soft, that the world had never once used for her. A promise about the winter: that she would not leave before the frost, and she had not, quite. The last note hung in the warm air like the smell of bread.

The father wept. The girl did not speak. She watched the door, and then she watched Halden.

He took his fee — a loaf, a place by the fire — and walked back toward the smithy through the falling snow. Behind him, soft, the creak of a door. She followed him all the way across the village, and stood in his doorway until he let her in.

She wrote on the slate, the chalk grinding slow: "Play her again."

"I cannot," he said. "One playing. She has said her last."

The girl erased the line with her sleeve. She wrote again, and turned the slate to him.

"Then play me. I am not dead."

Halden stood very still. All his life the flute had been for the dead — one breath, one truth, one last time. The living were the ones who listened, who grieved, who lied. He did not know the notes for a living thing. He did not know if the bone would take her.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I do not know the song yet."

She nodded once, and sat by his fire, and watched the door.

Chapter 32: The Second Playing

The drowned boy was first. Sela led him to the riverbank at dawn and pointed into the shallows where the ice had cracked — a boy of nine, gone under the millrace in the spring, fished out by his own father. His bone was a finger, kept in a lidded jar in the mother's kitchen. The mother stood dry-eyed while Halden played; the song was the rope, the slick of the bank, the lie the boy had told her about staying off the ice. When it ended she knelt in the snow and burned his clothes in the yard, and the whole village gathered at the smoke, and no one looked away.

The hanged man nobody loved was a stranger who had swung from the bridge oak two winters past. The village had called it grief or guilt and buried him in the field corner. His song was a debt — six bushels of seed he owed a widow, taken in a bad year, never repaid. Halden played it in the square, and the widow, an old woman with a face like a shut door, wept and said the seed had been forgiven the day the rope took him, but that she had never dared to say it aloud. She said it aloud now.

The old woman who owed everyone an apology sat up in her bed and listened to her own mother sing through Halden's hands — a song of a dozen small cruelties, a mislaid letter, a harsh word, a wedding she had gossiped away. The village listened. The old woman said, at the end, to the room full of her neighbors: "All true. Every note."

That night there was no playing. There was a table set long in the square, and food carried out, and the drowned boy's mother sat between the widow and the old woman, and they talked until the candles drowned in their own wax.

Sela sat apart, watching, but the watching had changed. Her eyes had lost their door-keeping. She wrote on her slate, at the edge of the firelight: "Truth is safe here." And then, quieter, so that he almost did not read it: "So are you."

Halden looked at the village, at the smoke going up from ten chimneys into the winter dark, and felt something loosen in his chest that had been cinched tight since the pass. He had carried the dead for so long, one per person, one breath each. He had never once seen what happened to the living afterward — the airing of grief, the paying of debts, the burning of a boy's clothes in the honest smoke of the yard.

He had only ever played the last note. The village was showing him the song that came after.

Chapter 33: The Reputation

Word went downriver the way water goes — finding every low place, every ear. The truth-telling flutist was at Kestrel Ford. The bone man who played the dead straight, no coin for it but a loaf and a place by the fire.

The pilgrims came on the thaw road. A woman from a village downriver brought her husband's hand bone wrapped in linen and asked for his last words; the song was a field he had meant to plow, a name he had wanted said at his grave — her name, said properly. She left with the linen empty and her face washed clean.

A lord came with a closed face and his brother's finger bone in a ring box. He did not say what he wanted. The song was a confession — a small poisoning, a stolen betrothal, done in the years when they were boys and nobody believed the younger. The lord sat a long time in the snow after, and then he rode home, and Halden heard later that he had restored the land and written the letter. The truth had wanted that all along.

A priest came with his own doubt and a dead man's rib he should not have been carrying. He wanted to know if God answered. The song was a man dying of a fever in a hayloft, who sang to himself in the dark and called it prayer, and who died holding a sound like an answer. The priest wept, because the sound was not a word. It did not need to be.

Halden played them all, one per dead, one breath each, and sent them away lighter, and the flow did not stop. He told himself it would pass, the way fame passes in small places — a season's wonder, then the mill wheel turns and the wonder drowns. But the road stayed rutted with feet.

Sela found him at dusk on the smithy step, chalk in hand. She wrote: "They came for the dead."

He nodded. "It is the trade. They always do."

She erased it, slowly, so that he would watch. She wrote again: "They'll stay for you."

Halden looked at the words a long time. He had wanted to be nothing here — a man with a flute, a tune at a fair, no name at all. But the village had named him anyway. They had given him a second name, the way the capital had, and he understood with a small cold that every name he took became a door, and doors could be knocked upon, and a door that is knocked upon is a door that is found.

He had been found before. He remembered the north road and the gray cloaks behind him, and the snow. The road had never stopped being the road. It had only, for a season, stopped being followed.

Chapter 34: The King's Men

The riders came up the thaw road at noon, twelve of them, cloaks of the crown's gray, the captain's hand on his pommel where other men keep their hearts. Captain Voren. Halden knew the name before the herald cried it — Voren was a good man, which was worse than a bad one, because a bad man could be bribed or fought or fled, and a good man could only be hidden from.

The village gave him up the way it gave up everything, with a shrug and a fee. No one had told the riders where the bone man lodged — not exactly. But the innkeeper had met the captain's eyes, and the captain had read the answer there, and that was enough. A shrug and a fee. It was all it ever took.

Sela's hand closed on his wrist. The root cellar was cold and dark and smelled of apples going soft. She pushed him down the ladder, pulled the door shut over his head, and stood on it.

He heard the boots. The smithy, turned over. The inn, the chapel, the priest's own house. Voren was thorough, and he was gentle, and he was afraid of nothing, and that was the worst thing about a good man doing a bad job — he did it well.

Then the boots came to the farmhouse. Halden heard the door open, the silence, the scrape of Voren's heel on the flagged floor.

"There is a man. A bone flutist. You know him."

Nothing. The scrape of chalk on slate — Sela writing.

"Aye, I can read. 'He is not here.' Do you swear it?"

More chalk. Halden, in the dark, pressed his forehead to the cold of the stairs and understood what she was doing. She was telling the truth — the slate was true, every word of it. He was not in the house. She was not lying. She was only choosing which truth to set down, the way the flute chose which dead to speak.

"She's a mute, captain," one of the riders said. "Half the village says the flutist's got her."

"I can see she's a mute," Voren said. "Look at her hand. Still as water. If she were lying, the hand would tell. A liar's hand always tells."

Halden stopped breathing. Sela was not shaking on the outside. She had taught her hand not to lie, but to hold a truth so still that no other truth could move it. She was shaking on the inside; he could feel it through the floorboards and the frozen earth, the way he felt the marsh's dead through reeds.

The boots moved away. The door closed. The riders' hooves went down the road, and the silence they left was the loudest thing Halden had ever heard.

He came up from the dark. Sela stood exactly where she had stood, slate in her arms, tears running down a face that had not moved.

She wrote: "He is not here." And then, under it, smaller: "But I knew he was not far."

Chapter 35: The Close Call

Voren rode on, and Kestrel Ford drew its breath, and everything had changed by the time the breath was out.

Halden felt it in the street the next morning — the turned faces, the doors that closed a half-moment too late, the innkeeper who served his breakfast without meeting his eyes. The village had sheltered him when he was a wonder. It had never bargained for a harbored outlaw, a name that brought gray cloaks up the road.

The innkeeper's son came to him that night with a face like guilt and a message he was too young to understand. The boy had told the truth to the wrong ears at the ford — a rider's friendly question, a boy's proud answer — and now he had heard the answer carried back: riders at the mill, a man pointing at the smithy with the flat of his hand.

"They'll be here by dark," the boy said. "My father says to go."

Halden did not ask what the father meant by go. He packed the flute in his sleeve, a loaf in his pocket, and put on his boots. Sela was at the door before he was, her pack already strapped, her slate wrapped in oilcloth against the river's cold.

They left at midnight by the frozen river, where the ice held and the moon lay flat and white. He walked behind her, watching her breath plume and vanish, counting each one the way he counted notes. Somewhere ahead the marsh waited, and beyond it — he did not let himself say the word. He let the river say it, ice creaking underfoot, the sound a held note that never resolved.

Behind them, when they were a mile gone, the sky over the village went orange. He turned and watched it. The innkeeper's son had been told to burn the room — the whole room, the bed, the chair, the pipe he had been taught to play. To prove the village had never known him.

The fire was small, a single window of it, swallowed quickly by the snow's darkness. But Halden had seen betrayal before, and he knew its arithmetic. A small fire, a large lie — the village choosing a comfortable story over an uncomfortable truth, the way every liar he had ever played for had chosen.

Sela stopped and looked back with him. She wrote, the chalk invisible in the dark, and held the slate to the moon's flat light so he could read: "The fire is theirs. The river is ours."

He nodded. The ice held. They walked on into the marsh's dark.

Chapter 36: The Marsh

The marsh took them before they knew they had entered it — the river thinning to reeds, the reeds to water, the water to a mirror of the sky. Fog lay over it in sheets, and beneath the fog the marsh held its dead the way a held note holds its breath.

The drowned were everywhere. Halden felt them the way he felt Maren's bone in his sleeve — a pressure, a humming, a hundred mouths closed around a hundred last songs. He raised the flute without thinking, and the marsh answered.

It was not one voice. It was a hundred, braided — a girl who had gone in for watercress, a ferryman who had missed his step in the dark, a soldier drowned in armor, singing the weight of it, the pull of it, the last green light. The songs lay over the ground like paths. He learned to walk by the music: where the melody ran clear, the footing held; where it broke and gurgled, the water deepened. The dead were guiding him, note by note, and he followed them as he had followed nothing since the pass.

Sela walked behind him, her skirt hitched, her pack high on her back. The fog swallowed sound; only the flute's thread held them together. He played and walked, walked and played, and the marsh's dead sang the ground.

Then the music went wrong. A step ahead of her, where the reeds gave way to open water without warning — her foot found nothing, and she went down with a gasp that was not a word, was the first sound he had heard from her throat in months, and the marsh closed over her head.

Halden did not think. He dropped to his knees at the water's edge and played — not the drowned, not the dead, not testimony at all. He played the tune he had taught the innkeeper's son, the reel from the fair, the plain living tune that held no truth but joy, and he poured every breath he had into it.

The marsh went quiet. One by one the drowned voices ceased their guiding, the way a room hushes for a child — and the water, as if listening, let her go. She came up gasping, hair streaming, alive, and the only music left was his, the living tune, ringing out over the reeds and the fog.

They came out the far side at dawn, wet and alive and suddenly certain of each other, the way two notes are certain when they resolve. She was shaking with cold, and she looked at him with eyes that had stopped watching doors and started watching him.

She could not write; the slate was drowned. She opened her mouth, and made a sound — not a word, not yet, but the shape of one, a breath given back to the air for the first time in her life. He played the living tune once more, softly, into the new day, and the marsh was silent behind them, its hundred songs spent and at rest.

Chapter 37: The Sanctuary

Beyond the marsh the land rose into hills, and the hills into a road of white stones that wound upward like the line of a plain chant. Halden had stopped playing a day ago — no dead here to play, or none to trouble him, he could not say which — and in the silence the absence of the flute was a second voice, walking beside them.

At the top of the road there was a gate, and the gate had no lock. It was not broken; it had simply never been fitted for one, the hinges open, the posts white with winter. Above it, carved into the lintel, a single note of music — a staff, one note, held forever.

The monastery of the Still Song. Halden had heard the name once, in the capital, in the mouth of a court composer who had crossed himself speaking it. A refuge for the hunted, they said. Monks who kept the bones of their dead and sang them every evening, so that no dead in that place ever went unsung.

The gate opened onto a courtyard of swept white stone. A monk came out of the cloister — old, tonsured, his hands stained with ink and candle-wax — and stopped at the sight of Halden. He looked at the flute in Halden's sleeve, and then at Halden's face, and then he said:

"Halden."

The name, spoken aloud in this green country, fell on Halden like a blow. He had left it in Valdris like a coat too heavy for the road. He had not heard it since the bells rang it over the northern gate.

"You have the advantage of me," Halden said. "I have been called many things, but not that, not for a year."

"Years ago," the abbot said, "a man carried his daughter's bone into this courtyard, and his son, a boy of twelve, breathed the first note into it in the guest cell. I would know that breath anywhere. It is in your flute, and it is in you." He did not ask the story. He did not ask the name Halden was running from, or the names he carried. He only said: "Come in. There is a fire, and supper, and a cell with a window. Keep your story for the song — here, the song is the story."

Halden looked at the gate with no lock, at the swept stones, at the man who had known his father, and felt something in his chest give way, the way ice gives way at the first true thaw.

Behind him, Sela wrote on her drowned slate, careful, word by word: "I like this place."

She had said more than that, in a way. She had said everything.

Chapter 38: The Monastery

The Still Song kept a schedule like a heartbeat. Morning choir in the cold stone chapel, voices rising to the vaults before light; then the garden, frozen and faithful, where the monks sang to the seedlings; then the scriptorium, where tunes were copied the way other monasteries copied scripture, staff and note, note and staff, until the walls themselves seemed to hold a tune in their plaster.

Halden was given a cell with a window, a robe of undyed wool, a place at the long table. No one asked his trade; everyone knew it. The brothers nodded to him as they passed, the way men nod to weather — a fact, not a judgment.

The evening singing was the thing he waited for. At dusk the monks carried the bones of their dead from the keeping-room — not to play, never to play, but to sing. Every death a note in a longer song, they said; their dead were not silenced, they were kept. The voices rose and braided and held, and the bones on their shelf seemed to lean into the sound like reeds into a wind.

Halden sat at the back the first evening with the flute cold in his sleeve and understood, slowly, the difference between his trade and theirs. He gave each dead one breath — one truth, one song, then silence forever. The monks gave their dead a song that never ended, carried in a hundred living throats, passed down, added to. They did not confess the dead. They companioned them.

He had been playing only half the world. He had been the man who unlocked doors, one per person, one per lifetime, and walked away. Here they did not lock the doors at all. They held them open and sang through them every dusk.

Sela was given paper. At supper, on the second night, she set her paper beside his bowl. The writing was careful, practiced in the dusk of the farmhouse:

"The dead are not the only ones with songs."

Halden read it twice. He looked at the flute in his sleeve, the forearm of his sister, the instrument of one breath and forever. Then he looked at the long table, the gray heads bowed over bowls, the hum of the living, and he understood what she was telling him.

He had carried the dead so long that he had forgotten the living could sing too. That he himself could sing. That Maren's bone, which had given a hundred dead their last breath, might yet be taught to give the living their first.

"The dead are not the only ones with songs," he said aloud, tasting it.

Sela smiled — the first smile he had seen her wear — and nodded.

Chapter 39: The Abbot

Brother Anselm was the abbot's name, and it was not a monk's name at all — it was a musician's name, worn like a scar. Halden learned it the way one learns a knot of grief: in pieces.

"He was the first flutist the old court silenced," a brother told him in the garden, pruning, the secateurs clicking like a metronome. "Before your trade was feared, it was merely useful. He played for the old king's father. He played a truth the court did not want. They did not kill him — they made him a silence. They took his flute and gave him a choice, and he chose."

"Chose what?"

"To never play again. To be the only man in the kingdom who could have sung the truth and did not — and who chose the silence instead of the lie." The brother straightened. "They meant it as an ending. He made it a monastery."

Halden found Anselm that evening by the keeping-room fire, the bones of the dead glowing faintly in their shelves, the abbot's hands open in his lap like a man who has stopped holding anything.

"Your father played for me once," Anselm said, "before he was a father. We were both court musicians. He chose the salt town and a family; I chose a lie and then a silence." He looked at Halden with eyes the color of old ash. "You will have heard the old law of the trade. The dead speak once."

"I have heard it," Halden said. "I have lived it. One playing, ever. I have given them all away."

"The law is older than kings," Anselm said, "and it does not end where you think it ends. The dead speak once — but the living must speak always. That is the second half of the law, and it is the half that matters. You have told the truth to a hall of nobles, and to a girl with a slate, and to a marsh full of drowned men. Now I ask you what you fear, now that the truth is told."

Halden sat with the question a long time. The fire leaned and settled. The bones on their shelves held their silence the way a held note holds its breath.

"Silence," he said at last. "I fear the day the flute is silent. I fear that when the dead are done speaking, I will be done speaking too, and there will be nothing left of me but a man who used to play."

Anselm nodded slowly, as if he had been expecting the answer. "Then you have never heard the silence after a true song. You have heard the silence before it — the holding, the waiting, the breath a man takes before he tells. But the silence after a true song is not an ending. It is the only music that costs nothing. The dead ask nothing of it. The living are not wounded by it. It simply is — the way a hall is still after the last note, and the stillness is not empty but full."

He reached out and, very gently, touched the flute in Halden's sleeve.

"One day your flute will fall silent, boy. And on that day you will learn what I learned here: that silence is not the absence of music. It is music that has finished being afraid."

Chapter 40: The Confession

The confession came out of him the way a song comes out of a flute — not all at once, but note by note, in the keeping-room, at the dying of the fire, with the dead listening from their shelves.

He told Anselm the choice. The memorial, the censored score in his hand, the false song the court composer had helped him write — a nameless thief, a knife, a fall. He told him the note of the true song he had played into it, and how the composer had not heard it, and how that had almost been the worst thing: that the lie was so smooth it swallowed the truth whole.

He told him the fear. His mother and father, left in the salt town when he ran, left to the mercy of a king whose name he had sung before his court. He had asked himself a hundred times whether the truth was worth their safety, and he had run before he could answer, and he had been answering it ever since, alone, in the dark, in a hundred rooms with a hundred doors.

He told him the name he carried. Not Moris — Moris was a name the whole kingdom knew now, the killer sung aloud, walking into the snow. He meant the other name, the one the flute had sung beneath the bass line, the one that had made the hall go still: the name of the man who sent his son to a place he knew his brother hunted. He had carried that name for a year like a bone he could not lay down.

And he told him the girl. The one who followed him through the snow, who stood before a soldier's sword with a slate that said he is not here, who went under the marsh water and came up looking at him. He told him that he did not know what to do with a living voice, that all his life he had known only the last note of the dead, and that she had asked him to play her, and he did not know the song.

Anselm heard him the way the flute heard the dead. Without judgment. Note by note. When Halden fell silent, the abbot sat a long moment, and the fire settled, and the bones kept their vigil.

"This is what I know," Anselm said at last. "The dead do not need the living to avenge them. They need the living to tell the truth. That is the whole of the law. You did not write the order. You did not sign the posting. You only played what was true, in a room where everyone else was playing what was safe — and then you carried it north, and you told it again, and again, to a village and a marsh and a girl."

He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to the register of a man speaking only what he knows.

"What remains is not guilt. Guilt is a debt you have paid in full. What remains is grief — and grief is only a song you have not finished playing. It is not a sin. It is a tune. It can be held, and it can be released, and it can be played again in a different key, for a different listener."

Halden looked at his hands. For the first time in a year, they were not shaking.

"What do I do," he said, "with the girl?"

Anselm smiled, a small and weathered thing. "You play her. She told you she is not dead. The flute has spent its whole life giving the dead one last breath. Perhaps it is ready to give the living their first."

Chapter 41: The Peace

He played them one by one, the monks of the Still Song, a century of small songs laid out in the chapel's stone. Each bone had its shelf, each shelf its name, and the names were sung every evening in the choir; but the flute gave each dead man his one last word, and Halden heard them the way the abbot heard confessions, without flinching. A cellarer who had hoarded a sister's gold for fifty years and never spent a coin of it. A prior who had loved the gardener's daughter and built her a fountain he called a well. A novice who had died at twelve of the same fever that had taken Maren, and whose last song was a question about the rain.

The flute's voice had no edge to it. For the first time since Maren died he heard the bone as an instrument and not a wound, and he understood that the monks had been keeping this for him: a place where the dead were not testimony but company.

The abbot asked him, in his quiet way, if he would play the dead brother — the one the old court had silenced along with him, the one whose silence had bought Brother Anselm his. Halden said yes because the old man had not asked before, and because he had let Sela keep her paper.

The song was a garden in rain. That was all. No confession in it, no name, no unfinished business — a garden in rain, and a man's hand holding a trowel, and the smell of turned earth. Halden finished and looked up, and Brother Anselm was weeping without sound, the way the drowned sing, and he said, "I have not heard his voice in forty years. I had forgotten he was gentle."

That night Halden slept through the darkness without dreaming of the pass. No snow, no hoofbeats, no bass line under the wind. He woke at the morning choir to the monks singing the bones, and he lay in his cell and listened, and he thought that this must be what Maren meant by rest — not an ending, but a tune that had finally found its proper tempo.

In the garden after, Sela found him and wrote on her slate: "You played them all. Did they say anything that mattered?"

He thought about it. "They said they were here," he said. "That's all anyone wants to say."

She considered this a long time, and then she wrote: "Play me." He had not played her yet — the trade was for the dead, and she was not dead. But she had asked him before, in the doorway in the snow, and he had not known how. He looked at the flute, and the flute was warm, and he thought of the monks' small songs, and he wondered if he was beginning to understand.

Chapter 42: The Sister's Rest

She came to him at the evening singing, when the chapel was full of monks and candlelight and the long low hum of the bones — and no one saw her but him. She was not the girl of fourteen who had died of the fever and left her arm in his flute. She was a grown woman of his own age, whole and smiling, with both her arms and all her breath, and she walked out of the choir's song the way a refrain walks out of a melody and sits down beside you.

"You're older," he said.

"I waited for you to catch up," Maren said. "You were twelve when I left. I had to wait a long time for you to be old enough to hear this."

She told him that the truth was told and the name sung, and that her bone had said everything it was carved to say. She told him that Aldric was at rest, and the duke was at rest, and the old king was almost at rest, and that none of them needed anything from him now. "The dead do not need the living to avenge them," she said. "You told the truth. That was the whole of the work."

He wanted to argue. He wanted to say the work was not done, that there would always be another dead person with a song, that the flute was the only way he knew to be useful in the world. She heard all of it in his silence, the way the flute heard the dead.

"You kept me alive in music," she said. "Now keep yourself."

She asked him for one thing: to play the last song, and then to stop. Not a testimony — there was nothing left to testify. A lullaby, the one from his childhood, the one she had taught him before the fever, the one he half-remembered and had never played for anyone.

He begged her to stay. He said her name until his throat closed. She smiled the way she had smiled at him through the window the winter she was dying, and she said, "You don't need the dead anymore, Halden. You never did. You just needed to know you could tell the truth."

She stood, and she walked back into the choir's song, and she did not turn back. The monks' voices took her in like water taking a stone, and for a moment the chapel was full of her, and then it was full only of music, and Halden was alone with the flute and the silence after the true song, which the abbot had said was the only music that cost nothing.

He did not play the lullaby that night. He was not ready. But he carried it, whole and safe, in the hollow of his chest, and he knew — with a knowledge that sat in him like a bone — that it was the last song she would ever ask him for, and that he would have to play it eventually, and that it was not for the dead at all.

Chapter 43: The Flute's Silence

He played it in the morning, in his cell, with the light lying grey on the floor, and nothing came out.

The flute was warm under his fingers. The holes were where his fingers remembered them. He put his breath into it the way he had put breath into it for seventeen years, and the bone gave back only the thin noise of a man breathing into a tube — no melody, no confession, no voice.

He tried again. He tried the way you try a word that has slipped your tongue, gently, then insistently. He played the song of the border pass from memory, and the flute refused it. He played the drowned boy's song, the miller's song, the garden in rain, and the bone was bone again, dumb as a winter field.

The trade was gone.

He knew it before he had finished his second attempt, and the knowledge came into him the way the snow came into the pass that night — not suddenly, but as a settling, a whiteness that took the shape of everything it covered. The one dead who had spoken through him had finished. Maren had said the truth was told, the name sung, her bone had said everything it was carved to say — and she had meant it literally. The flute was not a door anymore. It was a bone again, a keepsake, a piece of his sister that would never speak.

Grief and terror, tangled like threads. Who was he without the dead? He had been the bone man since he was twelve. The living crossed the street from him; children rhymed about him; the dead were his congregation, his family, his only company. Without them he was a man with a flute and no reason to play it, a man who had spent his whole life learning to carry other people's last words and had never once asked what his own might be.

He found Sela in the garden, where the monks' evening sun lay long and yellow on the rows. She watched him a moment, and then she came and put her hand over his on the flute, and he felt how cold his own hand was, and how warm hers.

He told her. "It's silent. She's gone. There's no one left in it."

Sela took her slate and wrote, and held it up. He read it twice, the way he read everything twice now. It said: "Now you play me."

"But you're not dead," he said.

"I am not dead," she wrote, and underlined it. "Then play me. You have been playing the dead for a lifetime. It's time you learned the living."

He looked at the flute. He looked at her. And he put the bone to his lips and played a note — a single, simple note, the first note of the lullaby — and for the first time, a living voice came out of it.

His own.

Chapter 44: The New Life

Weeks passed, and the flute learned to speak for the living.

It was not like the trade. There was no one-playing rule, no testimony, no last word. The bone asked nothing of the dead; the dead were at rest; what came out of the flute now was the world's own music, and Halden found that he had to learn it the way a child learns speech, slowly, with mistakes, with wonder.

He played Sela's heartbeat — he had laid his palm on her wrist to find it, and then he had played it, a small steady rhythm under the melody, and she had closed her eyes and said nothing, and that had been the whole of it. He played the abbot's breath, the long slow tide of an old man asleep. He played the innkeeper's laugh, the blacksmith's hammer, the shape of a room full of people who were not grieving. The monastery's dead still sang in the evening choir, and he could hear them — the monks' old tradition, every death a note in a longer song — but the flute did not need them anymore, and they did not need him.

Sela spoke now, a little.

It had begun at the singing of the bones, on a night when the chapel was full and the candles low. He had been playing the evening tune, and she had opened her mouth as if to sing, and the monks had gone quiet, and her first word had come out rough and small and perfect, and it had been his name. She had written it a hundred times on her slate; she had never said it aloud. When she said it, the chapel held its breath, and Halden felt the word land in him like a stone in still water, and he had played it back to her on the flute, note for note, until she laughed.

They walked the hills now. She carried her slate less; she spoke in broken pieces, like a song being remembered. He played her the lullaby at last — the one from his childhood, the one Maren had taught him, the one she had asked for and that he had carried whole and safe for all these weeks — and Sela listened with her whole body, and when it was done she said, "She would have liked me."

"She would have," Halden said, and it was the easiest truth he had ever told.

For the first time in his life he was not afraid of the morning. The flute was warm in his sleeve, not as a door to the dead but as a voice of his own, and he understood at last what Maren had meant. The dead did not need him to carry them. They were at rest. What the world needed was a man who could play the living, and he was learning, note by note, to be that man.

Chapter 45: The Letter

The rider came up the white-stone road at the tail of the autumn, a royal courier in mud-stained livery, and the monks met him at the gate with no lock and watched him dismount like a man who had ridden a long way to be certain of something.

He asked for Halden by name — the name Halden had left in Valdris, the name that had become a bell the city learned to ring. The abbot received the letter and did not open it, only held it out across the refectory table, and said: "Sealed with the regency's seal. Her own hand, I'd say, and her own seal, which is the same thing."

The letter was Lira's. Halden read it twice, the way he read everything twice now — once for the words, and once for the name on them.

The realm was mended, she wrote. The name was kept. The lie was unmade. She was to be crowned at the winter solstice — the crown that in old Valdrian was called the King, the name passing to a new wearer as it always had, and the wearer being a woman now, and the realm, she said, none the worse for it. The ossuary doors were open to the people. The dead were being allowed their last words in public, one by one, and the city had learned to hold its breath for them, and to let it go.

But the old king was dying. Renn, white and small in the tower where he had chosen to wait, had asked in his last weeks for one thing only: to hear the truth played whole, once, the way it had never been played for him in the hall — the whole song, border and snow and names, played not to a court that wanted it silenced but to the one man who had spent his life silencing it.

She asked Halden to come home and play it.

She wrote, at the end, in the careful exact hand that had once asked a duke the question that undid him: "The kingdom owes you a song. And I think — though I have no right to think it — the king owes you his pardon, and you owe him his peace."

Halden sat with the letter in the evening light and read it a third time, slowly, because the request in it had the weight of a held note, and because he could not decide whether going home was the bravest thing he had ever been asked to do or the simplest. The flute was warm in his sleeve. It had been silent for the dead for months now; it spoke for the living; and it seemed to him that the living had finally asked him for something worth playing.

He folded the letter. He did not answer the abbot's unspoken question. He only said, to Sela, who had come to stand in the doorway: "The kingdom owes me a song."

"Then go," she said, and her voice was small and sure, the way a first word is sure. "And take me with you. I want to hear it."

Chapter 46: The Return

The pass where Aldric died was green.

They came to it on the third day of the ride south, Halden and Sela and a monk the abbot had sent as far as the foothills, and Halden had braced himself for the snow — the snow of every dream, the white withholding, the wind that carried the bass line under the melody. But the pass was green, and hung with white ribbons, and the ribbons moved in the wind like a song being sung by a hundred small mouths.

The new regime had made the truth a shrine. There was a stone at the place where the prince had fallen, with his name on it, and below the name, in letters carved deep, the words the flute had sung: the killer and the author, the blood and the will. Pilgrims had tied the ribbons. Children had left small flutes of reed, and the wind played them, and the sound was not mournful. It was almost a lullaby.

Halden stopped at the stone and played the prince a note — one note, not a playing, for the prince had had his one playing, and his soul was at rest — and the note went out over the green pass and came back to him changed, and he put the flute away, and they rode on.

The capital was not the capital he had fled.

The ossuary doors stood open, and the people moved through them without fear, and the keepers stood at the threshold no longer silent, or at least no longer silent in the old way. The streets were different; his name was spoken without fear; the city had kept it like a bell it had learned to ring, and it rang now, softly, as he passed. A baker knew his face and gave him bread. A child recited the rhyme about the bone man and then laughed, and the rhyme had changed, and the laughter was kind.

Halden was afraid. He admitted it to himself in the street, walking toward the palace with Sela at his side and the flute warm in his sleeve. He was afraid of what he would find in the old king's room — the haunted man, white and small, the guilt sung aloud before his court, the abdication, the tower. He had played the truth once, in the great hall, and the truth had broken a crown. He was asked now to play it again, alone, for the man the truth had broken.

But he was more afraid, he found, of finding nothing — of arriving too late, of the tower room standing empty, of a king who had died before the whole song could be finished, so that the last word he heard from his son's death would always be the censored one.

They gave him a room in the palace. The old king, the chamberlain said, was holding on. He had asked, every morning, whether the flutist had come.

"Then I'll play tonight," Halden said.

Chapter 47: The New King

The coronation was at the winter solstice, in the great hall where he had played the truth, and Halden stood among the freed rather than the condemned.

He had never watched a crown placed. He had watched a crown broken — that was the night of the memorial, when the king's name had hung in the air like smoke and the hall had cracked along its seams. This was different. The hall was the same stone, the same masks on the same faces, the same chair kept for a soul; but the chair was not empty now. It held the dead prince's name, spoken aloud, and the hall spoke it with love, and the snow lay white on the city outside, and no one was withholding anything.

The crown that in old Valdrian was called the King passed, as it always had, from wearer to wearer; the name remained the name, and the wearer was new. Lira wore it. She was small and exact on the throne, and when the crown was set on her head the herald cried the old words, and the hall cried them back, and Halden heard the word King and understood that it meant the crown, and that the crown was hers, and that this was the thing he had helped unmake and now helped make.

After, she came down from the throne to him, in front of everyone, and the hall went quiet the way it had gone quiet on the night of the playing.

"Flutist," she said, and it was not an accusation, as it had once been spoken by half the court. "You played the truth for the hall, and the hall survived it. Now I ask you to play it for one man."

"The old king," he said.

"He is dying in the tower. He asks each morning whether you have come." Lira's exact eyes were steady on him. "I ask you what I think you already know: will you play for him?"

Halden was quiet a moment. "What does he want to hear?"

"The truth," Lira said. "All of it. The way you played it for the hall — but this time, for him. The border, the snow, the name of the killer and the name of the author. And," she said, and her voice changed, small and careful, "anything the flute never told. The part under the music. The part only a living man could play."

Halden looked at the flute in his hands. It was silent for the dead now; it spoke for the living. The old king was not dead yet, and the song he needed was not the dead prince's testimony — that had been sung, and Aldric was at rest. The song Renn needed was the truth played whole, completed by mercy, the way the abbot had said a true song was completed.

"I'll play for him," Halden said. "But I don't know if the flute will speak. It has given up the dead."

"Then play him yourself," Lira said. "I have heard you play the living. It is the same thing, only braver."

Chapter 48: The Pardon

The pardon was read in the square at noon, before he climbed the tower, and the herald's voice carried the words out over the snow-covered stones: that Halden of the salt town, flutist, was cleared of every charge by royal decree; that the truth he had played was the truth the realm kept; that his name, once spoken with fear, was now a name the city would keep.

Halden stood in the crowd and listened to his own name spoken and absolved, and it felt the way it feels to hear a note you have been holding come back resolved. The lie was unmade. The name was lifted. The man who had been half hero, half outlaw, riding north with the bells ringing his name and half the court wanting his tongue, stood in the square where the death of a prince had once been sung and was now, instead, pardoned.

And then he saw his mother.

She was in the front of the crowd, and his father was with her, and they were whole and well, and Halden understood in the same breath that they had been watched over all along — that the old king's guilt had been their guard, that the king's men had kept them safe in the salt town from the moment the truth was sung, because the man who had signed the order to send his son north had been determined, in his broken way, that the flutist's family should not pay for what the flutist had played.

His mother crossed the square and took his face in her hands, the way she had taken his face when he was twelve and Maren was dying, and she looked at him for a long moment and did not speak. Then she reached out and touched the flute, the bone of her dead daughter, and her hand trembled on it, and she asked: "Is she still in it?"

Halden could have lied. The old Halden — the one who had nearly played a false song to save his family — could have said yes, and it would have been a kindness, and the lie would have lived in his mother's heart like a held note that never resolved.

He said: "She is at rest."

His mother wept — the first truth she had let herself hear in years, the first time she had allowed that Maren was gone and not merely waiting, and the weeping was grief, and the grief was clean, the way a true song leaves the singer clean. She held the bone of her daughter, and the bone was warm from her son's breath, and she said, "Then she said everything she needed to say."

"She did," Halden said. "And it was true."

His father said nothing, but he put his hand on Halden's shoulder, and that was enough. The square rang with the pardon. The snow lay white on the roofs, and the sun came out, and the name that had been carried and buried and carried again lay down at last, and it was light.

Chapter 49: The Ossuary, Opened

The old king's room in the tower was small and white, and Renn was smaller than the memory of him. He lay against the pillows like a man who had been hollowed out and filled with snow, the grey hair thin, the hands that had signed the posting gone translucent as bone. But his eyes were open, and they were not haunted. They had the clarity of a man who had stopped running, and he watched Halden come in and set the flute on the table, and he said, in a voice like a dry reed: "You came."

"I came," Halden said.

"I asked each morning," Renn said. "I was afraid you would wait until I was dead, so that the last song you ever played for my son would be the censored one." He closed his eyes a moment. "I have lived with the censored song for years. I did not want to die with it."

Halden understood then what was being asked. Not a playing for the dead — Aldric had had his one playing, and Aldric was at rest, and the flute no longer spoke for the dead at all. This was a song for the living, or the nearly living: the truth played whole, from the beginning, not to a hall that wanted it silenced but to the one man who had spent a lifetime silencing it.

He played.

He played the border pass in winter, the snow, the changed guard, the duke's riders, the face of Moris in the moment of killing — and under it, the bass line the flute had caught that dawn in the ossuary: the hand that signed, the will that posted, the name of the author. He played the whole story, the killer and the author, the blood and the will, the way he had played it in the great hall with the court's masks frozen around him.

And then, because the old king was not dead yet, and because the flute spoke for the living now, he played the part the flute had never told. He played the king's love — the love that had sent Aldric north in the terrible arithmetic of peace, the love that had feared losing the crown to a better man. He played the king's remorse, the night Renn had signed the order and wept, the nights after, the mornings of asking whether the flutist had come. He played the truth completed by mercy, the way the abbot had taught him a true song was completed.

The dead cannot lie, and the living can forgive.

When the music ended, the room was quiet in the way the chapel was quiet after the true song — the silence that costs nothing. Renn lay with his eyes closed, and there were tears on his face, and his hand had found Halden's wrist and held it, and he said: "You played the whole song."

"I did," Halden said.

"Then my son is avenged," Renn said, "and I am forgiven," and he let go, and his breath went out, and he was at peace in the way the dead are at peace, and the song was finished.

Chapter 50: The Final Song

The autumn festival came to Valdris with the leaves turning and the ribbons on the pass still moving, and the ossuary doors stood open to the people the way the truth stood open now — the dead allowed their last words in public, one by one, the lie unmade, the name kept. The city had learned to hold its breath for the dead, and to let it go, and the children who rhymed about the bone man rhymed now without fear, because the bone man was the man who had told the truth, and truth had become a thing a city could keep.

Halden stood in the great hall, before the whole city, with the flute in his hands.

It had been silent for the dead for months. It spoke for the living; it had learned Sela's heartbeat and the abbot's breath and the shape of a room full of people who were not grieving. But on this night, at the autumn festival, he did not play for one person — not for one dead, not for one living. He played for all of them.

He played for Aldric, the prince who had sat with the feared bone man when no one else would. He played for Maren, his sister, whose bone was the flute and whose voice was the truth. He played for the monks of the Still Song and their century of small songs. He played for the drowned boy of the marsh and the miller and the garden in rain. He played for the father who had lost his son to the wave, and the mother who had finally burned her son's clothes, and the old king in the tower, and the duke who had chosen the wrong song and walked into the snow.

The flute, which had been silent for the dead, sang them all at once.

The whole city listened. The hall held its breath the way it had held its breath on the night of the memorial, but this breath was not fear; it was the breath the dead no longer had, given back in music, the breath of a people who had learned to hear the truth and to let it resolve. The melody carried the border and the snow and the names; it carried the lullaby Maren had taught him, and the garden in rain, and the small justice of the dead, and the living voice he had found at last.

When the music ended, there was a silence that costs nothing.

Sela took his hand. The dead were at peace; Halden was alive; the flute was a flute again, and the song was only beginning. Outside, the leaves turned, and the ribbons moved on the green pass, and somewhere a child asked who the bone man was, and her mother said: "He is the one who plays the truth." And Halden, standing in the hall with the whole city listening to the silence, began, softly, the first note of the next song.
