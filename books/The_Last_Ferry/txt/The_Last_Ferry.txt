The Last Ferry
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Crossing

The sun sat low over the west bank, red as a coal. Amos Reed worked the drum with both hands, slow and even, and the ferry came off the far shore like a leaf lifted.

The wagon held a man and a woman and three children. The man's hands were raw from the reins. The woman held a baby against her chest and did not look back at the country they had left.

Amos had seen a thousand wagons. They all came the same way — wheels over the plank, the horses snorting at the water smell, the children hanging off the tailgate. He had never asked one of them a single question. That was the rule, and the rule was the only law he kept.

The cable sang under his grip. Forty years of his hands had worn it smooth in places, and he knew each rough inch the way he knew the seams of his own coat. The drum turned, the rope paid out, and the water slid past dark and patient, tasting of alkali.

"Hold the line," he said. The man took the line like a man who had taken a hundred lines and let them all go.

The river here was wide and slow, brown as cured hide. It did not hurry. It carried what it was given — leaves, drowned wood, the memory of men — and it gave nothing back until it chose.

"Thank you," the woman said at the far bank. Her voice was small against the water.

Amos nodded. He took the fare — a few coins, counted into the tin by feel, the weight of them familiar as breath — and he did not ask where the road would take them, or what they had left behind them, or what they were running from. He never asked.

The wagon climbed the west bank. The children waved. Amos raised one hand, then turned the ferry back to the work of the rope.

He did not know their names. He would not have known them if they had told him. That was the shape of his life, worn like a groove in the drum post — forty years of faces crossing from one side to the other, and Amos the only thing the river let them stand on.

At the east bank he tied off and sat on the edge of the deck, the tin of fares warm in his hand. The sun had gone. On the far bank the family's fire came up, small and yellow, one more life the river had carried and let go.

He thought of nothing. That was how he kept the rest of it down.

Forty years. He ran a thumb along the drum post where the notches ran, smooth as bone, and the river ran past in the dark, keeping its own count.

Chapter 2: Bitter Creek

The water tasted of alkali, and that was how the creek got its name. Bitter Creek. Men said the creek was named for the water. Amos thought it was named for the grief — that the two were the same thing, the way a wound and its scar are the same skin.

The creek came down out of the high country, ran wide and slow through the flats, and laid its brown tongue on the west bank where the free ground began. East of the water lay Bitter Creek Landing, two hundred souls — a church and a saloon and a store and a freight office and Mrs. Marsh's rooming house, all of them set along one road that ended at the water. West of the water lay nothing a man could name. Men went over to be forgotten. Some of them were. Some came back changed, and some did not come back at all.

The creek had taken its share. There were drownings every few years — a drunk man in the spring dark, a boy who could not swim, a farmer who tried the ford at flood and paid the crossing with his life. Amos knew the number. He kept it like the notches, counted and uncounted at once.

The flood of '71 had put the water six feet up the store front and carried the first ferry off her cable. Amos built the new one on the same plans, the same oak, and painted the watermark on the landing post so that nothing would be forgot. The river rose past it twice since. He had watched it each time and remembered what water could do.

Men thought a crossing was a thing you did. It was not. A crossing was a bargain with the river, and the river always took its toll — a wheel, a wagon, a man's nerve, sometimes the whole of it.

The water was the color of a wet rope. It carried the taste of the mountains and the flats and every grave it had ever made a bed over. Amos drank from it like a man drinking from his own past, and it never sweetened.

On the east bank the town went on without him — the store, the freight office, the noise. On the west bank the ground ran free to the horizon and no law followed. Between them, Amos and his cable and his forty years, the only thing the river let stand.

He thought the town east of the water did not know what the water was. The water knew. The water held every crossing, every body, every bargain, and it would hold his too, when his time came, and it would not blink.

That was why he stayed. Someone had to know the river's whole story, even if the river kept it better than any man.

Chapter 3: The Ferry

Her name was nothing. She had never had one. Forty years, and she was just the ferry, the way a man's right hand is just his hand until it fails him.

She was oak, flat-decked, wide enough for a wagon and a team, with a rail on either side a man could brace against. The planks were scarred by a thousand hooves and worn smooth in the middle where feet had crossed, decade on decade, the wood holding the shape of all that passing. Amos knew her by hand the way a man knows his own face — by touch, in the dark, without looking.

The cable ran from the drum on the east bank to a block on the west, and she rode on it, worked by hand. Forty years of turning the drum had put grooves in his palms that did not fill in anymore. He did not mind. The grooves were the tally of his life, and they were honest.

She carried her scars like a soldier. There was a bullet gouge in the deck planking from the war — some soldier had fired at the shore from the water, or a man had fired at a soldier; Amos had never known which, and he had never patched it. It was hers. And there was a charred plank at the stern from the fire, the year the livery burned and a brand had come down the bank and set her edge alight. He had beaten the fire out with his own coat and kept the plank, black and glassy, so the ferry would remember the way he remembered.

He kept her oiled. He paid her back for the years — a plank, a bolt, a length of rope, a coat of tar on her bottom each spring. When he worked on her he talked to her, low, the way a man talks to a horse, and she did not answer, and that was answer enough.

The drum post stood at the east end of her work, and in it the notches — one for every year he had run the crossing. Forty of them, cut close and even, the way a man cuts them when he means to stay. He ran a thumb over the newest one. It had been cut the day before, the start of the forty-first year, and it was still pale as new wood, not yet darkened by the river air.

The river moved under her all day and all night. She rose and fell with it, patient as a held breath, and Amos rose and fell with her, and the two of them had outlived every other ferry on the creek.

He laid a hand flat on the warm oak and felt the river tremble through it, and for a moment he let himself believe that a deck could miss a man, the way he believed a drum post could count.

Chapter 4: The Forty Years

He was twenty-three when he built her, the same year he married Lena, the same spring, so that the ferry and the marriage started together like twins and neither had ever fully outgrown the other.

He had cut the oak himself, green and heavy, and worked it with his father's tools. There had been no ferry at Bitter Creek then. Men forded the creek when it was low and waited when it was not, and the town was smaller than the graveyard it would keep. Amos had seen a woman drown at the ford, her dress caught in the current, and he had decided that day that no one would cross that water without something under their feet.

Lena had laughed at the plan, then had helped him — carrying tar, holding the line, keeping the lamp lit when he worked past dark. She had loved the river too. They had stood on the bank the night the ferry first ran and watched the lantern move across the black water, and Lena had said, "You've given them a floor over the water." He had not known it was a thing a man could give.

The boy came two years later. They named him after Amos's father — Joseph, Joe. Joe grew into the ferry house like a root into a bank. He rode the ferry a hundred times before he could walk, held at the drum in Amos's arms, learning the rope before he learned his letters. The river was his playfield and his nurse and his first friend.

He was six when the river took him, upstream, where the water ran faster, where Amos had warned him a hundred times. Amos had not been there. He had been at the drum, working the crossing, carrying other men's lives, when the boy went under like a stone, and the river kept him.

Lena lasted seven more years. The fever year, they called it. It took a third of the town and it took her, and Amos buried her on the high bank where the creek could not reach, and stood over the grave and understood at last that the river did not love him back. It had only ever kept him busy.

He stayed. That was the whole of it. He stayed because the notches were cut and the cable was his and the town needed a floor over the water, and because going away would have meant the river had beaten him.

Forty years. He was sixty-three. His hands were grooved and his back was tired, and the boy would have been a grown man by now, forty-one, with sons of his own, except that the river kept the young and the drunk and the unlucky, and it had kept Joe the way it kept everything — without asking.

He cut a notch for the forty-first year and went to bed, and in the dark he heard the river running past the ferry house, patient as ever, and he knew it was still waiting for the rest of what he owed it.

Chapter 5: The Rule

The rule had a price on it, and the price was a man's life, and Amos had been paying it ever since.

It was the tenth year, a night in the autumn of his work, before the flood, before the fire. A man had come down the east bank in the dark, wet and shaking, with a story in his mouth and a town at his back. Amos could still see his face, the way a face looks when the law is close — flat, and then too bright, like a lantern turned up.

The man wanted the crossing. Amos asked the one question he should not have asked. He asked what the man had done.

He had not refused for a reason. That was the hard part, the part that never smoothed with time. He refused because the man's coat was new and his hands were soft and his story did not fit him, and Amos had been tired, and had wanted his bed.

The man had not argued. He turned and walked off the landing into the water where the ford ran deep, and the current took him like it took everything — without asking. They found him a mile down at daylight, caught in a snag, his soft hands open.

Amos stood over the body and heard the town talk about the drowned stranger, and no one said his name. No one asked why the ferryman had not carried him.

That night he went down to the water and made his vow to it, the only vow he had ever made that he kept. He would not ask what a man had done. He would not ask where a man was going. He would put his hands to the drum and carry whoever came, and the river could judge the rest, since it judged everything anyway.

Everyone, no questions.

He had kept it for thirty-one years. Drunkards, murderers, women with nothing, men with everything, soldiers, preachers, the dying and the running — he carried them all, and he asked nothing of any of them but the fare.

Even the fare had a shape now. A man who could not pay, Amos did not turn. The fare was owing, not demanded. Some of it sat paid in the tin, some of it owed, the ledger kept only in Amos's head, and the river kept its own ledger, and the two balanced better than the town's books.

He touched the drum post where the notches ran. Somewhere in the water's keeping there was a man in a new coat with soft hands, and the water had never once asked Amos what he had done.

The rule was not mercy. He knew that. It was arithmetic — one refusal, one death, and never again. It was the only equation the river had ever taught him, and he had learned it the way the river teaches everything, by taking what he could not spare.

Chapter 6: The Outlaw

He came west on foot, and the land had taken most of what he had.

Amos saw him first from the ferry house door, a figure small against the water — no horse, no wagon, walking like a man who had walked a long time and did not mean to stop. He came down the east bank and stood at the landing, and the water slid past his boots, and he was young. That was the first thing Amos noticed. Not that he was tired or thin or watching the road behind him — that he was young, and the weight of whatever he carried sat wrong on a young man's shoulders.

"Ferry," the man said. His voice was worn down.

"Aye." Amos put a hand on the drum. "East to west?"

"West."

The man had nothing. Amos could see that at a glance, the way he could see a wagon with a loose wheel or a horse about to founder. No coat worth the name. No bedroll. Empty hands.

"The fare is two bits," Amos said. It was the rule's only other word, and he said it flat, the way he said everything.

The man's hand went to his pocket and came out empty. He stood a moment, then reached inside his shirt and brought out a watch — silver, thin, warm from his skin. He held it out.

"I've nothing else," he said. "I'll come back for it. I'll pay it back."

Amos looked at the watch, then at the man. It was a good watch, the kind a man does not carry unless it was his father's or his own marker of a better time. He took it. It was lighter than it looked, and the case was worn at the stem from years of thumbs.

"Step on," Amos said. That was all.

He worked the drum, and the cable sang, and the water slid past, and the young man stood at the rail with his hands loose and did not look back at the east bank once. He looked west, where the free ground ran out to the horizon, and his face was the face of a man reading his own future in an empty country.

At the far bank he stepped off onto the mud and stood a moment, as if the ground were strange to him.

"I'm obliged," he said. "The watch — I'll send the money. The ferryman of Bitter Creek. I'll find you."

"Everyone," Amos said. "No questions. That's the whole of the rule. You owe the fare, and the fare will come, or it won't. Either way, you crossed."

The young man looked at him a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes, some word he almost said, and then it went down, and he nodded, and turned, and walked west into the ground that swallowed men whole.

Amos watched until the figure was gone into the flat light. Then he turned the ferry back across the water, and at the tin of fares he set the watch in among the coins, where it lay warm as a living thing, holding a man's whole story in the shape of a thumb-worn case.

Chapter 7: The Crossing, Yesterday

He remembered it plank by plank, the way he remembered every crossing he had ever carried, but this one sat different in him, like a stone that did not settle.

The young man had stepped onto the deck like a man stepping onto a floor he did not trust. Amos braced the drum, the cable took the weight, and the water began to slide past the planks, brown and patient, and for a long while neither of them spoke.

"I got a daughter," the young man said finally. He said it to the water, not to Amos. "She's all right now. She's fixed. That's what I'm thinking about, crossing this water. That she's fixed."

Amos said nothing. It was not his place to speak. But he watched the young man's hands, and they were gentle hands — the kind that hold a child, the kind that know the heft of a cup of water. Not the hands of the men who usually crossed at dusk with their eyes on the road behind them.

"She's in Fairhope," the young man said. "With her aunt. She's eight years old and she's fixed, and I ain't ever going to see her grow up. That's the price." He laughed then, a small flat sound. "I don't know why I'm telling you that. You don't know me."

"Everyone," Amos said. "No questions. I don't need to know you to carry you."

The young man nodded, and they worked the crossing in silence after that, the drum turning, the cable singing its one note, and Amos felt the weight of the deck shift under the young man's boots like the ferry itself was learning him.

At the far bank the young man did not step off right away. He stood at the rail and looked west, where the ground went to nothing, and his mouth opened — the thing he almost said, some word that would have been a name or a truth or a question — and then he closed it again, and the word went into the river with the rest of the water's takings.

"Thank you," he said. "For not asking."

"It's the rule," Amos said.

"No," the young man said. "It ain't a rule. It's a kindness, and you wear it like you don't know you've got it on."

He stepped off onto the west bank then and walked away into the flat light, and Amos worked the ferry back across the darkening water with the young man's words hanging over the deck like the smell of the river.

Later, in the ferry house, he stood at the tin of fares and lifted the watch out from among the coins. It was warm from the young man's skin, heavy in his hand. He held it, felt the years of thumbs on the case, and thought of the eight-year-old girl in Fairhope, fixed, and the price, and the word the young man had not said.

The word was still in the river. The river would keep it. The river kept everything.

Chapter 8: The Law

The horse came up the road from Fairhope at dusk, and the town turned to watch it the way a field turns to watch a cloud.

Marshal Eli Boone rode like a man who had been a long time in the saddle and meant to be a long time more. Stiff-backed, straight in the seat, his bad hip held careful and still, the way a man holds a thing that has failed him before. He wore a star on his coat and a face that had given up surprise, and the dust of the road lay on him like a second coat.

He stopped at the store, and the store emptied behind him as he asked his question. He stopped at the freight office, and the freight office emptied. The town carried his name ahead of him the way water carries a smell — Boone, the marshal, law from Fairhope — and by the time he reined up at Mrs. Marsh's rooming house, every porch on the road had a pair of eyes on him.

He asked for the ferryman.

Mrs. Marsh had stood in her doorway with her hands folded, and she had told him the ferryman would be at the landing, and the marshal had turned his horse east along the road that ended at the water, and the town had let him go ahead of them and had followed at a distance with its lamps.

Amos saw him coming down the bank, the star catching the last of the light. He had been coiling the rope, and he went on coiling it, and the marshal came down the slope with the hitch in his step that men get when their bones have outlived their hips, and stopped at the landing with his hand on the post.

"You the ferryman?" Boone said.

"I am."

"Marshal Eli Boone, from Fairhope. They say you're the man who carries everyone across this water."

"That's the rule," Amos said. "Everyone, no questions."

Boone had looked at him a long moment, the way a man looks at a face he means to read. "Then we'll talk," he said. "I've got paper for a man who crossed this water, and I mean to know which side of it he's on."

The drum post stood between them, the notches pale in the failing light, and the river ran past brown and patient, carrying nothing and everything.

Amos did not look at the tin of fares. He did not look at his own coat where the silver watch lay warm. He looked at the marshal's face, and he saw the same weight there he had seen in the young man's — a weight that sat wrong on a man — and he understood, with a cold still certainty, that the crossing he had carried yesterday had been carrying him, and that the river had been saving the reckoning for a day like this one.

"Supper's on the stove," Amos said. "You can talk or you can eat, or both. But the rule is the rule, and the water don't care which one of us is tired."

Chapter 9: Marshal Boone

Eli Boone had been a marshal for thirty years, and he had carried the law the way other men carry water — a little at a time, careful, on level ground. He had never spilled any, and he had never once doubted that carrying it was the whole of a man's work.

He carried a warrant for Cal Vane, and the warrant was clean paper. Bank robbery, Fairhope, three hundred dollars taken, no one hurt — the kind of job a lawman calls cowardly because a coward is easier to write down than a reason. Boone had written it down. He was good at that. He read faces the way a man reads a ledger, adding the columns, looking for the sum that did not balance.

He had read the ferryman's face at the landing and found no sum in it at all — only a worn man and a worn rope and a rule that sat on him like a coat too big. Boone had not trusted it. A rule that plain was either the truth or a door, and he had not yet determined which.

He did not sleep in the saddle. Mrs. Marsh had given him the corner room, and he had lain awake in it with his hip burning and his star on the chair and the warrant folded on the table, and he had thought about his son.

The son was not something he mentioned. He had carried it for eleven years the way he carried his hip — a pain he did not speak of, because speaking of it would have made it a thing to be pitied, and he had never learned to be pitied. The boy had died of a gunshot, nineteen, in a scrape over a game of cards in a town Boone had passed through on duty. Boone had not been there. He had been somewhere else, being the law, and the law had not been able to save his own blood, and that was the arithmetic that did not balance, the one column he could not add.

When he looked at the ferryman's face he looked for the same wound, the way a man who has lost a leg watches other men's knees. He found something there — he was sure of it. Some cost, paid and still paying. It made him slow to trust the plain rule, and slow to distrust it.

He slept badly. The creek ran past his window all night, low and steady, a sound like a rope drawn slowly through water, and he lay in the dark and heard it and thought that a river that big must keep accounts of its own, and that a man who ran a ferry on it for forty years would have heard a hundred confessions and carried a thousand secrets, and that the ferryman would know the name on the warrant, and would not tell it, because the rule was the rule.

Boone had not decided what to do about the rule. That was the whole of his doubt, and it was heavier than the warrant, heavier than the hip. He lay in the dark and held it, and the river held its own, and neither of them let go before dawn.

Chapter 10: The Warrant

The warrant lay on the table between them, and Amos read it slowly, the way a man reads a thing he means to remember.

CALEB VANE. He read the name twice. Robbery of the Fairhope bank. Three hundred dollars. A reward of one hundred offered, and the word COWARD written into the charge, low and deliberate, in the careful hand of the man who had sworn it.

Amos read it with the watch heavy in his coat pocket, and the weight of it was the weight of the young man's hand on the rail, and the weight of the word the young man had almost said, and he kept his face flat the way a ferryman keeps a deck level in a chop.

"Cal Vane," Boone said. "You know the name."

"Everyone who runs the water knows the names the water carries," Amos said. "But I don't ask them. That's the rule."

"The rule." Boone said it like a man testing a plank with his weight. "I'll tell you about the rule, ferryman. This man took three hundred dollars out of a bank with a pistol and no mask, in broad daylight, and he walked out past a teller and two farmers and did not hurt a one of them. Clean. Quiet. The law calls that cowardice, because a man who does not fight is a man who does not want to be remembered."

Amos said nothing. He was looking at the name on the paper. CALEB VANE. The letters were plain and black, and behind them he could see the young man's face in the flat light of the far bank, and the gentle hands, and the daughter, fixed, in Fairhope.

"Three hundred dollars," Amos said.

"It's a lot of money to a bank."

"It's a lot of money to a man with a sick child," Amos said, and the words were out before the rule could stop them, and he felt the silence in the room go deep, the way a pool goes deep before it takes you under.

Boone had gone still. The lamp between them made his face a mask of shadow and light, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had just seen a column in his ledger that did not balance, and did not know yet whether the error was his or the ferryman's.

"You know him," Boone said.

"I know the fare's owing," Amos said, "and I know the watch in my pocket, and I know a rule is a rule, and I've kept it thirty-one years, and the water has kept a man I refused, and I mean to keep it thirty-one more before I answer for the first."

He folded the warrant along its creases and slid it back across the table, and the paper was heavy with the young man's whole story, and the watch was heavy in his coat, and the river ran past the ferry house in the dark, patient, keeping its own accounts.

Boone picked up the warrant and looked at the name a long moment.

"Then we've got a question between us, ferryman," he said. "The law, or the rule. One of them's going to carry this water."

"The water'll carry whichever one of us is lighter," Amos said. "It always has."

Chapter 11: The Demand

The ferry house was one room, and a man could see all of it at once — the lamp, the stove, the bunk, the tin of fares. Tonight it held two men, and the room felt full.

Boone laid his hat on the table. The brim was worn thin as a rope end. He did not sit. A man with a bad hip stands more than he sits.

"You read the paper," he said.

"I read it."

"Cal Vane." Boone said the name like a man testing the weight of it. "Came through here. Four days back, near as I can count from his tracks."

Amos said nothing. The watch sat in his pocket against his hip like a stone.

"Did he cross?" Boone asked.

The question hung like a held breath.

"Everyone crosses," Amos said.

"Everyone."

"That's the rule."

Boone turned the word over. "You take your rules from where, ferryman?"

"From the water." Amos said it flat. "Forty years of it. A man on this bank asks one thing of me, and I give it — the crossing. I don't ask his name, and I don't ask his errand."

The marshal's jaw worked. "A man robbed the Fairhope bank. Three hundred dollars. No one hurt, but there's a warrant, and it's mine to serve."

"That may be."

"So you'll tell me he crossed."

"I'll tell you he crossed," Amos said, "or I'll tell you he didn't. Either way I'd be guessing, because I don't ask, and so I don't know."

Boone's eyes went flat, like the river on a windless morning. "You'd lie for him."

"I wouldn't lie for any man." Amos's voice came quieter than he meant. "That's the whole of it. I don't ask, so I can't lie. What a man brings to the water is between him and the water."

Outside, the river turned in the dark. The lantern on the drum post made a small circle of light on the deck.

Boone stood a long time, working the hip. Then he picked up his hat.

"I'll be at Mrs. Marsh's," he said. "This isn't finished."

Amos nodded. When the door shut he stood at the table, and the lamp made his shadow long across the floor. He took the watch out of his pocket and turned it over — the cheap chain, the worn face, the name scratched faint on the inside of the case. Vane. He had not meant to look at it. He had not meant to keep it out of the tin.

The river ran on, brown and slow, and it did not say whether he had done right.

Chapter 12: The Test

Boone did not leave. That was the test, though no one named it that at first. He stayed at Mrs. Marsh's rooming house, and each morning he came down to the landing and stood on the bank with his hat on and his hands folded, watching the water the way a man watches a trail he means to come back to.

The town watched him watch.

Brant came first, down from the store with his apron still tied, standing beside Boone like he had business at the water's edge.

"You got a warrant," Brant said.

"I got a warrant."

"Three hundred dollars, that's what the paper says. A reward on top, I hear."

Boone did not answer.

"A man in your position," Brant said — "a man with a warrant and a bad hip and a long way back to Fairhope — he might take a partner in the doing of it."

Boone turned his head slow, the way a bull turns in a field. "You offering?"

"I'm offering to be helpful."

"Helpful." Boone said the word like it had a smell. "I carry the law. The law don't take partners in a cut of reward money. The law takes the man. Go on back to your store."

Brant went, and the door shut, and the town watched from behind the glass.

On the ferry, Amos worked. He worked the cable and the drum, and the deck moved over the brown water like a slow patient animal. He ferried a wagon of salt pork. He ferried a preacher with a satchel. He ferried two boys with a fishing pole and a dog. He dropped their fares into the tin and did not ask where they were going.

But he saw Boone on the bank, and the doubt came up in him slow, the way water rises in a dry creek bed, from a place he did not want to look at. He had taken a watch from a man with no coin. He had kept it out of the tin. A fare is a fare and a rule is a rule, and he had not asked the man's name.

But he had turned the watch over in his hand. He had read the name on the inside of the case.

That was not the rule. That was something else, and it sat in his chest the way a pebble sits in a boot — small, certain, in the way.

At dusk Boone came down to the landing and stood on the deck without a word. Amos watched him a moment, then turned the drum, and the boat took the marshal across in silence, the water dark under them both.

Chapter 13: The Robbery

They told it in the store, and they told it in the saloon, and by the third telling the robbery had grown teeth and a beard. Amos heard it all, standing by the counter for lamp oil, and underneath the telling was the same bare bones.

Fairhope had one bank. Stone front, iron door, a vault the size of a cell. The teller was a man named Oakes, thin as a rail and soft-spoken, and he had seen the stranger come in at the end of the day, when the light went long and the counting began.

Quiet. That was the word every telling used. Quiet, clean, no one hurt. The stranger waited his turn like a farmer at the mill. He handed Oakes a note, written in a careful hand, and Oakes read it and his hands went cold, and he did what the note said.

No pistol drawn. No shouting. The stranger walked out the front door with three hundred dollars in a canvas sack, and he did not hurry, and by the time the alarm went out, the man was gone west.

Strange, the town said. Strange for an outlaw.

A man who robs a bank ought to shoot the ceiling and kick the teller. This one left nothing. Oakes counted the chairs, the dust, the empty sack. No mess at all.

"They'll call it cowardice," Boone said, coming through the door of the store. "A quiet job. No nerve. A man who can't even make a loud crime."

Amos turned from the counter. "That how you read it, Marshal?"

"I read the paper," Boone said. "The paper says robbery. The reward says three hundred. The rest of it — the quiet of it — that's a man's business, and I don't weigh it."

Amos paid for the oil and walked out onto the landing. The river ran past, brown and indifferent, and he thought about a man who robbed a bank with a note and no pistol and no hurry, and he thought about the way a man walks when the money is already spent in his head before he takes it — because he has to take it.

He did not say that. He took his oil down to the ferry house, and the water closed over the sound of the telling behind him.

Chapter 14: The Bank

The vault was not much of a vault, Boone said. It was a room behind the teller's cage, walled in iron, with a door the bank trusted more than any man trusted his brother. The door had a wheel on it, like a ship's, and it groaned when it turned, and the sound carried through the building like a held breath.

"They heard the door," Boone said. He sat on the ferry house step, hat in his hands, the bad hip eased against the post. "Oakes heard it. That's how the taking went. The note got read, and Oakes emptied the cage into a sack."

"Oakes," Amos said.

"Teller. Fifteen years at the same window. Green eyeshade, a pen he cleaned after every line."

Amos nodded. He had seen such men — a whole life behind a pane of glass, counting money that was never theirs.

"The law calls it cowardice," Boone said. "A man who takes money with a note is a man who won't fight for his thieving. He slides in quiet and slides out quiet, and he leaves the honest man to wonder if it happened at all."

Amos turned this over. "The vault?"

"Never opened. The safe held more than three hundred, but he took what was in the cage and not a dollar more. The counted money, the day's money. He left the locked money where it lay."

Boone said it, and then he went quiet, because both of them heard what it sounded like. A man who takes exactly what he needs, and no more, is not a man who hates the bank. He is a man who needs.

Boone worked the hip, stood, looked out at the water. "That's the trouble," he said. "The quiet. The exactness. A loud crime, a messy crime — that a lawman can hate clean. This one I could almost see the man."

Amos said nothing. The watch lay in his pocket, and the name inside the case was Vane, and he had crossed that man four days back, and the man had been gentle.

The river ran past the landing, brown and slow.

"If I could almost see him," Boone said, "then he was a man like any other. And if he was a man like any other, he'll come to a man's end. There's no crossing the law." He looked at Amos. "You tell me, ferryman. Is there?"

Amos looked down at his hands, worn as old rope. "The water crosses everything," he said. "The law, the ferryman, the man with the note. It crosses them all, and it doesn't ask."

Chapter 15: The Daughter

The word came up from Cole's freight office, where a rider from Fairhope had watered and talked, and from the rider's mouth it went to the store and the livery and the rooming house before the day was out.

Mrs. Marsh brought it down to the ferry house in the evening, a shawl over her shoulders, and she stood at the door until Amos looked up from the tin of fares.

"There's a story going round," she said. "From Fairhope. About the man who took the bank."

Amos set the tin down. "I've heard the robbery."

"Not the robbery. The man." She folded her hands the way she did when she carried something heavy. "He's got a daughter. Eight years old. Ruthie."

The lamp made a small circle on the table. Amos said nothing.

"The child was sick," Mrs. Marsh said. "A growth in her neck. It was growing, and the Fairhope doctor said there was a surgeon in the city could take it out, and the price of the taking was three hundred dollars."

Amos heard the number, and it went into him like a nail into green oak. Three hundred.

"The man worked honest," she said. "Farm work when there was farm work. Horses when there was a horse to be had. The mine when the mine was open. All of it slow. The doctor said the child's time was short, and the honest money came too slow to buy the time."

She stopped, and the room was quiet, and the river ran past outside in the dark.

"They say he was a quiet man," she said. "Gentle. The kind who holds a door and thanks a cook. And one day he went to the bank with a piece of paper and a canvas sack, and he took the three hundred, and he walked out the front door and did not hurry."

Amos stood at the table a long time. The watch was in his pocket.

"That's the arithmetic of it," he said at last. "A child's life at three hundred dollars, and a man's honest years at nothing a week. The robbery was the only sum that came out even."

Mrs. Marsh looked at him. "You sound like you pity him."

"I don't pity any man," Amos said. "I ferry them. The water don't ask the price of their crossing."

After she left he stood in the door of the ferry house and looked out at the river, and he thought of the girl, healed now, wherever she was, breathing and growing in a way that had been bought with a note and a canvas sack. And he thought of the man who had walked out of the bank without hurrying, and he knew then, the way a man knows a thing in his chest before he can say it, that he had crossed that man and taken his watch and let him go to the free ground.

The river ran on, brown and slow. It did not judge. It only held.

Chapter 16: The Surgery

The letter came on the third day, folded small, in a hand that was careful and slow — a woman's hand, Mrs. Marsh said, though the writing was plain as a fence.

She read it to Amos in the ferry house, by the lamp, because she said a thing read alone is a thing half believed.

"The operation was the week before the robbery," she read. "They took the growth out of the child's neck in the city hospital, and the surgeon was a small man with clean hands, and he did the work while the child slept, and he said it was done in time."

Mrs. Marsh looked up. "Done in time. Those are the words she used. Done in time."

Amos nodded. The lamp flame stood still as a held breath.

"The money was owed," Mrs. Marsh read on. "The surgeon's price, the room, the nursing. The child's mother scraped and saved. The father worked every honest hour the country would sell him, and it was not enough. The surgeon would not wait. The child could not wait. And the week after the surgery, when the bill came due and the money was not there, the man went to the bank."

The room was quiet. Outside, the river ran, and the sound of it filled the small dark.

"He took exactly what was owed," she said. "The bank men counted it, and it matched the bill to the dollar. Three hundred. Not a dollar more, not a dollar less. And he paid the debt, and then he walked west to the river and did not come back."

Amos stood at the stove, the heat on his face, and he did not speak for a long time.

"So the money was spent," he said at last. "Spent before I ever saw the man. The watch he gave me was all he had left in the world."

Mrs. Marsh folded the letter back into its creases. "The town will hear it," she said. "Brant will say the child is an excuse. Cole will say a crime is a crime. But some of them will hear it, and some will weigh it, and the weighing won't come out clean on either side."

Amos looked out the door at the water. It was dark, and the lantern on the drum post made a small light on the deck, and the river carried the light away from him in a long, broken ribbon.

"That's the way of it," he said. "A man can do a clean thing for a dirty reason, or a dirty thing for a clean one. The water don't split the difference. It carries both, and remembers both, and forgives neither."

Mrs. Marsh left the letter on the table. It lay beside the tin of fares, a small white thing in the lamplight, and Amos did not move it.

Chapter 17: The Spent Money

The trail west of the river was cold, and Boone said so plainly, the way he said everything plainly, as if a word with a bend in it carried a lie.

He came down to the landing at dusk, when the light went the color of old iron, and he stood at the water's edge and watched the current.

"I walked the far bank two days," he said. "The ground's soft. A man leaves tracks in soft ground if he means to, and this man left a few, and then he left none. The trail don't end so much as it stops caring."

Amos worked the rope through his hands, slow, a habit when he had nothing to carry. "Free ground."

"Free ground." Boone said the words like a man testing a plank before he trusts his weight to it. "A hundred miles of it, and no law on any of it, and a man could lose himself in it the way a stone loses itself in a river."

The light went down. The river between them ran brown and slow.

"They found the sack," Boone said. "The canvas sack, in a ditch by the road, with the cents in the bottom of it and the dollars gone. The dollars went into the child's body. That's what the doctor's paper says, and the paper's honest."

Boone stopped, and the quiet sat between them like a third man.

"The warrant says three hundred," he said. "A man who stole three hundred dollars. But the money's spent, every dollar of it, and the child is well, and the man is on free ground, and I've got a paper that says I'm to bring him back."

He looked out at the water. "Thirty years I've served the law. I never once doubted the paper. Now I hold a paper that names a crime, and the crime is spent, and what's left of it is a healed child I will never see."

Amos said nothing. The watch was in his pocket. He had taken to carrying it the way a man carries a load he doesn't want to set down.

"You doubt the rule, don't you," Boone said.

Amos looked at him across the dark water. "I doubt that I took his watch. The fare was owed, and he had no coin, and the watch was the fare, and I took it. But I kept it out of the tin, and I turned it over in my hand, and I read the name inside the case."

"That's not the rule," Boone said. "That's the size of a man's attention."

"Maybe," Amos said. "Or maybe the rule was never meant to be carried in a pocket."

The river ran between them, brown and slow, carrying the light away. They stood on opposite banks in the dark — one with a name on his paper, one with a rule on his conscience — and the water held them apart like a scale held level.

Chapter 18: The Town

The town assembled the way towns do — not by any plan, but by the pull of a shared weight. By the second week they had settled into their positions like water finding its level.

At the store, the talk ran to the reward. Brant kept the handbill in the drawer under the cash and took it out the way a man takes out a thing to feel its heft. Three hundred dollars. A reward on top. The ferryman knew which way the man had gone, and the ferryman was not saying.

At the livery, the talk ran to the ferry. The old man ran the cable for anybody. Everybody, no questions. A rule like that had a shine when the river was quiet and a stink when the river carried a warrant.

At the saloon, the talk ran to the outlaw. A man who took a bank with a piece of paper — the men at the saloon did not admire it, but they did not exactly condemn it. They had all, at one time or another, wished for money they had not earned. Most had settled for the wishing.

At the church, the talk ran quiet, and it ran to the widows.

Because the widows would not speak against the man. That was the strange part, and the town chewed it like a splinter. Mrs. Hennessy, widow Bickley, old Mrs. Pearsall — they would not say a word against the man who took the bank. They would not even say his name. They pressed their lips together and looked at the floor, and the more the town pushed, the more they would not.

"One body," the mayor said to the livery man, "with two minds in it. And the ferryman at the hinge of it, holding the halves apart."

The ferryman did not attend the meeting. He worked the cable through the day, and at evening he sat in the ferry house with the lamp low, and the town's talk came to him only as rumor — the way sound comes across water, bent and slow, not quite what was said.

But he heard enough. He heard that Brant had called him a fool in the store. He heard that Cole had called the rule a shirker's law. And he heard that the widows had gone silent, and that the silence was a kind of speaking, and that the town had felt the silence like a hand on its shoulder.

That night he sat on the deck of the ferry, alone, the lantern making a small circle on the planks, and the river ran past, brown and slow, and he listened to it the way a man listens to a thing he trusts more than he trusts men.

The town could split. The town could talk. The water would still be here in the morning, and it would not have changed its mind about anything.

Chapter 19: The Merchants

The delegation came down to the ferry house in the middle of the afternoon, when the sun lay flat on the water and the ferry sat empty on the far bank. Brant and Cole, and behind them a boy from the livery who carried a crate and looked unhappy about it.

Amos saw them coming from the drum post. He kept his hands on the rope. He did not stop working, and he did not meet them at the door. A man who meets a delegation at the door has already agreed to something.

Brant came first, as he always came first, the apron traded for a coat. "Ferryman," he said.

"Mr. Brant."

"We've come about the outlaw."

"There's no outlaw on my deck," Amos said.

"The one that crossed four days back," Cole said. Cole was a thin man, and his words came thin, like a penny shaved at the edges. "The one you took a watch from. We heard about the watch."

Amos went still.

"The reward is the town's money," Brant said. "Three hundred dollars out of a bank that lends to this town. Half the goods in my store are bought with Fairhope money. The bank's loss is my loss, and Cole's loss, and the town's loss. The man that took it —"

"Paid his fare," Amos said.

"Paid his fare." Brant laughed, short and dry. "You took a watch off a thief and let him walk onto free ground with the town's money in his pockets. That's not a fare. That's a partnership."

Amos looked at him across the deck, and his face did not move. "He had a sick child."

"We've all heard the child," Cole said. "A child's a good coat for a thief to wear. You're the only man alive who knows which way he went. Tell the marshal. And the reward would cover a man's trouble handsomely."

The boy shuffled. The river ran past, brown and slow.

Amos turned the rope once around the drum. "Here's what I'll tell you. I don't know which way he went. I don't ask. I never asked a man's direction, and I'm not starting with this one. If the marshal wants to know, the marshal can ask the water, same as me. The water's the only witness that don't lie."

Brant's mouth made a hard line. "You're a fool, ferryman."

"I've been called worse," Amos said, "by better men. Now the drum's mine till dark, and the far bank's got freight, and I've got work."

They stood a moment, the two merchants and the boy. Then Cole turned, and Brant followed. Halfway up the bank Brant stopped.

"The town will remember this," he said.

Amos watched them go. Then he turned the drum, and the boat moved out onto the water, and the water closed over the sound of their boots.

He worked the cable the rest of the day without stopping, and when the light went, he stood at the drum post and ran his hand over the notch he had cut forty years ago — worn smooth by his own hands.

One rule, one man. And the town on the bank behind him, splitting in two.

Chapter 20: The Widows

The gift came out the way such things come out — not all at once, but in pieces, and each piece a small shame or a small mercy, depending on whose mouth it passed through.

It had been months before the robbery. The man who would take the Fairhope bank had come to Bitter Creek Landing first, the way a river comes to a bend — slow, and then all at once. He had stayed at Mrs. Marsh's, and he had worked odd jobs, and he had been quiet, and then one day he had gone. No one had thought much of it.

But while he was here, he had given.

Mrs. Hennessy's roof had leaked that spring — a slow, patient leak that rotted a rafter and then a joist. The man had seen her looking up at the stains, and the next morning he was on her roof with a saw and a ladder, and he refused pay, and he left her a dry house and a stack of spare shingles in the shed.

Widow Bickley's rent had fallen three months behind. The man had settled the debt with Cole out of his own pocket, coin counted onto the counter like a man counting out his own blood, and he had told Bickley the landlord had forgiven the old month. He had not said it was him.

Old Mrs. Pearsall's stove had cracked along its back, so the fire drew wrong and the smoke came into the room, and a new stove was out of reach of her pension. The man bought her one from Brant's store and had it hauled to her door.

It was Brant who put the pieces together, on the steps of the church after Sunday meeting, loud enough for the whole town to hear.

"The man who robbed the bank," he said, "is the same man who patched Hennessy's roof and paid Bickley's rent and bought Pearsall her stove. A bank thief, and there's women in this town carrying his charity."

He meant it as a rope to hang the man by. He did not see how it would read.

Because the widows would not speak against him. Mrs. Hennessy looked at her shoes. Widow Bickley pressed her lips. Old Mrs. Pearsall, who had said little in twenty years, stood up slow as a tree and looked Brant in the face.

"He fixed my roof," Hennessy said.

"He paid my rent," Bickley said.

"He bought my stove," Pearsall said. Then the old woman added, in her dry voice, "And he never asked a one of us for anything but our quiet. We gave him our quiet, and we'll keep giving it."

The steps went silent, and the town felt the silence like a held breath.

That night Amos stood at the drum post and turned the watch over in his hand, and the river ran past, brown and slow, and he understood then what the rule was worth — not in the keeping of it, but in the way it ran through a man like a current and touched what it touched.

The widows' quiet was a kind of rule, too. And the river held it, the way it held everything, and did not let it go.

Chapter 21: The Gift

It had been March when the money came, and it had come quiet, the way all real gifts do.

Amos remembered the month because the ice had gone off the river that week and the drum was singing again for the first time since January. A man crossed west to east in the last of the daylight, paying his fare in silver that was too new, and left nothing in the tin but the crossing. That same evening Mrs. Hennessy's roof was whole again, and nobody could say who had paid for the shingles.

Now the town knew. Brant knew. A secret is only a secret until it does good; then it becomes a story.

Mrs. Hennessy's roof — twenty-one years of leaks, patched like a fisherman's mending — had been replaced board for board. Widow Bickley's rent had been paid a year in advance, to Cole himself, and Cole kept the man's name out of it because the man had asked. The church stove, cracked these ten winters, had come home from the smith new-fired, and the church women said a prayer over it that named no one.

Three gifts. A roof, a rent, a stove. The widows kept a ledger, and in it the three gifts were one line: what was given, when, and to whom. They did not write the giver's name. They wrote the shape of the giving — quiet, months before the robbery, before the warrant, before the town knew a man named Cal Vane had ever crossed the water.

Amos heard it all from Brant's own mouth, at the drum, his hand on the worn rope. Brant could smell a dollar through a ledger. The widows' gratitude had gone public, and public gratitude is a coin Brant had never been able to spend, and it galled him.

"Three gifts," Brant said, "from a bank robber. What does that make them, Reed? Receivers?"

Amos did not answer. He let the question lie in the water where Brant had thrown it. The river took it downstream, the way it took everything — the way it would take the truth, if the town let it float.

But he remembered the month, and the man, and the silver that was too new. He remembered how the man had stood at the gunwale as they crossed, looking west, and how gentle his voice was when he asked after the Landing — whether it was a kind town, whether a body could be forgotten here. Amos had answered the way he answered everyone. No questions.

Now the questions came back upstream, from the town's own mouth, and they came for the widows' gratitude like a debt collector calling.

Mrs. Hennessy's roof held. Widow Bickley's rooms were warm. The stove would fire all winter and fire again. Gratitude did not care who the law thought the giver was. It only cared that the giving happened.

And Amos, at the drum, felt suspicion and gratitude at war in his chest like two currents meeting at a sandbar, and knew the river had seen this fight before — on the same water, in the same town, in the same kind of year.

Chapter 22: The Split

By the second week the town had two mouths, and both of them spoke.

The church porch had the widows and the women who had taken the stove's warmth, and they said what had been given was given, and a man who gave as that man gave did not rob to break a child's life. The store porch had Brant and Cole and the livery, and they said a bank robber is a bank robber, and three hundred dollars is three hundred dollars, and gratitude was the rope a clever thief used to keep a town quiet.

The two porches faced each other across the single street of Bitter Creek Landing, and the street between them filled with the weather of the argument. It rained. It dried. The church women carried bread to the ferry house, and the merchants measured the ferryman's silence against the reward.

The mayor, Holbrook, small and balding, walked the street each evening with his hands clasped behind him, weighing one porch against the other the way a man weighs two sacks of meal and finds neither full. He told the church porch the law would do its work. He told the store porch the law would do its work. The law, waiting in a rooming-house window above them both, did not tell him anything.

Amos did not go to either porch. The ferry was the only ground that belonged to no side. He worked the cable, and the cable did not care which porch had the better of the day, and the planks did not care who crossed them, and that was the rule and the rule held.

But the town watched him cross it now. Every boat that went out, the eyes on the bank counted who he carried and what he asked — and he asked nothing, carried all, and the two porches read his silence each for its own side. The church porch read it as mercy. The store porch read it as judgment. Both were wrong, and both were partly right, and Amos let the water answer them, because the water answered nothing.

Boone watched from the window of Mrs. Marsh's rooming house, where the whole street ran like a slow river between its banks. He had the warrant in his coat. He had the doubt in his chest. He watched the porches choose their sides and the ferryman move between them, and he thought how a law, once walked into a town, divides it like water dividing around a rock — and how the ferryman was the rock, and the current ran past him, and the current always won in the end.

The two porches stood. The mayor wavered. The ferry turned in the water, the only thing that touched both banks.

And in the window, Boone held the paper in the lamplight and did not once look away from the man who would not choose.

Chapter 23: The Weather

The rain came on a Tuesday and did not stop for three days.

It came straight down, then sideways, then in the slow gray steady way that means the ground has given up drinking. The street turned to mud up to a man's boot-top. The gutters ran brown, and the Landing's few dogs stood under the store porch and watched the water with the flat eyes of animals that know.

Amos knew the smell. It was in the air before the river showed it — old mud and cold iron and something like turned earth, the breath of the bottom land waking up. He had smelled it nine times in forty years, and nine times it had been right, and he trusted the smell alone.

The river rose. Not fast — the river never did anything fast — but steadily, a hand's breadth a day, then a foot, then more. The watermarks on the bank went under one by one, and Amos read them going the way a man reads a familiar face losing its lines: the mark of '71, the mark of '66, the high-water mark his own father had cut into the east-bank oak before Amos was born.

He checked the cable twice a day, morning and evening, and the cable was his other hand, the long muscle of the crossing, and it sang a different note when the river pulled at it. He ran his palm along its whole length the way a man runs a hand along a rope he has trusted for forty years, feeling for the thin place, the failed strand, the rust that lied. The cable was sound. The drum was sound. The ferry sat at her mooring and took the waves the way she had taken them every year — and still he checked, twice, because the water was coming and the water did not warn.

At dusk of the third day the rain held, and the river had the smell in it now, the bottom of the flood, and the town stood along the bank in a ragged line and watched the water rise against the ferry house pilings.

Amos checked the cable a third time, against his own rule, and the third time he felt the drum post shiver.

Not the cable. The water. The water was moving the earth.

He stood with his hand on the rope while the lantern guttered on the drum, and he did not look at the town, and he did not look at the river, and he only held the rope, and the rope held the cable, and the cable held the boat, and the boat held the water, and the water held everything else.

Chapter 24: The Flood

The bottom land went under on the fourth morning, and the crossing became the town.

The river took the low fields first, then the road, then the yards of the two or three houses that sat low enough to drink. Families came in with their stock and their beds and their histories, and the ferry worked as it had not worked since the flood year of '71, when Amos was twenty-three and new to the rope. All day and all night the drum turned. People, then stock, then goods, then more people — cows on the deck with their eyes white, a coffin lashed flat, a bed with a woman in it who had not walked since winter.

Amos worked the cable until his hands forgot their own shape. He did not count the crossings. The crossings counted themselves, in the notches of wear on the rope, in the way the boat sat lower with each load, in the faces that came and went and came again, east and west, none of them asking why the water had come.

The river changed color on the second day — from the slow brown of the summer creek to red-brown, the color of turned earth, and then to something grayer, the color of old iron, the color of the flood. It smelled of uprooted things, of torn roots and drowned fields, of the whole watershed coming down to the crossing at once. The water was no longer a road. It was a moving country, and the ferry was the only ford in it.

Boone took the rope on the second night.

Amos did not ask him to. The marshal had come down to the landing in the dark, hip stiff, coat soaked, and stood watching the drum until the ferry came in empty, and then he took the rope from the ferryman's hands and hauled, and the boat went out again into the moving dark with a load of frightened cattle and a boy crying. Boone worked the rope like a man who needed the work — who needed his hands doing a thing with a clear purpose, a thing the law could not complicate. The cable sang between them, and the river, for once, had more to carry than either man's doubt.

Toward dawn the water took the ferry house steps, and the boat came in with the last load of the night — a widow and her stove, the stove new-fired the spring before, and the widow's ledger in her lap.

Amos looked at the stove and the ledger, and then at the marshal, who had set down the rope and was standing with his hands raw and empty, and he thought: the town is on the water now. Both of its mouths. Both of its minds.

The river rose. The cable sang. The drum turned.

And the color of the water was the color of everything the town had ever carried, coming down to the crossing at once.

Chapter 25: The Rising

The water climbed past the watermark of '71 on the fifth day, and the town made its last evacuation.

Amos watched the mark go under — the deep cut in the east-bank oak, forty years of the river's worst, and the river was taking it back, careless, the way it took everything it had ever given. The ferry house was a foot under. The drum stood in water, the cable a black line through the moving brown, and the boat rode at her mooring like a thing that knew she might not be coming back.

The evacuation was not a thing the town decided. It was a thing the water decided. The bottom land was gone. The street was a channel. The church stood with water around its porch and the store with water around its steps, and the two porches, for the first time in the flood, looked the same — both drowning, both equally under. The mayor was the first to go, and after him the livery, and after him, in a slow line of carts and children and stock, the rest.

The ferry nearly lost, twice.

The first time, a whole oak came down the channel, roots and all, traveling the flood like a dark animal, and struck the boat at the bow while Amos had her outbound with the church's women and the stove. The oak rode up the deck, grinding, and the boat heeled, and for a long moment the river had her, and then the oak passed, and she came level, and Amos brought her in with the stove still lashed and the women still praying.

The second time, the cable sang a note he had never heard in forty years, high and wrong, the note of the whole flood pulling at the line, and Amos cut the drum loose from its post, ran the boat free, and brought her around on the current, steering by the river's own push, and tied her off on the top of the bank where the ferry house had been. She had nearly gone under. She had held.

The last crossing came at night, by lantern, with the water at the store counter. Amos carried the last of the town east — the widows' ledger and the stove, two of the church women, and Brant, who came aboard with his ledgers and his counting and did not once look at the water. And then, when the deck was empty and the lantern guttered, the marshal stood on the bank with his hip in his hand and his coat streaming, and did not come aboard.

The crossing of the crossing.

"Last boat," Amos said. "East. Or you stay on the water side of it."

Boone looked at the boat, and at the water, and at the ferryman, and behind them the Landing had gone dark and low, the flood at its knees, the street a river, the two porches drowned and equal.

"I'm not east," Boone said.

And the river rose, and the cable sang, and Amos held the rope while the marshal made his choice — the way the river held everything, patient, taking its time, taking the whole town downstream to somewhere it would all have to be sorted out again.

Chapter 26: The Last Crossing

Word came on the sixth morning, carried by a man who had come up the channel in a skiff, wet to the waist: the east bridge upstream had gone down in the night.

Amos heard it and said nothing. The bridge was the only other way across for a hundred miles — the iron span at Gray's Ford, the crossing the town used when the water was too high for the ferry. Now it was gone, under the flood, carried off like a length of rope, and Bitter Creek Crossing was the only crossing left on the whole bend of the river. The ferry was the only door between east and west, and the water had closed every other one.

Amos worked her that day the way he had worked her every day of the flood, load after load, the east bank giving up its last people to the west, or the west its last to the east — it did not matter which way. The water did not care. The boat did not care. The rule did not care. He carried a family with a sick child. He carried the smith and his anvil. He carried a woman with a coffin, small, child-sized, lashed to the deck, and he did not ask whose, and the water took it downstream past him without comment.

At dusk the rain held, and the river had stopped rising.

Amos felt it in the rope — the pull easing, the flood's grip letting go its worst, the water settling into the slow terrible weight that would take days to fall. He stood on the bank in the gray light, the lantern lit on the drum, and counted what was left on the east bank: the ferry house gone, the store half under, the church dark — and Boone, standing alone on the landing, hip in his hand, the warrant in his coat, waiting.

Amos called the last crossing.

He called it the way he had called it a thousand evenings, the way he would call it when the water was down and the town rebuilt and the years turned — his voice carrying over the moving water, flat and plain, the way a man calls a thing that is just a thing.

"Last crossing. West bank, or turn back."

The water ran. The cable sang. Boone stood on the landing and looked at the ferry and at the water and at the ferryman, and the river, which had carried the whole argument downstream all these days, held its breath — if a river can hold its breath — and waited to see which way the law would go.

Amos held the rope. The drum turned slow in the current. The lantern on the drum burned.

And Boone, in the falling dark, walked down to the boat.

Chapter 27: The Boat

She was awash when he came to her, and his fear for her was a thing he had not named in forty years.

Amos had never been afraid of the water. He had been afraid of the boat — not of losing her, but of the years in her, of the forty years of plank and oak that would go down with her, of the weight of crossings she had carried. The boat was his ledger, his other book, and every scar in her was a page.

He knew her by heart. He knew the plank forward, replaced in '74, a hand's breadth of new oak gone gray with the years; the charred plank from the fire of '61, burned almost through before he doused it, the black grain of it like a bruise in the wood; the bullet gouge from the war, the long splintered furrow where a shot had come down from the east bank and the wood had caught it, the way a body catches a blow, and kept the crossing whole. He knew the worn place where forty years of feet had climbed aboard, the smooth hollow of it, the shape of the town's boot-heels worn into the grain like a map of who had crossed and when.

She was awash now. The flood ran over her deck a hand deep, the water coming and going across the planks with each push of the current, and she rode heavy, low, the way she had never ridden in all the years he had worked her. The cable sang a note he had not heard since the flood of '71, the high taut note of the whole river pulling, and the drum turned against the current, and the lantern on the drum burned low against the dark.

Amos put his hand on the gunwale and stood with her a long moment, the way a man stands with an old horse, feeling for what is sound and what is spent.

He ran his palm along the planks, one by one, the ones he knew by heart — the charred plank, the gouged plank, the replaced plank, the worn hollow at the boarding place — and the boat took his hand the way she always had, silent, level, ready. She was more than timber. She was the crossing itself, the whole of it, forty years of rope and drum and the town's weight on her back, and the flood had her now, and his fear was that the river would take her before the last crossing was done.

The deck was awash. The cable sang. The boat held.

And Amos, standing on her, in the dark, with the marshal waiting on the bank behind him and the river running past — Amos Reed felt the boat move under his feet the way she had moved under his feet for forty years, and he knew, the way a man knows the hand of an old friend in the dark, that she would carry them.

Chapter 28: The River

The river had seen it all, and the river kept it all, and the river would not say.

Amos stood on the bank at dusk while the boat rode at her mooring, and the water ran past him carrying the whole of the story downstream where it could not be read. The river knew the boy first — the boy at six, the boy who had gone down the bank on an afternoon like any afternoon and had been taken up by the current and kept, kept for twenty years now in the river's patient dark. The river had not given him back. The river did not give things back.

It knew the drowned. Amos had pulled them out, over the years, or watched the town pull them — the ones who had gone in drunk, the ones who had gone in for good, the farmer of '63, the girl of '77, the two who had gone in together and been found together a mile down. The river held them the way it held everything, in the slow moving dark, and the mud of the bottom kept their stories, and the water forgot nothing and forgave nothing.

It knew this story. It had seen the man come west on foot, trail-worn, no coin, with a watch for the fare and a daughter's name he did not speak. It had seen the marshal ride in from Fairhope with a warrant and a bad hip and a dead son he did not mention. It had seen the town divide, the porches choose, the flood take the bottom land and the bridge and the ferry house. It had seen the warrant in the marshal's coat and the watch in the ferryman's tin, and it was the only witness to both sides of the whole thing, and it was not talking.

Amos knew the river did not judge the way a man judged. It did not weigh the robbery against the child, the rule against the law, the gift against the taking. It held them all, in the same water, at the same depth, the way it held the boy and the drowned and the years — all of it down there together, none of it separable, none of it ever coming up again the way it went down.

The river was the judge the town had never had. Not because it would decide. Because it would keep the evidence forever, and never say which side was right, and never let either side be forgotten.

The water ran. The lantern burned on the drum. The marshal stood on the landing, and the ferryman on the bank, and between them the river moved, the whole long record of the crossing moving past — the boy, the drowned, the years, the gift, the robbery, the rule, the warrant, the watch — all of it in the dark, all of it held, none of it answered.

Amos looked down at the water and did not ask it anything.

The river would not have answered. The river kept. That was its work.

Chapter 29: The Reckoning

They stood on the bank at dusk, the ferryman and the marshal, and the river ran loud between the world and the water.

The town was behind them, up the slope, in the dark — what was left of it, the church and the store both under, the porches drowned and equal, the last of the people gone east or west, carried over on the boat. There was no one left to hear them but the water.

Boone stood with the warrant in his coat, hip aching, the whole weight of it in him. He had carried the paper from Fairhope, and the doubt since, and the town's two mouths, and the flood, and he was tired the way a rope is tired, held too long. He looked at the ferryman and heard himself ask it.

"What is the rule for?"

Amos did not answer. The water ran. He had been asked the rule before — by the merchants, by his own doubt in the long nights — but never like this, never by the law itself, with a warrant that had gone cold.

"The rule," Boone said, "is a wall. You carry everyone. No questions. You carried him, and he went free, and the bank's money is gone into a child's body, and I rode out here to bring him back, and the whole town has picked sides over it. I want to know what the rule is for. What it earns you. What it is supposed to buy."

Amos looked at the water. The river ran loud, full, carrying everything — the boy, the drowned, the years, the crossing, the two men standing on the bank, the whole of the story already moving past them into the dark.

"The rule," Amos said, "is not a wall."

He had not known he would say it. The words came up out of him the way water comes up through the sand, slow, unbidden, the way the truth comes when the flood has taken everything else.

"It is not a wall. It is the crossing itself. A man can come to the water carrying anything — a watch, a warrant, a child's name, a dead son — and the boat will take him, and the water will carry him, and on the other side he is the same man with the same load, only the river has seen it. And the river keeps it. That is all. That is the whole of it."

He stopped. The water ran. The lantern burned on the drum.

"The rule is not for them," Amos said. "It is for the water. So that when the river keeps the evidence — and it will — there is one man who can say he carried everyone, and asked nothing, and was the same to both banks."

Boone understood, or felt, the shape of it: the ferryman had built his whole life so that the river's record would have no case against him. Not against the law. Against the water.

And Boone, who had carried the law as a rope held too long, felt something give in him, the way the bank gives under a flood, slow, and then all at once.

"Take me across," he said.

Chapter 30: The Marshal

Boone had not meant to decide anything, but the deciding had been done for him, down the long days of the flood, the way the river decides a channel.

The hip had been bad for years — the bullet that had found it at the Ford County line, in the year his son was eleven, the year before the boy was shot. The son. Boone had carried him the way he carried the hip, without speaking of it, the way a man carries a thing he has no words for. The boy had been seventeen, and the gun had gone off at a card table in a town Boone could not name, and the law he served had not been able to unkill him, and Boone had kept the law anyway, because the law was the only rope he had left that held.

Now he stood at the ferryman's boat, in the dark, and understood that he had been holding the law too long, the way a man holds a rope past the burning of it, past the point where holding is still holding and not just refusing to let go.

He thought about Vane. He had a paper with a name and a robbery and a reward, a town divided, a child healed — the operation, the money spent, the small grave that had not had to be dug. And the warrant was cold now, the trail gone under the flood, the man gone west where the law did not run and the water did not follow.

He decided what he would carry west.

Not the man. Not the paper. Not the reward, though Brant had offered him a cut of it and he had said no without deciding to, the way a man says no to a thing he knows will cost him more than it pays. He would carry the law, because he was the law. But he would carry it empty, the way the ferryman carried everyone — asking nothing, judging nothing, letting the river keep what the river kept.

The hip ached. The water ran. The ferryman stood at the rope.

"Take me across," Boone said, and the two men took the boat.

They cast off together, the marshal at the rope with the ferryman, the first time the law and the rule had worked the crossing side by side. The boat came about in the current, the deck awash, the cable singing its high note, the lantern throwing the two men's shadows out over the moving water.

Boone worked the rope and did not think about the warrant. He worked it the way the ferryman worked it, in tasks, in the pull and the give, and the river carried them, and for the first time in years the marshal's hands had a thing in them that did not weigh like a dead boy and a bad hip.

The east bank fell away behind them. The west bank came on in the dark.

And Boone, with the warrant in his coat and the river running loud under the planks, decided what he would leave on the deck — and what he would carry west into the free ground, alone, where the law did not run and the water did not follow.

Chapter 31: The Fare

The river had the deck. Amos felt it under the planks, the whole brown weight of it alive beneath them, pulling at the boat like a thing with hands, and he kept his own hands on the rope and did not look at the far bank, because looking did not move the boat. The cable sang. It was a note he had heard forty years, drawn out of a cable the way a bow is drawn across a fiddle string, but tonight it sang high and thin, pulled past its temper by the flood, and the note put a cold thing in his chest.

Boone sat on the bench with the river washing over his boots. He had said nothing since they shoved off, and Amos did not expect talk. A man crossing at night on a river in flood does not talk; he listens. The rope came through Amos's hands in a rhythm older than speech, and the boat answered it, and for a while that was enough — the two men and the flood and the rope and the dark.

Then Boone moved. Amos heard it before he saw it, the creak of the bench and the drag of a wet hand into a coat, and he looked up. Boone had the paper out. He held it not toward Amos's hand but down to the deck between them, and he laid it flat on the planks and set his palm on one edge of it to hold it against the water.

The warrant. Amos knew it by the size of it, the deep crease of it, the way a paper that has lived a long time against a man's chest keeps its fold. It lay on the wet oak with the river curling at its edges like a tongue tasting it.

"There," Boone said.

Amos did not take it up. His hands were on the rope, and they were the hands of the ferry, and the boat was mid-river, and there was no hand to spare for paper. He looked at the warrant, and then at the water beyond it, and pulled.

The cable sang. The far bank came on out of the dark. The paper lay between them like a third man on the deck, and neither of them spoke of it, and the river carried the boat and the boat carried the paper, and the lantern on the drum burned and threw their two shadows out across the water, one long, one short, joined at the planks.

A fare, Amos thought, was a thing given for a crossing. He had taken coin and corn and once a string of horseshoes, and in the fall he had taken a watch from a man with nothing else. He had never taken a warrant. The water took it now, edge by edge, and he let it, and the crossing went on, and the free ground came up black ahead of them, and he worked the rope and kept his eyes on the work, because there are some fares a man should not look at too long.

Chapter 32: The Choice

The water ran past the boat with the whole flood behind it, and Amos worked the crossing the way he had worked ten thousand crossings, in tasks. Hands to the rope. Feet to the deck. The drum turning its slow round of light. A man who stops to think on a river in flood does not get off the river, and Amos did not think. He pulled, and the boat answered, and the paper lay where it had been laid, and he did not look at it.

Boone watched the water. The flood had taken everything Amos knew — the willow that marked the old channel was gone, the far landing a smear of mud and dark. Boone did not know the river out here. He knew it the way a man knows a debt, by its weight, and the weight of it sat beside him on the bench, a man with a bad hip and a coat that had come empty of its warrant.

The thought went through Amos like a current through a rope, and he let it pass. He did not reach for the paper. He did not ask. That was the choice, and it was made the way all his choices were made, in the hands, without a word, and the river took it and held it, the way it held everything, and carried the moment away downstream into the dark where it would keep it.

The boat came on. The near bank rose out of the water, the landing eaten down to mud, the pole where the rope came ashore standing in the current. Amos turned the drum for the last of it and the boat kissed the mud and settled, and the cable went slack and sang its low note once and was still.

Boone stood up slow, the way a man with a bad hip stands up, and he held the post and pulled himself upright, and he looked at the deck where the paper lay, and then he looked at Amos, and his face was the face of a man who had held a rope too long and had just let it go.

"I'll say it once," Boone said. "No man owes me a thing across this water."

He turned toward the bow, and the dark of the free ground stood open behind him, and he left the paper where it lay on the wet planks, and the river carried the moment, and the two men stood on the grounded boat with the warrant between them, and neither moved, and the water went on past, carrying the whole of it downstream into the place where the river keeps what it is given, and Amos understood, at the far bank, what the crossing had cost and what it had bought, and the understanding sat in him the way a stone sits in a current, held, and not yet moving.

Chapter 33: The Warrant, Left

Boone stepped off the bow and the mud took his boot, and he pulled it free and went up the bank. The water had eaten the low ground and the path climbed through mud to his knees, and he climbed slow with his hand on his bad hip, and he did not look back.

Amos watched him go. Men turned on this bank. Men looked back at the lantern and the water and the lit window of the ferry house across the river, and then they went on. Boone did not turn. He reached the top of the bank and stood a moment against the sky, and then the dark took him, and the free ground was empty, and the west had swallowed the last of the law of Fairhope.

The paper was still on the deck. The water had got one edge of it, the wet running up the fold like a slow tide, and the ink had blurred where it had touched, the name gone soft at the tail — Vane's name, or what was left of it, running into the grain of the oak.

Amos stood over it. He had not picked it up mid-river, and he had not picked it up when Boone turned, and now he bent and took it by the dry edge, and it came up out of the water with the wet edge dripping, and he held it in his hand.

Boone had said nothing of Vane. Not to press it, not to close it. He had carried the warrant across and left it, and he had said his one thing about owing, and he had gone. The paper was the fare, and a fare is a thing given for a crossing, and Boone had given it and asked for nothing.

Amos folded the warrant along its old creases and put it inside his coat, against his chest. The wet edge soaked his shirt. It was a weight like a fare in his pocket, only nothing had been owed to him before that was not coin or a kindness, and this was neither, and it was both.

He looked across the water. The east bank was a string of lanterns now — the town had come down to the landing, and someone had lit the big lamp, and the light stood on the water in a broken path. They would be waiting, and they would ask, and he would have nothing for them.

He took the rope and turned the drum, alone, and the cable took up its song, and the free ground stood empty behind him, and the paper lay against his heart, and he carried it back over the water the way he carried every man's last thing, without asking what it was for.

Chapter 34: The Landing

The crossing back was the same rope and the same water, and none of it was the same. The boat rode light without Boone's weight, and the cable sang a lower note, and Amos worked it alone with the warrant against his chest and the town's lanterns growing on the near bank like a string of coals drawn out of the dark.

He could see them now. They had come down through the mud — Brant in his store coat with a lamp, Cole with a lantern in each hand, the livery man, a knot of women from the church with their skirts dragged black. Mrs. Marsh stood at the head of them with her shawl pulled tight, watching the boat the way a woman watches a grave.

The water was going down. He felt it in the cable, in the angle of the pull; the river had crested sometime in the dark and was leaving, and the old watermark of '71 stood above the boat's wake, and the new line had drawn itself below it, and the river was writing its memory on the bank as it went, the way it had written every year he had known it.

He brought her in. The deck kissed the mud and the mud held, and he dropped the rope over the post and the drum fell still, and the silence where the cable had sung was a thing the whole landing heard, and the town stood at the water's edge holding its lanterns up.

"Where's the marshal?" Brant said.

"West," Amos said.

Cole said, "He mean to come back?"

Amos did not answer. He tied the rope the way he tied it at the end of a day, and he walked up the bank past them, and the lanterns made a path of him, and no one asked about the paper, because no one had seen it, and the town stood at the water's edge with the flood going down and the boat settling at her post.

At the door of the ferry house he stopped and looked back. The drum stood under the lantern, and the notches ran their forty years up its face, and the last crossing was done, and the water was falling past the watermark, and the river was taking back what it had lent, and it did not say what it had seen, and Amos went in and shut the door, and the drum was silent under the lantern, and the river went on falling through the dark.

Chapter 35: The Aftermath

The water went down the way it had come up, slow and certain, and left the town changed. The bottom land was gone, the low fields a brown sheet that dried into cracks and ruts, and the whole landing smelled of mud and green things that had drowned. Men counted what the flood had taken — a barn, a coop of drowned hens, the bridge's wreckage hung up in the cottonwoods a mile down, boards and a wheel and the drowned harness of a team. Brant stood on the store porch among goods saved and goods ruined, tallying on his fingers, and what he tallied did not satisfy him.

No one asked Amos what had passed on the water. That was the strange part, the part he turned over in his hands in the evenings. They asked where the marshal was, and he said west, and they let it stand. The town half-knew. It knew the ferryman had carried a warrant out and come back without the marshal and without the talk, and it did not press, because the flood had given it a thing to bury that was easier than the truth, and a town that has buried its dead does not dig up a question.

Amos kept the warrant in his coat through that first day, folded at the old creases, the wet edge gone stiff and brown. That night, when the town had gone up from the landing and the ferry house was shut, he took it out and stood at the stove with the lamp low, and he did not read it. He had read it enough. He opened the stove door, and the fire was banked and low, and he laid the paper in, and it caught at a corner and curled and burned, and the name on it went to ash, and the three hundred dollars and the reward and the charge and the law of Fairhope went up with it, and the room was warm a moment, and then the fire was only the fire.

He opened the door again after, when the paper was nothing, and he took a little of the ash on his fingertips and put it in his shirt pocket, where it would not blow away.

No one asked what passed on the water. The town went on counting its losses, and Brant and Cole spoke of the reward less and less, and the ferry ran again when the bank took its shape, and Amos worked her with the ash against his chest and the watch in the tin, and the river kept what it had seen and did not give it back.

Chapter 36: The Town Heals

The mud went out of the streets, and the roofs got mended, and the town settled back onto its foundations the way a man stands up after a fall and feels for his bones and finds them whole.

Mrs. Hennessy's roof was the last. The flood had lifted a corner of it, and the rain had found the lift, and the water had come into her room the way water comes into a sinking boat, quiet and everywhere at once. The church men went up with the shingles and the nails, and widow Bickley stood below handing them up, and the roof went on straight and true, and Mrs. Hennessy made coffee and set it on the porch and would not let them leave without it, and the porch stood warm in the afternoon with the steam and the talk.

Brant and Cole grumbled into quiet. The reward had been a bright thing between them all fall, and now it was a dead thing, and the town could feel the lack of it the way you feel a missing tooth. Cole spoke of the marshal once, and then twice, and then stopped, because there was nothing to say that did not come around to the ferryman, and the ferryman said nothing, and there is no argument that stands against a man who will not speak.

The ferry ran. The river had fallen to its ordinary brown, and the landing had its shape again, and Amos worked her the way he had worked her for forty years — the rope, the drum, the worn planks. The water still tasted of the flood, heavy and full of the fields and the drowned, but it was going bitter again, going back to what it was, and the taste on his hands was the taste of every other year he had drawn a rope across it.

A riverbank heals by going on, he thought. The grass comes back, the water levels, the mark of the flood goes pale on the bank. And the town was the same — the widows' roof, the store porch, the livery with its dry stalls, the church door standing open again. He cut no notch. The drum post stood with its forty marks, and the forty-first year was out on the water somewhere, waiting to be crossed, and the river ran past the ferry house the way it had run past every year, and the town came and went across the water, and it healed.

Chapter 37: Vane's Trial, Gone

Word came up the road from Fairhope in the mail pouch, carried by a man from Cole's freight who had been down for the store goods. The letter was addressed to the mayor, but news in a town of two hundred souls does not stay in one house, and by evening it was on the store porch and in the saloon and at Mrs. Marsh's table, and by morning it was everywhere.

The warrant had been recalled. There was no man to try. Cal Vane had crossed a river and a line of the law, and the law had looked at the empty west and closed its book, and the reward was not offered anymore because there was no one left to put a price on.

Brant heard it and said nothing, and the next day he said less. Cole folded the talk up the way a man folds a bill that has come due and gone past, and put it away.

Amos was at the ferry when they told him, working the rope across an empty crossing, and he heard it the way he heard the river, without stopping the work. Vane was free ground. The money had gone into a child's body and done its work, and the child was alive, and the law had given up on the man who had paid for it, and Amos stood at the drum with the ash of the warrant in his shirt pocket, where it had lain all those weeks, and he felt the weight of it go light.

He carried the ash a while longer. He took it out on the mornings when the river ran clear and put his thumb to it, and he did not know why he kept it, except that a thing that has burned is still a thing, and a man who has carried the law against his heart does not let it go all at once.

Then one evening at the landing, alone, with the water running low and grey, he took the ash out and opened his hand over the river. The wind took it, and the water took it, and it went downstream with the current toward the bend where the drowned boy had gone under, all those years and all those floods ago, and the water did not change and did not comment, and Amos turned and went up to the ferry house, and the tin of fares stood on the shelf, and the watch was in it, and the letter lay beside it, and the forty-first year was coming on toward the ice.

Chapter 38: The Daughter's Thanks

The letter came on an ordinary day, folded and addressed in a child's hand, the letters big and careful, the words spelled the way a girl of eight spells them, by sound. Mrs. Marsh brought it down to the ferry house herself and stood at the door holding it out like a thing that might break.

"From Fairhope," she said. "For the ferryman."

Amos took it. He sat at the table under the lamp and turned it over. The paper was the cheap kind, and the fold was a child's fold, and the name on the front was his, written large — Mr. Amos Reed, the Ferry, Bitter Creek — with the creek spelled two ways across two tries, the first one crossed out and tried again.

He opened it. The lamp was going and he held the paper to the light, and the letters stood up from the page the way a child's letters stand, built one stroke at a time, and the words came slow to him because they were slow to write.

Dear Mr. Reed. Pa told me about the ferry and about the watch. He said you took it when he had nothing and you did not ask him anything. He said you were kind. I am well now. The doctor says I am well. Pa went across your river to the other side and I think he is fine there. Thank you for the crossing. Thank you for not asking. — Ruthie

The lamp burned. Amos read it once, and the words did the thing that words do when they are true, and he read it twice, and then a third time, slow, the way a man reads a thing he means to keep, and the letters stood and did not change, and the name at the bottom stood the way a girl's name stands when she has written it carefully.

He did not tell anyone. He folded the letter along the child's fold and put it in the tin of fares, beside the watch, where the fare that had bought a crossing lay next to the thanks that had bought nothing and bought everything, and he shut the tin and set it back on the shelf.

The lamp burned. The river ran past the window, brown and going cold. Ruthie was well. The watch was warm against the paper in the dark of the tin, and the tin held them both, the one that had been given and the one that had come back, and Amos sat at the table a long time with his hands on his knees, and he did not read it a fourth time, but he knew the words by heart already, and the river went on, and the ice was coming, and the room was warm.

Chapter 39: Amos's Rest

The evening came down clean. The day's last crossing had been a wagon of rails for the bridge work, and Amos had taken the fare and watched them go up the road toward the timber, and now the river lay flat and brown in the last light, and there was no one on the bank, and the ferry sat at her post like a thing resting.

He went in and lit the lamp and made his supper and ate it slow, and then he took down the tin of fares and set it on the table and opened it.

Coins. The day's honest crossings, a few cents worn smooth as river stones. The watch. The letter. They lay together in the tin like the two sides of a single account, and Amos lifted the watch out and held it in his hand, and it was warm the way it had been warm the first time, the day a trail-worn man had laid it down for a crossing and had not asked for anything back.

He sat with it a long time. He did not wind it. It was not his time it kept; it had never kept his time, and that was the right of it. It had crossed west with its owner and come back to him in his hand, and it owed no one anything, and neither did he.

He thought of the forty years. The rope in his hands. The planks under his feet. Lena in the fever year, the year the whole town burned through, and the boy upstream who had gone under at six and never come home, kept by the river. The notches on the drum, one for every year, running up the post like the marks of a man measuring himself. And the years ran one into another the way the water ran, and for the first time in forty years the running did not weigh on him.

The lamp burned low. He put the watch back in the tin and shut it and set it on the shelf, and he stood at the door with the river going dark at the edge of the light. The ease sat in his chest the way a boat sits at her mooring, loose on the rope, and the river ran, and it did not ask him for anything, and he had nothing left to give it but the keeping of the crossing, and that he could give, and he closed the door, and the night came down over the ferry house and the water, and the ferry rested.

Chapter 40: The Rule, Kept

The stranger came down the road in the cold of the year's turn, walking with his coat collar up and his eyes ahead, and he stood at the bank a long time before he called across.

"Ferryman."

Amos came out. He had seen the man coming, the way he walked, the way a man walks when he has a thing behind him and not a thing ahead. The river was low and grey, and the air had the first edge of the ice in it, and the stranger's boots were caked with the mud of a long road, and he kept his hand at his belt where the gun rode.

"Crossing," the stranger said.

Amos looked at the far bank, and then at the man. He did not ask where from. He did not ask what he had done, or what was after him, or why his hands shook when they closed on the tin. The rule was older than any of those questions, and the rule was this: everyone, no questions.

He took the fare. Coin, light in the hand, a few coins from a man who had run with what he could carry. Amos put them in the tin and hefted the rope, and the stranger got on the boat and stood at the rail with his back to the east, watching the water, and Amos worked the cable, and the drum turned, and the boat went out into the grey.

The stranger was not Vane. He was not built like Vane, did not carry himself like Vane, had none of Vane's gentleness at the edges. But he could have been. He was a man running, and the river was the line he meant to cross, and Amos did not ask, and the boat went on, and the far bank came up through the cold, and the stranger stepped off and went west without looking back, and the cable sang its low note, and Amos brought the empty boat back.

He cut no notch. The year was not done. But he stood at the drum with the rope in his hand, and the rule had outlived the flood and the warrant and the law and the leaving, and it would outlive him, and the river ran under the grey sky, and the water was going cold, and the ice was coming, and the crossing held, and the rule was kept.

Chapter 41: The Winter

Ice on the cable. The river shut itself in the third week of December, a skin of white creeping out from both banks until the brown was gone and there was nothing but a field no one had plowed.

Amos took the ferry in before it closed over. It took him most of a day. He walked the deck clear of snow, greased the drum, wound the cable easy onto its reel so no frost could kink it. The rope he coiled in the ferry house over the stove pipe, where it would keep dry. A rope frozen wet was a rope half dead, and he knew it the way he knew the notches in the drum post, forty of them then, blind and sure.

The town went quiet under the snow. Smoke stood straight off Mrs. Marsh's chimney. Brant's store kept its lamp lit past dark for the company of the light. Men crossed the frozen river on foot now, quick and blue, and the ferry sat in its slip like a grave.

Amos sat by the stove with the tin of fares in his lap. The watch was in it. He did not take it out. He knew its weight without looking, the way a man knows the weight of a coat he has worn forty years. Some nights he talked to the water. Not praying. Just talking, the way a man talks to a mule because talking is company. The ice could not hear him, but he talked anyway, and the water heard him through the ice, and held what he said the way it held everything.

The river remembered under the ice. It kept the boy at six, down in the cold dark, kept the sound of his name where the current could carry it. It kept the drowned, the ones he had hauled out and the ones he had not found. It kept the ash of the warrant, drifted and gone. It kept every crossing, every fare, every foot that had walked the planks. It forgave nothing and it forgot nothing, and under the ice it ran dark and patient as a held breath.

Amos put the tin back on the shelf. He banked the stove. Outside, the ice lay white as a page with nothing written on it, and under it the water went on moving, going where it had always gone.

He lay down on the bunk and listened to the ice groan and settle, and in the groan he heard the water underneath, talking, waiting.

Spring would come. It had never once failed to come. It was the only promise he had ever trusted that had not lied.

Chapter 42: The Spring

The thaw came sudden, the way grief comes. One morning the ice was white and solid. By noon the channel opened in the middle, black water smoking in the cold air, and the ice went out with a sound like a door tearing loose.

Amos stood on the bank and watched it go. Whole fields of it, carrying snow and sticks and one drowned cow, all of it sliding west toward the free ground, the water taking back its skin. The river rose over its banks a little, then settled, brown and patient and ashamed of nothing.

He put the ferry back in. It took him a day and a half. He freed the cable and ran it over the drum, checking for frost cracks the way a man checks a rope for rot, inch by inch, by feel. He re-tarred the planks where the ice had scarred them. The rope he uncoiled from the stove pipe and worked with linseed oil until it came soft and willing in his hands.

The first crossing was a mail rider bound west. Amos paid out the line and the drum sang its first song of the year, and the sound of it went across the brown water and up into the town, and men came out onto their porches to hear it, like a bell that had been silent all winter.

The banks greened. Willow shoots came up pale as candle flame. The mud of the landing softened and smelled of the river, of alkali and rot and new things. Amos cranked the drum and the deck slid out over the water and the current talked under the planks, and he remembered the feel of it the way a hand remembers a tool.

The widow Hennessy crossed with a basket of eggs. The livery man crossed with two mules that balked on the planks, and Amos waited, and did not hurry them. The church women crossed to the west bank to plant something by a grave of nothing in particular. People talked again. The town had been a held breath all winter, and now it let the breath out.

In the evenings Amos sat on the drum post and watched the river run. It was the same river. It had not been anywhere. It had only waited, the way he had waited, and now it went on with its work, carrying what the land gave it, forgetting nothing.

He put his hand on the drum. The wood was warm from the day's turning. Forty years of his hands had worn it smooth, and the notch for the forty-first year stood fresh in the post, waiting to be made real by the turning of a season.

The water slipped past, brown and endless, and Amos thought: it came back.

It always came back.

Chapter 43: The Ferry, Again

Summer came in green and heavy. The river dropped slow, leaving mudbanks baked white as bone. And the ferry ran.

Amos worked her from first light to last, and the work was not a weight anymore. Forty-one years had turned it into something else, the way a rope turns into a rope, the way a man's hands turn into his hands. He knew every plank by its scar: the charred one from the fire, the bullet gouge from the war, the long crack Lena had watched him pin and never once said would hold. She had been right. It had held.

A wedding party crossed in June. The bride's mother wept all the way over, and the bride laughed at the water and threw a penny in, and Amos counted no fare for them because the penny had gone to the river and the river was the only toll that mattered. The river took the penny and said nothing.

A coffin crossed in July. A farm wife, old, who had crossed on the ferry forty years to market and never once complained of the fare. Amos carried the box himself, set it square on the deck, walked the cable slow so as not to jostle. He did not know her people. He did not ask. He said the one thing a ferryman says, which is nothing, and the river carried her west one last time and gave her to the free ground, and the family paid in silver and did not look back.

A load of lumber crossed in August for the new barn behind Cole's freight office. The mules complained. The planks stacked high as Amos's shoulder, and he rigged a line to steady them, working the drum with his weight, patient as the water. Cole paid the fare and did not count the change, which was its own kind of apology, and Amos took it without comment, because money was money and the river did not care how it was earned.

In the evenings the town came down to the bank to sit. The children threw stones. Brant and Cole smoked their pipes and talked prices and were almost pleasant. Mrs. Marsh brought lemonade. Even the stranger from the spring, the shifty one who had crossed and crossed back, was gone now, and no one spoke of him, and the river kept whatever he had been running from, the way it kept everything.

Amos sat on the drum post and watched them. They did not see him. They saw the ferry, the flat oak deck and the cable and the drum, forty years of feet worn into the planks, and they thought it was just a boat.

He knew better. The boat was the heart of them. It was the only thing on the creek that took all of them, fair and no questions, and carried them, and brought them back.

He put his hand on the drum, warm and smooth, and the river ran past, brown and endless, carrying the day away.

The ferry worked her. Or he worked her. It did not matter which anymore.

Chapter 44: The Settlers

They came in September, a wagon and a team and a family, and Amos saw them from far off, the way the river saw everything, a little dust moving along the road from Fairhope.

The man drove. The woman held a small child, gray-faced, wrapped to the chin in a blanket though the day was warm. Two boys hung off the back of the wagon, sunburned and thin. The whole rig creaked like an old gate.

The man pulled up at the landing and looked at the water.

"West," he said. Just that.

Amos nodded. He had heard the word a thousand times. It was a whole sentence in one word.

He took the team aboard first, gentle with the horses, talking low. Then the wagon, blocked at the wheels. Then the woman with the child, and Amos took her elbow, light, the way he had taken Lena's elbow, and she did not thank him, just looked at the water, and the child's eyes were open and watching everything, too old in them.

They crossed slow. Amos walked the cable easy, giving the river time, and the river was quiet under them, holding the weight of the wagon and the family and the sick child, not judging, not caring, just holding.

"My husband says there's free ground out there," the woman said. "Land that will take a man." She said it flat, the way she had said it to herself a hundred times.

"There's land," Amos said. "I don't know that it's free."

"Nothing's free," she said. "But a body can try."

The child coughed. Small, dry, like a stone dropped in a well. Amos felt it in his own chest, and for a moment he was on the bank twenty-five years ago, the boy at six, the water taking him, and there was nothing in the world that sound did not reach.

He said nothing. There was nothing to say. The river was the river.

The far bank came under the deck and the wagon rolled off into the green. The man shook his hand, hard, like a man shaking a promise. The woman did not look back. The child watched him over her shoulder, those old eyes, until the wagon dipped behind the rise and was gone west.

Amos walked the boat back. The cable sang, and the river ran under him, holding the child's cough somewhere in its memory, holding the woman's flat voice, holding the word west.

He put the fare in the tin. Silver. More than he had asked.

He did not count it. Some crossings were not fares. Some were beginnings, and a ferryman learned to tell the difference the way he learned the water.

The river carried them now. It was his job to set them down easy on the water. The rest was the water's.

Chapter 45: The Soldiers

The cavalry came at dusk, six men and a prisoner, and Amos saw the blue coats from the bank and waited.

The sergeant rode up to the landing. He was young, with a hard young face and a holster worn soft. The prisoner sat his horse with wrists bound to the horn, a man of forty, gray, head down.

"Ferryman," the sergeant said. "We're crossing."

Amos looked at the prisoner. The man did not look up. The river was the color of old iron in the failing light.

"Coin or goods," Amos said.

The sergeant counted out army silver into his palm. Enough for six men and a horse each. Amos put it in the tin. The fare was the fare. The river did not care what a man carried.

They crossed in two loads. The prisoner stood in the middle of the deck and did not move. Amos worked the cable and watched the water instead, and let the river do the reading.

Mid-river, the prisoner spoke, low, to the deck at his feet.

"Ferryman. You don't know what they're taking me back for."

"No," Amos said. "And I don't ask."

"They'll hang me," the prisoner said. "That's what they're taking me back for."

Amos cranked the drum. The cable sang. The water talked under the planks.

"If I asked you to set me over on the west bank and keep my horse," the prisoner said, "what would you say?"

Amos thought of the rule. Everyone, no questions. The rule did not say he had to carry a man to his death. It said he had to carry him.

"I'd say the same thing I always say," Amos said. "The fare's already paid."

The prisoner looked at him a long moment. Then he looked away, at the water, and said nothing else.

The deck grounded on the east bank, and the soldiers took their prisoner and rode off toward Fairhope and the hanging, and Amos walked the boat back across the dark river alone.

The blue coats went with them. The river carried the color of them a while, then let it go, and Amos put the army silver in the tin and did not count it.

The rule had held. It had held him, too, and it was heavier than the fare.

He thought: some men you carry to their grave.

The river said nothing.

Chapter 46: The Outlaws

They came at night, three of them, on three horses, and they did not call out from the bank like honest men. They came down through the willows silent, and Amos heard them before he saw them, and he lit the lantern on the drum and waited.

The leader was a lean man with a beard like a trap. The other two hung back in the dark, and their horses were too good for the road they had come. Amos held the lantern up.

"Ferry," he said. "Fare's a dime a man, a dime a horse."

"We're short," the lean man said.

Amos looked at him. The river was black and moving in the lantern light.

"Everyone, no questions," the lean man said, and Amos felt the words land like a rock in still water. Vane's road. The same road, worn by the same kind of men, going the same direction, west, to the free ground, to be forgotten.

"Fare's still a dime," Amos said.

The lean man reached into his saddlebag and tossed a coin. It rang on the deck and lay there, too bright in the lantern light. New-minted and clean, the kind of silver a working man never saw.

Amos bent and picked it up. It was warm from the man's hand.

The three crossed with him, leading their horses aboard, and the river carried them out into the dark, and nobody said a word until they were mid-water.

"You don't ask where a man's been," the lean man said.

"No," Amos said.

"You don't ask why a man's got silver in his bag and a road behind him."

"No," Amos said. "I just cross them. That's all a ferryman does."

The lean man laughed, quiet. "That's all there is to it. You're the only honest thing between here and the free ground."

They grounded on the west bank and rode off into the dark, and Amos cranked the boat back alone across the black water.

He counted the fare into the tin. One silver dollar for three men and three horses. He left it there, bright against the old coins, and he did not wonder where it had come from. That was the rule. That was the point of the rule.

But the river knew. The river had seen the silver taken and the silver spent, had seen Vane's money go into a child's body and this silver go into his tin, and it held it all, bright and dark together, and forgave none of it.

Amos put the lantern back on the drum. The light touched the water, and the water took the light and carried it away.

The rule had held. It would hold. It was all he had that the river could not take.

Chapter 47: The River, Remembering

Evening in late October, and Amos sat on the drum post and watched the river run.

It was the time of day he liked, when the water went dark and the light lay flat on it, the time the river did its remembering out loud. He had sat here forty-one years, most nights, and the river had talked to him in its one voice, and he had learned to listen.

Tonight it ran past him and showed him everything.

The boy at six, upstream, the summer the fever took Lena, the small body the river kept and would not give back. He saw it the way he saw it every night: the current taking the little shoulders, the brown water closing over. Twenty years of that, and still the river carried the sound of the name no one said anymore.

Lena's ashes, scattered from the bank at dawn, the one time he had let the water take something he loved on purpose. He watched them go, gray on the current, and the river had held them with the boy, both of them together now in the cold dark, both of them his.

The drowned. He counted them off the way he counted the notches. The woman in '63. The two men in the flood of '71. The boy who went in after a hat. The ones he had hauled out, gray and heavy, and the ones the river had kept, and every one of them still in the water, still held.

The warrants. The one he had burned, and the one he had carried west, and the one Boone had left on the deck. Ash, paper, and water. He had held all three, and the river held all three now.

The watch. It was in the tin in the ferry house. Vane had given it for a crossing, and Amos had carried it a year now, and it was heavier than any fare had a right to be.

The years. Forty-one of them, the notches in the drum post, the planks worn smooth, the cable singing the same song.

The water ran past him, brown and endless, and it did not speak his language, and still it spoke to him. It said: I have it all. I have the boy and the woman and the drowned and the ash and the paper and the watch and the years, and I have you, Amos Reed. I have your name and your hands, and I will keep it all, and I will forgive none of it.

He put his hand in the water. It was cold, the way it was always cold, and it moved over his fingers, going where it had always gone, and he let it hold his hand a moment, and then took it back, the way a man takes his hand back from a thing he loves and cannot keep.

The river ran on. It was the memory of the country, and he was in it, and it was in him.

He went in and banked the stove, and the tin was on the shelf, and he lay down, and the river went on remembering without him.

Chapter 48: The Forty-First Year

Spring came, and with it a letter.

The mail rider left it at Brant's store, and Brant carried it down to the ferry house himself, because a letter for the ferryman was a thing worth seeing. Amos took it and turned it over. No stamp. No return. Just his name, Amos Reed, in a hand that had not written much.

He opened it by the lamp. Inside was money. Folded bills, more than he had ever held at once, more than the fare had ever cost, more than the watch was worth.

And a line. One line, in the same unpracticed hand.

The fare, returned. With interest. Thank you for the crossing.

Amos read it twice. He did not read it a third time. He folded the bills with the paper and put them in the tin, beside the watch, and sat on the bunk a long time with the lamp burning down.

He knew who it was from. He had not needed the river to tell him. Vane. The free ground. The man gone west to be forgotten, who had crossed on a watch and gone on to make a life out of a child's surgery and a bank's money and a ferryman's silence.

The fare, returned. Worth more than the watch. The arithmetic was simple, and the weight of it was not.

Forty years done. He went out to the drum post with his knife and cut one more notch. The knife bit into the oak and the wood gave, and the fresh pale notch stood beside the old dark ones, the way a new year stands beside the old.

He put his hand flat on the post, over the notches, forty-one of them now, and felt them the way a man feels the years in his own body, blind and sure.

The money was in the tin. It was not his. It never had been. It was the river's, the way everything was the river's, and the river did not want it, so he kept it, the way he kept the watch, the way he kept everything the water had given him.

The river ran on. The notch was cut. The year was turned.

He was forty-one years a ferryman, and the rule was still holding, and he had been paid, once, for a crossing he had not asked a price for.

Chapter 49: The Crossing, Last

The young man came in April, on foot, carrying a sack, and stood on the bank and watched the ferry the way a man watches a thing he is about to give his life to.

Amos crossed over to him and grounded the boat.

"You want the river," Amos said.

The young man looked at him. "My father crossed here. Said a man named Reed runs the ferry. Said it's the only honest work on the creek."

Amos looked at the water, then at the young man. He had a quiet face, a face that would do for the river. "It's work," Amos said. "The honest part is yours."

He showed him the boat. The oak planks and the scars. The cable and the drum. The way to walk a load on so the deck rode even. The way to read the water, the pale slick under the brown. The young man listened the way a man listens to rope, with his hands.

"Forty-one years," Amos said. "That's the river's count. Mine's the notches."

He showed him the drum post. The young man ran his fingers over the notches, the old dark ones and the fresh pale one, and did not count them. Good. A ferryman did not count another man's years.

That night Amos lay awake and listened to the river, and he knew what it was asking.

At dawn he went down to the boat alone. The young man was sleeping in the ferry house, and Amos did not wake him. He freed the cable and took the ferry out across the water, west, the way he had carried a thousand men, and this time the deck was empty and the only fare was his own.

It was the first crossing he had made for himself in forty-one years.

Mid-river he stopped the drum and stood. The sun came up red over the east bank, over the town, over the notch-post with its forty-one marks, and the west bank came up green and empty and free. He had carried every soul who asked, and he had never once stood where they stood.

He took one step onto the far bank. The mud took his weight. It was just mud, and it was the whole world he had never walked on.

He could have gone on. He could have walked west and been forgotten, like all the men he had carried.

He turned and got back on the ferry and crossed home.

The young man was on the bank, waiting. Amos grounded the boat and picked up the rope, worn smooth by his hands, and put it into the young man's hands.

"Keep the drum greased," Amos said. "Read the water before it reads you. And no questions. Everyone crosses, and no questions."

"Yes, sir," the young man said.

Amos looked back across the water, at the free ground he had finally stood on and left.

"One more thing," he said. "The river remembers everything. It's the only thing that will hold you, and it will not let you go."

He let go of the rope. The young man held it.

The river ran past, carrying the morning, and the last crossing of the book was done, not west, not east, but his own, made and returned.

Chapter 50: The Water

Amos Reed walked down to the water's edge in the last of the light, and the river came up over his boots.

It was cold. It had always been cold. He stood and let it have his feet, the way it had had his hands, the way it had had his years, and he did not pull back.

The crossing was done. The young man was at the ferry house, learning the cable, and the boat was hauled up on the bank with the deck swept, and the drum was silent, and the notches were cut. Forty-one of them. That was the count, and it was enough.

He carried the tin. He had brought it down empty, and now he stood in the water and opened it. The watch was in it. Vane's watch, warm as a hand, heavy as a year. He held it a moment, the whole crossing in his palm: the man who had paid his fare with a dead wife's watch and gone west to raise a daughter, the money returned, the letter in a child's hand, the years.

He threw the watch into the river.

It went under the brown water without a sound, the way the boy had gone under, the way Lena's ashes had gone, the way everything went, and the river took it and held it and did not give it back.

He gave the notches too, all of them, the forty-one years, the mark of every season he had stood at the drum, he gave to the water with both hands, empty, the way he gave everything, and the river took the years and held them with the boy and the woman and the drowned and the ash and the paper and the watch.

He stood in the water a long time. It was up past his shins, and it was cold, and it did not move him, and it did not judge him, and it did not forgive him. It held him. It had always held him. He was the last thing he had to give it, and he stood there and gave it.

Then he walked out, one step, and then another, and the water let him go the way it let everyone go, carrying nothing away and keeping everything.

On the bank he did not look back. The light was almost gone. The river ran past, brown and endless, and it carried his name and his hands and his forty-one years and the boy and the woman and the watch and the warrant and every foot that had walked the planks, and it forgave none of it.

Amos Reed went up the bank toward the ferry house, where the lamp was lit and the young man waited, and behind him the river ran on, remembering everything.

And the last line belonged to the river, which said nothing at all, and held all of it, and forgot none of it, and went on.
