The Wax Light
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Seal

The room was dark except for the candle. It stood on the desk, a single gold point, and by it the hands moved without sound. The letter lay open on the board, its fold creased and re-creased, the paper gone soft at the hinges. The hands had read it once already. They did not read it again.

From the tin the left hand took a lump of wax the color of old honey, deep and heavy, and frost bloomed across its surface where the air had cooled it. The right hand held it over the candle flame. The wax softened, brightened, ran in a slow amber tear down the knuckles, and the smell that rose was not the smell of the room at all but the smell of a rendering fire remembered — oak, and rosemary, and clean beeswax.

The hands shook the letter flat and drew the melted wax along the fold where a seal had once sat. Beneath the new wax, if a man had looked closely, the paper carried the ghost of the older seal — a dark stain like a wound healing over. The hands did not look closely. They were not a sealer's hands. They had the quick, neat, eager habit of a clerk, a hand that wanted to be done.

The matrix was heavy brass, engraved with a device the hands had handled a thousand times and never once considered. They pressed it into the hot wax. Once. The impression came up sharp and clean, and a sealer would have let it stand.

The hands lifted the matrix, looked at the seal, and pressed again.

The second strike landed a hair off the first, the edge sliding a finger's breadth aside and blurring the rim. The hands studied the seal in the candlelight. They might have scraped the wax away and begun again — but the scrape would leave its own scar, and the scar was already there beneath it. They might have struck once, clean, the way the shop did it. Instead they put the matrix back in its box, because the seal was good enough, and no one examined a seal that closely. Seals were for keeping secrets.

The wax cooled. The bloom returned, frosting the surface, dulling. The hands returned the letter to the pile of letters and blew out the candle.

The letter had never been opened. The seal said so. That was the point of a seal — it said what a man needed it to say, and the city believed.

Chapter 2: Tanner Lane

The quarter stank, and the stink was a comfort. Benet walked to work through it every morning of his life: the hides hanging in yellowing sheets over the pits, the lime and the brine, the dogs curled in the steam, the smoke of the tanning fires lying along the lane like river fog. Frost bit at the air, and the river ran gray beyond the rooftops, and the Weir thrummed its low note that the whole city slept to.

He walked through the leather quarter the way other men walked through gardens, reading the weather in the stretch of a hide and the season in the color of the fires, and the quarter — which was the stink of the city to everyone else — was home to him because at its far end stood the sweetest-smelling craft in the city.

The Wax Light. A painted candle glowed behind the window glass, and above the sign hung the lantern, lit at dusk and never put out, by a custom as old as the shop. Even in the gray morning the lantern burned, a small gold eye over the lane. In a city of damp and fog and black canals, the honest flame of the Wax Light never went out. The tanners' smoke rose behind it, and the smell of oak and rosemary cut through the stink like a clean knife.

Nella, the tallow-chandler's daughter across the lane, was setting out her father's wares, and she waved with a hand that smelled of tallow and never seemed to mind it. Benet waved back. Tallow was the poor cousin of wax, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so.

The city ran on sealed letters. Every charter for the wharves, every deed struck in the Salt Market, every letter of credit that crossed the Weir on a barge, every writ and contract and promise — closed with a seal, and the seal was trusted because a seal could not lie. A letter traveled the roads unopened because its seal said: this is mine, this is true, this is as I left it. The city was held together by hot wax and a single clean blow. Benet knew it because he made the wax, and he walked through the morning with the weight of that knowledge on him like a coat.

He reached the shop, and the rendering fire already breathed its oak-and-rosemary smoke through the vents, and Marta's voice called something to Piet inside. The lantern above the sign swung a little in the river wind. It guttered, and held.

Chapter 3: The Wax Shop

Inside, the shop was all warm shadow and gold. The rendering fire breathed at the back wall, oak logs and rosemary tops feeding a flame that had not gone out since the shop was built. Along the bench stood the tins and the molds, the brass scales, the vials of dye; from the beams hung the wicks, and from the rafters the lot-laden smell of beeswax that a man could taste on his tongue.

Old Marta sat at the high desk, quill in hand, and Piet stood on a stool trimming wicks with the careful concentration of a boy who had burned himself once and meant never to again. At thirteen he was all elbows and serious eyes.

The lot ledger lay open under Marta's hand. Every lot the shop had ever poured had its page — a number, a recipe, a date, a buyer, and a shaving of the lot pasted beside the ink, thin as a petal, so that the book could speak for the wax long after the wax was gone. Marta ran her thumb along the newest page and looked up.

'My father was ruined by a forged seal when I was a girl,' she said, as she said at least once a season. 'A piece of wax that said a thing a man had never signed. They took his name, his shop, his standing. I was nine. I watched them do it. That is why this book exists.' She tapped the ledger. 'Wax lies only when a man makes it lie. We keep the truth in the book, and the book keeps the wax honest.'

Benet did not answer. He had heard it a hundred times, and it never got lighter.

They turned to the standing order. Tomorrow the shop would pour the Guild's new-year sealing wax — Lot 14, the recipe that Marta and Benet had worked three winters to get right: madder from the root, weld from the stem, and a pinch of iron vitriol to set the color and turn it, so that the amber went green in candlelight. It was the shop's signature now, deep amber with a frosted bloom and the smell of the fire that made it. No other wax in the city had that color, or that bloom, or that smell.

Benet's hands weighed the dyes and lined the vials up on the bench. He knew the lot before it was poured — knew how it would run, how it would cool, how it would bloom. His hands had been in wax since he was seven years old and first allowed to hold a tin.

At dusk Marta lit the lantern and set it in its place above the sign. Benet stood in the door a moment, watching the flame steady itself. Tomorrow would be a long day. The Guild's wax did not wait for anyone, and neither did the shop.

Chapter 4: The Dye

That evening, with the fire banked low, they talked dye. It was the lesson Marta gave every apprentice in the end, and Benet gave it to Piet now, though Piet was more interested in the wax cooling in its tin than in the words.

Wax took color the way a river took a current — it carried what was given and remembered the place. Madder came from the root of a herb and gave the deep red that stained the Master of the Seal's yearly order, the red of an old wound. Weld came from a stem and gave yellow, thin and bright as morning. Iron vitriol was the mordant, the pinch of bitter metal that bit into the wax and held the color fast, and in the amber lot it did more than hold — it turned. Tanner Amber was madder and weld with a pinch of vitriol, deep amber that shifted green when the candlelight crossed it. That green was the vitriol talking.

'The bloom,' said Benet, turning the cooling tin so the lamplight fell across the surface. A frost was creeping over the top of the wax, a cloudy pattern like breath on a window. 'Every lot blooms its own way. The heat of the pour, the pace of the cooling, the weather of the day — the bloom keeps them all.' He ran a thumb across the frosted skin. 'A man who knows a shop's wax can tell the lot by its bloom the way he'd tell a face from a crowd.'

Beneath the bloom, in the body of the wax, lay the small betrayals: bubbles, where the pour had been hurried or the fire too hot — a bubble was a weak place in a seal, a place where the wax would crack and a lie could get in. And the cooling cracks, fine as hair, that traced the wax's slow stiffening. Each crack was a word the wax had said about the day it was poured: how hot, how fast, how cold the room.

'And the smell.' Benet lifted the tin and breathed. Oak and rosemary, the shop's rendering fire, baked into the wax so that the lot carried the place and the season. 'A lot is its recipe and its day and its maker, all in one piece of wax. No two are alike. You cannot forge a dye any more than you can forge a face.'

Piet nodded without really hearing. But the words were for later, and Benet knew it; they had been spoken to him once, in this same room, and he had not heard them either until the day he needed them.

The test lot cooled, bloomed, and settled. Benet ran his thumb across it and knew the day it had been poured. The wax remembered. It always remembered.

Chapter 5: Benet's Work

Benet's day was a journeyman's day, and he loved every hour of it. He poured the small lots first, plain beeswax for the chandlers' trade, because a shop that made the city's finest wax also made the plain sort, and the plain sort paid for the fire. He watched each pour, judged the heat by the way the wax ran, caught the one tin where a bubble had hidden itself at the surface and set it aside to be remelted — a bubble was a weak place, and a weak place in a seal was a lie waiting to happen.

Then came the practice seals. The shop kept a worn matrix, its device long since softened by a thousand blows, and Benet worked through the afternoon the way he had worked every afternoon for seven years: wax to the board, matrix down, one strike, lift. He did not strike twice. That was the rule of the shop, and it was the rule of the Master of the Seal, whose single-strike seals every guildsman in the city could have picked from a hundred — one clean blow, never a second. If the first blow came up wrong, you scraped the wax and began again. You did not patch.

Benet had studied the Master's seals for years, in the wax that passed through the shop — the dark red yearly order, pressed always in that one clean way. He had learned a man's hand from the marks he left in hot wax, and he had modeled his own blows on Aldric's.

In the last of the afternoon light he struck one final seal, clean, and held it up. The impression stood sharp and true, no blur at the rim, no second edge. Old Marta had come out from the desk and stood watching him with her arms folded.

'You are ready,' she said, 'for your master-piece.'

The words came at him like a blow to the chest. He was seven and twenty, a journeyman since he was nineteen, and he had waited for those words until he had stopped expecting them.

Marta nodded once, as if she had settled something. 'Tomorrow we pour the Guild's lot. Yours. Your dye, your pour, your name on the entry. Then we will speak of the master-piece.'

She turned back to the shop. Benet stood a moment, the practice matrix still warm in his hand, and struck one more seal for the pure joy of it — once, clean, the honest hand that strikes once.

At dusk he lit the lantern and set it over the sign. The city went dark around the flame. The rendering fire settled into its low breathing, and the shop smelled of oak and rosemary and the long day's wax, and Benet went to his bed thinking of the morning's pour and the words he had waited eleven years to hear.

Chapter 6: The Death

They rose before light to pour the Guild's lot. The rendering fire was fed first, the oldest habit of the shop, and by the time the first gray showed over the rooftops the wax was running clear and the dyes were set out in their vials — madder, weld, the pinch of vitriol. Benet worked the pour, his hands steady, and Marta watched, and the amber came up exactly as it always did: deep, shifting green where the flame crossed it, and the bloom began to frost over the surface as it cooled.

Marta cut a shaving from the cooling lot, thin as a petal, and pasted it into the ledger beside the fresh ink. The entry was dated the 13th of Frost.

And then the door opened, and Coben stood in it.

Coben was the Master of the Seal's boy, fourteen years old, and his face was the color of tallow. He stood in the doorway and opened his mouth and closed it, and Marta crossed the shop to him before Benet could move.

'Master Aldric,' Coben said. 'He fell. On the stairs of his own house. I found him.'

The words lay in the warm air of the shop like stones. Physician Rosk had been sent for; the watch had come; Gilders' Row had gone quiet. A fall, the city said. Apoplexy, said the older men, nodding. An old man's end, in the night, on his own stairs. The Master of the Seal, who had held the city's seals in trust for thirty years, who struck once and never twice, who read a man's worth in his face and was feared and respected for it — gone in the dark on the 12th of Frost.

The shop poured on in silence, because a standing order cannot wait, and wax waits for no man's grief. The amber lot cooled in its tin; the bloom set; the shaving lay in the ledger beside the date. Benet looked at the new wax, the signature color of the shop, and thought of the dark red that would never be ordered again — the Master's own mixture, made yearly by the Wax Light, every winter for thirty years.

Piet asked a small question about the funeral and was answered with a look. Nella came across the lane at dusk with bread, and would not take payment, and stood a moment looking at the light in the window.

At dark Marta lit the lantern and set it in its place, and the honest flame burned over the lane while the city mourned. Benet stood in the door a long time. Somewhere across the city, in a house on Gilders' Row, a dead man's papers lay folded and unsorted, and among them a letter no one had yet thought to look at, with its seal turned to the wall.

Chapter 7: The Unbroken Seal

Two days after the death, the Guild summoned the shop. Marta and Benet walked the cold streets to the Guildhall and were shown to a small room off the long hall, where the dead Master's papers had been spread across the table for sorting.

Warden Haverel sat at the head, slow and heavy, his face a mask of the Guild's sorrow and the Guild's care. Steward Sorn hovered at his shoulder, wringing decent hands. Clerk Nessim stood with the arrival ledger open on his arm, the ledger that was his conscience and his glory, and beside him, easy in the room as oil on water, sat Treasurer Veyle — silver-tongued, open-palmed, twelve years the Guild's favorite, present out of courtesy to the shop's craft.

'The Master's effects,' said Haverel, 'must be settled. His letters returned, his books closed, his seal and matrices given into the city's keeping. But there is one letter we cannot settle, and you are the ones to tell us why.'

He lifted it from the table. It lay in his broad hand, folded, addressed to the Guild, closed with a seal of wax — and the seal was amber.

'The Master's wax,' said Haverel, 'was always dark red. His own mixture, made yearly at your shop. Every man in this hall could pick his seal from a hundred. This is not red.'

Benet looked at the seal, and the warmth went out of the room.

It was Tanner Amber. The shop's signature lot, deep and frosted, the green shift showing at the rim where the lamp caught it, and if he had been allowed to bring it to his nose he knew he would have smelled oak and rosemary. It was his own work. It was Lot 14. It had been poured the morning of the 13th — the morning after the Master died.

'Well?' said Haverel.

Benet did not answer. He was aware of Veyle watching him, the Treasurer's smile smooth and unreadable, a man who had built a life on making rooms comfortable.

'I should like to hold it,' Benet said, and his voice came out steady. 'To look closer. The shop knows its own wax, Warden, better than any eye in the city.'

Marta said nothing, but he felt her stillness behind him, fierce and loyal, and he knew she had seen the same thing he had seen and trusted him to speak of it in the right time.

Haverel held out the letter. Benet took it in both hands, careful as a man taking up a wounded bird, and the amber seal turned its face toward him, hiding what lay beneath it.

Chapter 8: The Letter

Benet held the dead Master's letter and did not move.

The seal was amber, deep and frosted, and the bloom across its surface was the bloom of the shop's own pour — he had watched that frost gather over the cooling tin on the morning of the 13th. He brought it near his nose, and the smell rose: oak and rosemary, the rendering fire, the lot's own signature. And beneath that, faint, another smell — candle smoke. The seal had been made over a household candle, not at a rendering fire. A seal poured at the shop carried the shop's fire in its wax; this one carried the smoke of a private flame, burning in some dark room, on some night it was never meant to have been made.

He turned the letter in his hands. The paper was creased along the fold, and creased again — the hinge of the fold gone soft and tired, as if the sheet had been opened and folded a second time after the first setting. A letter folded once and sealed once stays creased once. This paper had been folded twice in its life. Twice.

And then he found what he had been looking for, and his hands went very still.

Beneath the amber seal, at its edge, where the new wax had been laid down over an older surface, the paper carried the ghost of the old seal. A dark red stain, oily and leached into the fiber, the shadow of a seal that had sat there long before the amber — a seal of the Master's own dark red, his yearly mixture, the color that every guildsman in the city knew.

Two seals had been on this paper. The first, dark red, in the Master's hand, in the Master's time. The second, amber, Lot 14, poured after he was dead. The amber had been laid down over the scar of the red, the way a man lays new wax over a wound, and beneath it the wound still showed.

Benet stood in the little room with the letter in his hands and understood, cold and clear, that the unbroken seal was a lie. The letter had been sealed in red, opened, read — and re-sealed in amber, by a hand that meant the world to see an unbroken seal and believe no one had read it. The unbroken seal was a lie that told the truth. The wax remembered.

He did not yet know whose hand. He looked up and found Veyle watching him, the Treasurer's smile smooth and patient, and something in Benet's stillness made the smile flicker and hold.

'I should like,' said Benet carefully, 'to compare this wax against the lot ledger, at the shop. With your leave.'

He set the letter down on the table, gently, the way a man sets down a wounded thing, and said nothing about the two stains. Not yet. Not here. But he knew the lie was in his craft, and the maker of the lie was in this room.

Chapter 9: The Guild

The Guildhall convened the next day over the dead Master's effects, and the long table filled with the voices of the Guild — and the Guild was never a single voice. Fear sat beside avarice; avarice sat beside decency; and over all of them Warden Haverel presided, slow and heavy, determined to hear every man and decide last.

Clerk Nessim read from the arrival ledger, and his voice was the voice of a man reading scripture. The letter had arrived the night of the 11th of Frost, brought by Coben, the Master's boy, delivered to the Guildhall, entered in the ledger by the clerk's own hand before the night was out. Nessim set the book down and smoothed its page, and the room understood that the record would stand whatever they decided — the ledger was the only honest thing in the argument, and Nessim would not let it be moved.

The argument was over the reading. Some wanted the dead Master's papers sealed and returned to his kin, untouched, an old man's private words left private. Some wanted them read in the open, so that nothing could be hidden. Some wanted the whole matter quiet, and those voices were the loudest and the lowest, the ones that looked at their shoes.

Steward Sorn, decent and timid, suggested the letter might wait for the spring. Someone laughed. The laugh died.

And then Treasurer Veyle rose, smooth as oil, and the room stilled, because Veyle had a way of making the room still. He spoke of the dead Master with feeling, of thirty years of single-strike seals and honest service, of the city's trust in the seal and the seal fund. And then, gently, generously, he proposed that the letter be read at the formal gathering, before the whole Guild, in the light, so that the dead Master's last words to his Guild were honored in the open and nothing was left to whisper in corners.

It sounded like courage. It sounded like grief. Benet watched him and thought of the two stains, and the smoothness of the man's face did not reach his eyes.

From the back of the room, where the shop had been placed among the witnesses, Benet told Marta in a low voice what he had seen — the red ghost under the amber, the shop's own lot laid over a dead man's seal. Marta said nothing for a long moment. Then she set her jaw.

'My father,' she said, 'lost his name to a seal that lied. This one will not.' And she folded her hands and looked straight ahead, as if she had already given her testimony.

The formal gathering was set for the next day, at the hour of the candle, when the lamps would be lit and the whole Guild would be in its seats. The dead Master's letter would be broken open and read aloud.

Benet walked home through the dark quarter with the smell of oak and rosemary in his nose and the two stains behind his eyes, and he did not sleep well. In the morning he would stand before the Guild and say what the wax had told him.

Chapter 10: The Reading

At the hour of the candle the Guild was in its seats, and the long hall was a field of faces — the wardens at the table, the masters in their rows, the clerks and the witnesses in the gallery. Nessim stood at the Warden's right with the ledger, and the lamps had been lit all down the hall, because a reading was a public thing.

They began with the Master's effects. Nessim went through the inventory of the dead man's papers — the letters of condolence, the charter drafts, the old correspondence — and the hall listened with the patience of a hall waiting for something else. Then the letter was produced. Nessim read its registration: arrived the night of the 11th of Frost, brought by Coben, the Master's boy, entered by the clerk's hand. The letter lay on the table before the Warden.

Warden Haverel took it up. He turned it in his broad hands, and he looked at the seal, and the hall saw him look.

He held the letter up so that the lamps caught the wax. Amber. The seal that closed the dead Master's letter to his Guild was amber, deep and frosted, green at the rim — and every man in the hall knew that the Master of the Seal had never once in thirty years pressed his wax in any color but the dark red the Wax Light made for him, his own mixture, ordered yearly, known to the whole city.

The murmur began at the back and ran forward. Haverel silenced it with a look.

'The shop made this city's wax,' he said. 'The shop knew the Master's wax and the Master's hand. I would hear the shop speak to this seal before I break it.'

He looked out over the hall. 'The Wax Light.'

Benet felt Marta's hand on his sleeve, and then her hand falling away. The whole hall had turned. He was called to the table.

He walked the length of the hall with the eyes of the Guild on him, and every step said the thing he had not yet said aloud. At the table the letter lay unbroken, the amber seal turned to the lamps, hiding beneath it the scar of the old red seal — the wound under the new wax, the lie the wax had been made to tell.

Haverel set the letter down in front of him. 'Tell me,' he said, 'what wax this is, and whose hand pressed it.'

Benet looked at the seal, and the room held its breath.

'The seal,' he said, 'is the shop's work. It is Lot 14 of our ledger, the amber lot, poured the morning of the 13th of Frost — the morning after the Master died.'

He heard the room go still around him, and went on, because there was no going back now:

'And it was not pressed by the Master's hand. The Master struck once, clean, and never twice. This seal was struck twice — once, and again, a hair off.'

The Warden's hand had not moved. In the silence, the letter lay between them, unbroken, and Benet understood that the reading of it would change the city — but the seal had already told him what the reading would say.

Chapter 11: The Denunciation

The Warden broke the seal himself. He took up the letter, and for a moment he held it, and the hall held its breath with him. Then he drew his knife along the edge where the amber lay, and the wax parted clean — too clean, Benet thought, for a seal that had never been lifted. But the thought came late, and he put it by.

The reading fell to Clerk Nessim, whose voice was small and exact, the voice of a man who had learned to make records obey him. "To the Worshipful Guild of the City," he read. "From Aldric, Master of the Seal. I, who have kept your seals these thirty years, denounce the Treasurer Orrin Veyle." And the hall grew so quiet that Benet heard the rendering fire of his own shop as though he stood beside it.

Twelve years of skimming the Seal Fund. False entries in the levy books, page and figure. The Salt Venture losses — the ones Veyle had explained away as an honest merchant's mischance — covered with the city's money, and the difference never returned. Aldric had set it all down in his even, unyielding hand: sums, dates, the names of clerks who could be made to speak.

When Nessim finished, every head in the hall turned to the Treasurer. And Veyle — Veyle rose, and spread his hands, and smiled like a man absolved of a debt.

"The seal was unbroken," he said, gently, as to a child. "You saw it yourselves. A letter sealed is a letter not read. The Master wrote it in his last days — a dying man's malice, or a sick man's fancy — and it died with him. Charges from a letter no living hand has touched are not charges at all."

Benet saw the nods move through the hall like wind through dry grain. And then he heard the whisper, low and poisonous, born at the Treasurer's elbow and passing from mouth to mouth before the Warden could call order: who made that amber wax? Who had the dye, the lot, the tin — who stood to gain by a dead Master's denunciation dissolving into air?

The journeyman who wants to be Master.

Benet's throat closed. He looked down at his hands, which knew the feel of every lot the shop had poured, and he said nothing. But under his breastbone, where the heat of the render lives, something had caught alight, and he could not put it out.

Chapter 12: The Treasurer

The hall broke apart after the reading, and the Guild did what guilds do after a scare: it made comfort of small talk and claret. Benet stayed by the door, an uninvited journeyman in a room of masters, and watched the Treasurer work the crowd.

Veyle was forty and looked a comfortable thirty. Twelve years in the Treasury had given him the walk of a man who owns every floor he stands on — not the Guildhall's long oak, but the idea of it, the way he set his heel and let the room come to him. His purse was open by habit; Benet watched him press a coin into a page's palm, lend a handkerchief to an alderman's wife, and laugh at a warden's joke as though laughter were a courtesy he owed. The Guild loved him. Why would it not? He was the face of its generosity, the proof that money could be spent and still be there.

Benet told himself to leave. His hands were shaking with a journeyman's unearned anger, and he had no business feeling it. But he stayed, and he watched, because a man who has been called a poisoner does not walk away from the room where the word was spoken.

It happened at the fourth glass. Steward Sorn, decent and timid, had found his courage in claret and steered the Treasurer into a corner. "If only the Master had brought his figures to the table," Sorn said, "instead of the page — the levy books would have answered him, surely."

The flicker. It was nothing — the smallest tightening of the mouth, there and gone, like a bubble rising through a cooling lot and breaking before the surface sets. Veyle's hand, which had been mid-gesture, found his glass; his laugh came a heartbeat late, and pitched a note too high.

"The levy books," he said, "are in order, Steward, to the last pence. I have signed them myself."

And Benet, who had poured a thousand lots and learned to read the moment when a surface lies about what sits beneath it, went home to the Wax Light with a single image burning under his closed eyes: that flicker, the crack in the amber, the place where the light had not gone through.

Chapter 13: The Proxy

The shop that evening was quiet. The rendering fire had been banked; the lot ledger lay closed on its stand; and the only light was the lantern above the sign, casting its warm round stain on the wet cobbles of Tanner Lane. Benet sat at the workbench with Marta's receipt book open before him, and Marta stood at his shoulder, saying nothing, which was her way of giving him room to make a fool of himself or a man of himself, and not knowing yet which.

"The tin," he said. "The one that left the shelf the day after the Master died."

Marta had counted the stock that morning, because the stock was hers and she counted it when the world misbehaved. There had been one tin of Lot 14 gone, sold and not yet re-melted into the standing order. She turned the receipt book toward him, and there it was, in the hand of the shop's best customer of that week: a single tin of Tanner Amber, for a private sealing, paid in silver — silver, not the running tally of the ledger — and dated the thirteenth of Frost.

"The thirteenth," Benet said. "The morning we poured it."

"After the Master was dead," said Marta. "Before the funeral."

The receipt was signed, not by a man, but by a title: J. Croke, clerk. No house, no quarter, no face. Just a clerk, a tin of wax, and the only lot in the city that no one could mistake.

They roused Piet from the loft where he slept under the spare wicks, and the boy stood shivering in his nightshirt, and he remembered at once. The man had come early, when the shop still smelled of the pour. Clerk's hands — pale, soft, with ink under the nails. A mended cuff on his left sleeve, three neat stitches in a thread that did not match. And an eager hurry about him, the way a man hurries when he is being watched and wants no one to watch him back. He had paid in silver without counting the change, which no poor clerk does.

"Did he give a name?" Benet asked.

"He signed the book." Piet scratched his head. "J. Croke, he wrote. Like he wanted it remembered, not forgot."

Joss Croke. The name lay on the bench between them like a shaving lifted from a lot — thin, pale, and in it, if you held it to the light, the whole shape of the pour.

Chapter 14: The Purchase

Benet went to the watch-house in the morning, and Constable Ruhm heard him out with the flat patience of a man who has been told a hundred strange things and found exactly one of them true. Ruhm was blunt and honest, the city's own conscience in a leather jerkin, and he did not like guild quarrels — but he liked a receipt with a date on it less.

"The thirteenth," Ruhm said, laying the page flat. "The day after the Master fell. A clerk buys one tin of the shop's best amber, for a private sealing, and pays silver to leave no trail of ink. What does a clerk seal privately, on the morning after his master's rival dies, that he cannot wait a day for?"

Benet had asked himself the same question all night. The answer sat at the back of his throat, unformed: a letter that had to be re-sealed before it was noticed missing. He did not say it yet. Saying it made it real, and making it real made a murderer of a man the whole city loved.

They walked the trace together, Ruhm in his heavy step, Benet in his lighter one. The shop, where Piet's memory held. The receipt book, where the entry lay. And then the ground they could not walk: the clerk's house, the Treasury, the Guildhall — the places where a tin of wax goes to become a lie.

At the Salt Market, Ruhm bought two cups of hot wine and set one in front of Benet. "Say it plainly," he said. "You think the letter was opened."

"I think the wax says so."

"Wax says what it's pressed to say," said Ruhm, but he said it gently.

"Wax says when it was poured," Benet answered. "A lot is a time-stamp. This tin was poured the morning after a man died, and sold that same day to a clerk who has no seal of his own worth the price of it. That is not a purchase. That is a necessity."

Ruhm finished his wine and set the cup down with a click that carried across the stall. "Then the date is the first stone," he said, "and we have a wall to build around a dead man's letter. Show me the rest of the stones, shaper, and I will carry them with you."

Benet looked at his hands, wrapped around the warm cup. They knew wax, and now they were learning paper, and dates, and the long arithmetic of a lie.

Chapter 15: The Trace

Clerk Nessim met them at the Guildhall door, and Benet saw at once why the arrival ledger was Nessim's conscience and his glory. He carried it like a child; he opened it like a prayer book; and he had already, that morning, drawn the page for the eleventh of Frost.

"The Master's letter came by his household boy, Coben, at the second hour of the night," Nessim said, his finger riding the line. "Registered. Dated. Signed by the boy and by myself." He looked up, small and exact. "It was sealed, then. I remember the red wax. The Master's own dark red."

Benet felt the cold settle in his chest. "And the letter has not left the Guildhall since?"

"It has not. It lay in the registry chest until the Warden called the reading. I brought it myself, with a second clerk as witness, and neither of us touched the seal." Nessim's voice thinned. "I would swear to that. I have sworn to that."

Ruhm turned the letter over on the table — the letter, which the Warden had surrendered to the watch at Ruhm's word. The seal was broken now, cut by the reading, but the wax remained around the blade's path: the amber, and beneath it, where the lifted seal had scraped the paper, the two stains.

"Show me," Ruhm said.

Benet bent close. He had handled this letter before, at the Warden's summons, and the sight of it had gone through him like cold water. Now he looked at it as work. "Here," he said, and his finger hovered above the first stain — a ghost, a shadow of dark red, pressed into the paper's grain and gone dull with age. "The Master's wax. It lay here a night, maybe two."

"And here." A second stain, cleaner, fainter, amber where the red had been. "The new pour. It sat shorter. It did not feed the paper the way the first did."

Ruhm studied him. "Two seals?"

"Two seals," said Benet. "One honest, one not. And here —" he turned the letter's edge to the light, where the fibers had been scraped white by a blade "— the knife that lifted the first. A hot knife leaves the paper raw and the wax greasy. The smoke of it is still in the amber, if you have the nose for smoke."

Nessim made a small sound, like a man who has just seen a column of figures refuse to balance. "Then the letter was read," he whispered. "It was read, and re-sealed, before my ledger ever called it sealed."

The chain, Benet thought, was beginning to hold: a letter registered the eleventh, a red seal that lay a night, a knife, an amber pour from the thirteenth, and a dead man between the two. Four links. He did not yet know what they held, only that they held together, and that the grip of them was closing on someone.

Chapter 16: The Lot

Back at the Wax Light, Benet opened the lot ledger where it lay on its stand under the window, and Marta came to stand at his shoulder, and together they looked at the page for the thirteenth of Frost as though it were a stranger's confession.

Lot 14. The standing order for the Guild's new-year sealing wax — the order that had been due, by the shop's own custom, for a season now, and that Aldric's death had suddenly made urgent. In the left margin, in Marta's fierce hand: the recipe. Madder and weld, and a pinch of iron vitriol, the quantities weighed and recorded, the whole brewed over the oak-and-rosemary fire until it took the color of deep amber that shifted green in candlelight. In the right margin, the witnesses: Marta, and Piet, and the day's date.

The thirteenth.

"I poured it with my own hand," Marta said. "The Master was already cold. They were laying him out in Gilders' Row, and I was here, stirring the lot that was to be his year's wax, and I did not know it until the bell." She set her palm flat on the page, as though to keep it from lifting away. "That is the honest record of it. We cannot change it, and we should not."

Benet turned the page toward the light, to where the shaving lay pasted beside the entry — a thin curl of amber, cooled and gone to bloom, its surface frosted with the pattern no other lot in the city wore. He had known it was his work the moment he saw the seal at the Warden's table. He had known it in his hands before his eyes confirmed it: the weight, the give, the faint resistance of a pour he had helped settle.

"The seal on the letter," he said slowly, "can only be Lot 14, or a later lot."

"There is no later lot," said Marta. "Nothing has been poured since. The shop has stood at the old wax all week, for the death, for the mourning — a shop does not pour while its master's year turns over in a shroud." Her voice roughened. "It would be indecent."

Benet closed the ledger, and the shaving lay hidden between the pages, a pressed witness. The timing gap was opening at his feet like a cooling crack, and he could see the two edges of it drawing apart: on one side, a letter sealed in dark red on the eleventh, written by a living man; on the other, a seal of amber poured on the thirteenth, made after the man was dead.

Between them, unaccounted for, a whole night and a murder.

Nothing in the ledger would close that gap. But the ledger, as Marta had said, was honest — and honesty, he was beginning to understand, was the one thing a lie cannot stand against.

Chapter 17: The Dye Lot

The tin stood in the back room where the finished lots were kept, on the shelf that Marta reserved for the shop's signature wax. There was a little left in it — a shallow puddle of amber gone hard, its surface risen to the frosted bloom that Tanner Amber always wore, the pattern like the breath of winter on a window. Benet lifted it down with both hands, the way a man lifts a thing he means to read, and carried it to the bench.

He did not test it. He did not need to. He set the tin beside the broken seal that Ruhm had let him take from the letter — a flake of amber, no bigger than his thumbnail, still holding the impression of a matrix in its underside — and he looked from one to the other, and his hands knew.

The bloom matched: same frost, same grain, the same fine river of bubbles caught just under the surface where the lot had cooled too slowly on the thirteenth. The color matched: that deep shifting amber, green in the candle's low flame, that no other lot in the city had ever produced, because no other lot had ever been brewed with madder and weld and the pinch of iron vitriol in exactly those measures. And the smell matched — the smell above all, the thing no dye could counterfeit. He held the flake to his nose, and there it was, faint as a memory of a fire: oak and rosemary, the shop's rendering hearth, the smoke the whole lot had been cooked over on the morning of the thirteenth.

Marta came to the bench and stood over him. "It is the same pour," she said. It was not a question.

"It is the same pour."

"The dye is a fingerprint." Marta had said it a hundred times, teaching him. "No two lots are alike. A man's hand prints his name in the wax he pours. This lot prints the shop, the date, and the fire."

Benet turned the flake over in his fingers. Somewhere, in the dark of the night between the eleventh and the thirteenth, someone had held this wax over a candle, melted it to a puddle, and pressed a matrix into it — twice, once, twice — to seal a letter that had already been sealed. That man had chosen the best wax in the city, the shop's own lot, as though the excellence of the lie would make it honest.

He thought of the two stains on the letter's fold, the ghost of the old red lying under the new amber.

"The lot does not lie," he said. "And I poured it. I was here. I can swear to the day of it, and Marta can swear to me, and Piet can swear to us both." He set the flake down gently, as though it were a live thing. "Whoever sealed that letter did not know that the wax would speak. That is his vanity. And my hand is on the pour."

Marta nodded slowly. "Then the lot is a witness," she said, "and it has been waiting, all week, for you to ask it."

Chapter 18: The Match

The match was made in the Guildhall's small room, the one with the long table and the good light, because a match is a work of the eye and must not be done by candle. Nessim brought the ledger from the Wax Light under his arm, wrapped in oilcloth as though it were the city's charter; Ruhm brought the letter and the broken seal; and Benet brought the tin, with its dregs, and his hands, and nothing else, because a witness's hands are the only instruments that cannot be accused of lying.

They laid the shaving from the ledger beside the flake from the letter, and for a long moment no one spoke.

"Bloom for bloom," Benet said at last, and drew their eyes down. "The frost on the shaving is the frost on the flake. Same grain. Same rise. A lot cools as it cools; no two cool the same; these two cooled in the same pan, the same hour, the same fire."

Nessim leaned close, and his breath made the shaving stir. "And the green?"

"The green." Benet held the candle where the window could not reach, and the two pieces of amber turned, together, the same deep green — the iron vitriol taking the light, the madder and the weld answering it. "No other lot in the city shifts that color. This is the signature of the dye. The dye is a fingerprint, and these two prints are one."

Ruhm, who had said little, laid a ruler across the table. "The letter's seal," he said, "was made of this lot."

"It was made of this lot," said Benet. "And the lot was poured the thirteenth of Frost, after the Master died."

Nessim's pen moved. It was the pen of a man who had kept the arrival ledger for thirty years, and it wrote with the same exactness it had always used, setting down the match as he would set down any entry: the date, the witnesses, the finding. When he was done, he pushed the page across to Ruhm, and Ruhm signed it with a heavy hand, and then Nessim looked at Benet.

"Your signature, shaper. You have signed for lots before."

Benet took the pen. His hand was steady, though his heart was not. He signed beneath the line that said the seal on Aldric's letter was the wax of Lot 14, poured the morning after the Master's death — and the moment the ink dried, he understood that he had signed his own life into a quarrel with the most powerful man in the city.

"The seal was made after the Master died," Nessim read back. His voice was small and stunned. "Then someone took a dead man's letter, broke it, and sealed it again in new wax — with a matrix they should never have held."

Ruhm gathered the papers. "Then we have two questions," he said, "and the first is, whose hand pressed that matrix down. And the second is, whose matrix it was."

The match was signed. The first stone of the wall was set.

Chapter 19: The Press

They could not examine the matrix itself — it lay in the Treasury vault, behind iron and the Guild's own seal — but they had the impression it had made, and an impression, Benet told himself, was a confession written in reverse.

The flake of Lot 14 had taken the die's face and kept it, like a record of a single cold moment. They turned it to the light in Ruhm's watch-house, and there it was: not the clean single strike that had been Aldric's lifelong signature, but two strikes, the second landing a hair off the first, the double line of the die's edge lying like a shadow beside itself.

"A single strike," Benet said, "is the mark of a sealer. You lay the matrix, you press once, you lift. The wax remembers one blow — clean, whole, the whole design in one piece. That is what the Master did for thirty years. You could read his letters by the strike alone and know they had not been tampered with."

"And this?" said Ruhm.

"This is two. The first blow set the design; the second — a hair to the left, a breath too hard — shifted it, and the wax has recorded the shift. The hand that pressed this seal was not a sealer's hand. It is a hand that has learned to press, but not to strike." Benet set the flake down. "A clerk's hand. An ink-stained, neat, obedient hand."

Nessim had gone very still at the table. "The Treasury keeps the Guild's copy of the Master's matrix," he said, and his voice was barely a whisper. "By right of his office, it is the Treasurer who holds it."

They found the loan book in the afternoon. It was Nessim who fetched it, from the small locked press where the Treasury's records slept, and it was Nessim who turned the pages, his finger trembling on the entries, until he came to the one dated a year past: the Guild's copy of the Master's matrix, drawn out on loan, signed for in the Treasurer's own hand, and never returned.

Veyle. The signature was unmistakable — the confident flourish, the wide-set letters, the man who signed his name the way he walked into a room.

Ruhm read it twice, then closed the book. "The press," he said, "we have not found. The bronze letter-press of the Guild is missing from the Treasury, and no one has seen it since the night the Master died. But the hand that used it —" he tapped the page "— we have found."

Benet looked at his own hands, resting on the table. They had matched a lot, read a dye, and counted two strikes in a seal no one else had troubled to count. It had not occurred to him, until that moment, how far the wax had carried him.

Chapter 20: The Timing

They set it out on Ruhm's table, the whole of it, in the order a man lays out tools before a pour: the letter, the ledger, the receipt, the match, the strike, the loan book. Six pieces. Benet had carried each of them up from the dark, and now, in the watch-house's bare light, he set them in a line and let the line speak.

"The letter," he said, "was written by Aldric on the eleventh of Frost — the draft is in his letter-books, in his own hand, dated. He sealed it in his dark red wax, his own mixture, made yearly by the Wax Light, with a single clean strike. His boy Coben carried it to the Guildhall that night, and Nessim registered it in the arrival ledger: the eleventh, the hour, the seal unbroken."

He laid his hand on the letter. "On the twelfth, Aldric died. A fall on his stairs. An old man's end, we said."

His hand moved to the flake of amber. "And on the thirteenth, after the death, the Wax Light poured Lot 14, the standing order for the Guild's new-year wax, and sold one tin that same day to a clerk named Joss Croke, who paid silver and took a receipt dated the thirteenth. That tin, and no other lot, made the seal upon this letter. The match is signed; the dye is a fingerprint; the lot is a time-stamp."

"Lay it out plainly," Ruhm said, "and it solves itself. A letter sealed in red on the eleventh, in the Master's hand, in the Master's wax. A death on the twelfth. A seal of amber, poured the thirteenth, sitting over the ghost of that red seal. The seal was made after the letter was written — after the man who wrote it died. The letter was opened, read, and re-sealed. Its unbroken seal was a lie. Someone needed the denunciation dead." He looked up. "That someone is the one the letter denounced; the hand that re-sealed it is a clerk's hand; and the matrix that hand used is the Treasurer's, signed out of the Treasury and never returned."

The silence held a long moment. Then Ruhm drew a breath that seemed to go down to his boots. "And you, shaper, have built it out of wax and paper and dates."

"It is only what the wax says," Benet said. But he heard how it sounded — a man defending himself with his own craft — and he added, because honesty was all he had: "It says it plainly, and it does not change."

Ruhm stood and took up his coat. "Then we go to the Warden tonight, and we lay it before the Guild, the way you have laid it here." He paused at the door. "The Treasurer will not thank you for this."

"I know," said Benet. "I have known it since the reading."

The lamp burned between them, honest and small, and outside the window the canal ran black under the Frost night — and somewhere in the city, a man who trusted a seal to lie for him was sleeping easy, not knowing that the wax had already given him up.

Chapter 21: Old Marta

The Guildhall had filled for the inquest, and the heat of so many bodies had begun to fog the tall windows, so that the gray day stood blurred behind them like a sea seen through wax. Marta had insisted on the shop's ledger, and she carried it up the hall herself, in both arms, as though it were a child, or a wound.

Warden Haverel gave her the table. He had given no one else the table; the witnesses had stood at the bar. But Marta was the keeper of the lot ledger, and the ledger was the thing being tried, and the old woman set it down and opened it to the page for the 13th of Frost as though she were opening a door she had locked thirty years ago.

She told them about her father first. The room had heard the story in fragments — a ruined name, a forged seal, a shop that failed — but she told it whole, in the flat voice of a woman who has carried a weight so long it has stopped seeming heavy. "I was nine," she said. "They showed the Guild a seal that said my father had signed a debt. He had not signed it. But the seal was whole, and a whole seal cannot lie, and they took his name, his shop, his standing." She looked out over the hall. "No one asked who had pressed that wax. I have asked it every day since."

Nessim's pen moved, setting it down. Benet, at the bar, kept his eyes on her hands — the hands that had taught him to pour — and saw that they were perfectly still.

"The lot ledger exists because of that seal," Marta said. "Every lot the shop has poured has its page: its number, its recipe, its date, its buyer, and a shaving of the lot pasted beside the ink, so the book can speak for the wax when the wax is gone." She turned the page. "Lot 14 was poured the morning of the 13th of Frost. I stirred it. My journeyman Benet weighed the dyes. My apprentice Piet trimmed the fire. There is no later lot. Nothing has been poured since."

She lifted her head and looked, not at the Warden, but past him, to the Treasurer's seat. "The entry for the 13th names a buyer. One tin of Tanner Amber, sold that afternoon, paid in silver, receipt signed by the buyer's clerk." Her voice did not rise. It did not need to. "Let the Treasurer deny it. Let him name the day his clerk did not stand in my shop and buy the only lot in the city no one could mistake. The book is here. The shaving is here. The wax is here."

In the silence, Benet heard the Treasurer's chair creak, and he did not look, because he did not want to see the smile. He watched Marta instead, the fierce old woman who had carried her father's ruined name for fifty years and had chosen, at last, to spend it on the truth.

"Read the book," she said. "The book does not lie. Wax lies only when a man makes it lie, and this time the man will be caught at it."

Chapter 22: The Master's Tale

They heard the dead Master's story next, and the telling of it fell to Benet, because the shop had made Aldric's wax for thirty years and knew the man in his work the way no ledger could. He began with the seals. "The Master struck once," he said, "and never twice. That was not a habit; it was a creed. A single clean blow — the matrix set, the wax pressed once, the hand lifted. If the first blow came up wrong, he scraped the wax and began again. He never patched. You could read a man's honesty in the strike, he said, and he meant to be the most honest hand in the city."

Every guildsman had held a charter closed by Aldric's dark red seal and known, by the clean single strike, that it had not been touched. But they had not heard it said as Benet said it now, as the description of a man's character rather than a craft.

He told them the rivalry next — the story of two young men who had come up in the Guild together, one born to money and smoothness, one born to ink and patience. "They were friends once," Benet said. "Steward Sorn remembers it. But Veyle rose by charm, and Aldric rose by scrutiny, and a man who rises by scrutiny looks at the ledgers of the man who rose by charm." He said it plainly, without looking toward the Treasurer's seat. "That is the whole of their quarrel."

And then the audit. Aldric had gone into the Seal Fund at the turn of the year, as his office gave him the right to do, and had come out with the figures that lay in his letter — the twelve years of skimming, the false levy entries, the Salt Venture losses covered and never returned. He had not accused anyone in public; that was not his way. He had written it down, sealed it in his dark red wax, struck once, clean, and sent it to the Guild by his boy's hand, trusting the record to do what he could not do alive.

"They said he was a cold man," Benet said. "And it is true: the city feared his reading of men, because it was usually right. But he was only keeping the account. Thirty years of single-strike seals are thirty years of a man refusing to lie about himself. He feared nothing except a wrong figure, and that fear is the only fear that has ever kept a city honest."

He paused. In the silence he could hear the rain begin against the high windows. "The case has taught us what there was to fear for. It was never Aldric we should have feared. It was the man who needed him dead rather than heard."

He stepped back from the table, and the hall sat with the shape of the dead man finally filling the room — not the cold old sealer of the whispers, but the one honest reader of the books, whose last act had been to write the truth down and send it where it could not be buried with him.

Chapter 23: The Apprentice

They called Piet to the bar, and the boy stood a head shorter than the railing, so that he had to look up at the Warden like a lad at a market gate. Benet had schooled him in the morning: speak to the hall, not to your hands; say what you saw, not what you think. But Piet, seeing the whole Guild in its rows, forgot the schooling and told the truth the way he had told it in the shop, which was better.

"He came early," Piet said, "when the shop still smelled of the pour. A clerk's hands — pale, soft, ink under the nails. There was a mended cuff on his left sleeve, three stitches in a thread that did not match the cloth. He bought one tin of the amber, paid silver, and did not count the change, which no poor clerk does."

Nessim read back the name. "J. Croke." The hall murmured, because the name was the Treasurer's clerk, and the date on the receipt was the 13th, the morning after the Master died.

Benet had known it would land like that. What he had not known was that Coben was in the gallery.

Coben was Aldric's boy, fourteen years old, the first to find the body and the first to be told he had dreamed it. He had stood pale in the shop doorway on the morning of the 13th; he had carried the letter to the Guildhall on the night of the 11th; and the dead Master's household had kept him out of the inquest, because a boy who has found a body should not have to speak of it. But he came down from the gallery now, and he would not be turned back.

"I did not dream it," Coben said. His voice shook, but he planted his feet and let it carry. "The night my master died, I heard the door. I came down the stair and I saw him — the Treasurer — at the door of the study, closing it behind him. I heard the fall. I opened my mouth, and he turned and looked at me, and he said, 'You dreamed it, boy. Go back to your bed. There was no one here but you.'"

The hall was stone. Coben's face worked. "I believed him. I believed him for a week, because a boy believes what a Treasurer tells him, and because I wanted the sound of the fall to be a dream. It was not a dream. It was my master, falling, and the man who made him fall standing over him in the dark."

He looked at Veyle, and the look was the look of a boy who had been robbed of the man who raised him.

"I know what the letter was, now," Coben said. "The one I carried on the night of the 11th. And if my master had lived, he would have had the courage to say what I am saying. He struck once and never twice. I will say it once. It was him. It was him at the door."

In the silence, the Treasurer's smile had gone.

The night of the 12th of Frost was no longer a fall.

Chapter 24: The Trial of the Wax

The next day the Guild sat as an inquest over the wax itself, and the wax — Benet understood it with a cold, clear certainty — was on trial.

Veyle rose to open the case for the defense, the way he did everything: smoothly, generously, the whole hall leaning in to hear him. He spoke of his grief for the Master, of his twelve years of service to the Seal Fund, of his love for the Guild. He thanked the shop for its honesty — beyond question, he said, and that was the pity of it. And then he turned, gently, to the journeyman at the bar.

"Benet of the Wax Light." He said the name as though he were introducing a friend. "A fine craftsman. Ready, they say, for his master-piece — and a master-piece, in this city, is a step toward the Mastership of the Seal. Is that not the ladder a journeyman climbs?"

Benet heard the whisper move through the hall like wind through dry grain: the journeyman who wants to be Master. The words he had heard at the reading, and the poison had found its shape.

"The letter that denounces me," Veyle said, "carries a seal of the shop's own new lot — poured, by the shop's own ledger, the morning after the Master died. A color the Master never used in thirty years. Struck twice, in a hand the shop itself has called unskilled." He spread his hands. "Who had the dye? Who had the tin? Who knew the wax well enough to forge a seal that would be believed? Not the Treasurer of this Guild, who has never poured a lot in his life. The man who made the wax."

The hall turned. Benet felt the weight of a hundred faces, and among them the ones that had begun to harden — the masters who had always suspected the upstart, the wardens who would rather blame a journeyman than a Treasurer, the frightened ones who wanted the whole thing to be a craftsman's ambition and not a murder.

"Stand before the Guild," Veyle said, "and tell them it was not you. Tell them your lot did not seal a dead man's letter. Tell them the hand that struck twice was not the hand that poured the wax." He smiled, and the smile was kind. "The crime, you will have to prove, is someone else's."

He sat. The Warden turned his heavy face to the bar. "Benet of the Wax Light. The Treasurer accuses you of forging the dead Master's letter and sealing it with your own craft, to slander the Guild and seize the Mastership. You stand accused of your own craft's honesty." He paused. "How do you answer?"

Benet stood in the heat of a hundred bodies and the cold of his own knowledge. He thought of his hands, which had poured the lot that was stolen to make the lie. He thought of Marta's father, ruined by a seal. He thought of the two stains, the double strike, the loan book, the press gone into the canal.

"I answer," he said, "with the wax."

And he went to the table, and began to lay the evidence out in order, the way a man lays out tools before a pour.

Chapter 25: The Evidence

They cleared the table of everything but the letter, and Benet worked in the light of the hall's tall windows, setting each piece down and naming it, because a case was a pour and every pour needed its order.

"The letter," he said. "Written by Aldric on the night of the 11th of Frost, in his letter-books, in his own hand, dated — Clerk Nessim has verified the pages. Sealed in his dark red wax, his yearly mixture, made by the Wax Light for thirty years, and struck once, clean, as he always struck. His boy Coben carried it to the Guildhall that night; Nessim registered it in the arrival ledger: the 11th, the hour, the seal unbroken."

He laid his hand on the paper. "On the 12th, Aldric died. A fall, we said. Physician Rosk is here, and he will speak to the wound."

He lifted the letter and turned it to the window, so the light fell through the place where the seal had been. "And here is the first lie the wax tells us. Beneath this seal lie two stains. The first, a ghost of dark red — the Master's own wax, which lay here a night, feeding the paper. The second, amber, sitting over it, poured later, pressing the scar of the red into the fiber. Two seals have been on this paper."

He let the hall look. Nessim's pen moved, and the silence was total.

"The lot ledger." He set the book down open. "Lot 14 was poured the morning of the 13th — Marta, Piet, and I witnessed it. It was the shop's standing order for the Guild's new year. There is no later lot. The seal on the letter is Lot 14, matched bloom for bloom, grain for grain, in the signed match Clerk Nessim will read to you. A lot is a time-stamp. The seal was made after the Master died."

"The receipt." He laid it flat. "One tin of Tanner Amber, sold the 13th, paid in silver, signed by the buyer's clerk — Joss Croke, the Treasurer's own clerk, named by the apprentice who served him."

"The strike." He set the flake down, its underside to the light. "Not Aldric's single clean blow. Two strikes, the second a hair off. A clerk's hand — a hand that has learned to press, not to strike."

"And the matrix." Nessim produced the loan book, opened to the page. "The Guild's copy of the Master's matrix, drawn out on loan and signed for in the Treasurer's own hand — a year ago, and never returned. The matrix that made this seal."

He stepped back. "That is all. No rhetoric. Wax, paper, ink, and dates — six pieces, and each one dated. The letter, the 11th. The death, the 12th. The lot, the 13th. The receipt, the 13th. The loan book, a year past. And the two stains, which carry no date at all — only the record, in the paper's own grain, that one seal came first and one came after."

He met Veyle's eye for the first time. "The wax does not accuse. It only remembers. And it has remembered in front of you all."

Chapter 26: The Test

The Warden looked at the six pieces and then at Benet, and Benet saw that a demonstration would be required — that a room of merchants and masters would not be moved by argument, however exact, but only by seeing the lie made before their own eyes, by the craft they distrusted.

"Show us," Haverel said. "Show us how it was done."

The Guildhall had no rendering fire, so they brought a candle — a tall household candle, the kind that stands on a desk in a dark room — and a sheet of clean paper, and the shop's own dark red wax, and a fresh hot knife from the kitchen, and the worn practice matrix the shop used for lessons. Benet set them out on the cleared table, and the hall leaned forward as one body.

He wrote, with Nessim's pen and ink, a single harmless line on the paper, and folded it as a letter is folded, and ran the dark red wax along the fold, and struck the practice matrix into it once, clean. He held it up. The seal stood sharp and whole, the honest single blow.

"That is how the Master sealed his letter on the night of the 11th," Benet said. "Now watch what was done to it."

He took up the hot knife and worked its edge under the seal, lifting the wax away from the paper in one patient, careful motion — the way a thief lifts a seal, he thought, though he did not say it. Beneath, the paper showed the stain where the red wax had fed the fiber, dark and oily, the scar of the first seal. The hall saw it. Someone drew a breath.

He opened the folded paper along its crease, read his sentence aloud, and folded the paper again — and they saw the crease fall back on itself, gone soft, the way a letter's fold softens when it has been opened once and closed again.

Then he took the amber from the lot tin, melted it over the candle — the thin smell of candle smoke rose against the hall's still air — and laid it down over the scar of the red, and pressed the matrix into it twice: once, clean, and again, a hair off. He held the finished letter up to the window.

The ghost of the dark red seal lay beneath the amber. Faint, but there — a wound under new wax, a scar no sealing could hide. And beneath the amber's frosted bloom, the double line of the double strike.

"This letter," Benet said, "has never been opened. Its seal is unbroken. And yet I have read it, and you have all watched me read it." He set it down gently. "The unbroken seal was never proof that a letter was safe. It was only proof that no one had looked too closely. The Master looked closely. That is why he is dead."

The room did not move. The candle burned between the two letters — the one sealed once, honestly, and the one sealed twice, dishonestly — and Benet let them look at both.

"The scar proves the wound," he said. "The letter was opened. And the man who opened it could not leave it open."

Chapter 27: The Re-seal

The candle still burned on the table when the Warden asked him to do it again, slowly, and this time to name every step, so that the whole of the technique lay open to the hall like a ledger page.

Benet took up the practice letter — the one with the double strike — and showed them the seam where the lifted seal had been. "The first thing the re-sealer did was free the old wax. You cannot tear a seal off; it cracks, and a cracked seal is a questioned seal. So he used a hot knife, as I did. The heat softens the wax where it meets the paper. It lifts whole and leaves the paper raw beneath, the wax greasy with the paper's own oils."

He turned the paper to the light. "The old seal left its stain. The red wax lay here a night, maybe two — long enough to feed its oil deep into the paper, so that no scraping will lift it. That stain is the first scar. It cannot be removed. It can only be covered."

He showed them the crease. "The letter was read. When a letter is folded, sealed, and left, the fold sets like a cooled pour. Open it once and fold it again, and the hinge goes soft — the paper remembers being folded twice. The letter was folded twice in its life. Once, it was sealed. Twice, it was read."

He melted the amber over the candle, and the thin smoke of the household flame rose. "Now the new wax. The re-sealer had the tin of Lot 14 — the best wax in the city, the lot no one could mistake. He thought that was its virtue. He was wrong. It was its doom. He laid it over the scar, thinking he was hiding the wound, and pressed the matrix down — twice."

He pressed it twice again, the second strike a hair off the first. "A sealer strikes once. That is the whole art: the matrix set, the wax pressed, the hand lifted. The re-sealer's hand was a clerk's hand. It had learned to press, not to strike. It landed once, looked at the work, did not trust it, and pressed again — and the second blow, a hair to the left, is in the wax forever. No two strikes land the same."

He lifted the matrix and held the letter to the light. "And the smell. The Master's seals were poured at the rendering fire and carried the smoke of oak and rosemary in their wax — the shop's own fire. This seal, poured over a household candle in a dark room, carries the smoke of that candle. I have held it to my nose."

He set the letter down. "So the unbroken seal was broken, and remade, and the remaking left its trace at every step: the raw edge of the knife, the two stains, the softened fold, the double strike, the candle smoke. Each is small. Each is certain. And all of them together are the confession the wax has carried since the 13th of Frost. The unbroken seal was broken the moment we learned to read it."

Chapter 28: The Proof

They asked Nessim to set down the finding, because the finding was a record, and a record was Nessim's trade. He sat at the end of the long table with his pen and ink and his terrible exactness, and the hall watched him write as though watching a sentence carried out.

The Warden dictated, and the clerk's pen moved. "On the testimony of the Wax Light, and the match signed by three witnesses, the seal upon the letter of Aldric, Master of the Seal, was the wax of Lot 14, poured the morning of the 13th of Frost."

The pen scratched. "The Master's letter was written and sealed on the night of the 11th, in his own dark red wax, in his own hand, and registered in the arrival ledger by Clerk Nessim that same night. The Master died on the night of the 12th. The seal upon his letter was therefore made after the letter was written — and after the man who wrote it was dead."

The hall was so still that Benet heard the scratch of the nib and the rain. Nessim paused and looked up at the Warden, and Haverel nodded, and the pen went on.

"The letter was opened and read, and re-sealed, after Aldric's death. The two stains beneath the seal, the raw edge of the knife, the softened fold, the double strike, and the candle-smoke scent in the wax are the evidence of that, and no witness has contradicted them."

The pen stopped. Nessim read the finding back, and the words fell on the hall like the last stones of a wall being set.

"Therefore the accusation brought against Benet of the Wax Light is withdrawn. The journeyman did not forge the letter, and did not forge the seal. The letter is the dead Master's own; the seal is the shop's honest work, stolen to make a lie look honest."

Benet stood at the bar and heard his own name cleared, and found that the relief did not come. He was not thinking of his name. He was thinking of the line Nessim had not written yet, because it could not be written yet.

Warden Haverel rose, and the heavy slowness of him was suddenly the gravity of the room, pulling every face toward the Treasurer's seat.

"The seal was made after the death. The matrix that made it was drawn from the Treasury on loan, in the hand of the Treasurer of this Guild, and never returned. The hand that pressed it was a clerk's hand. The clerk who bought the wax was the Treasurer's own clerk, on the morning after the death, by a receipt dated the 13th. And a boy of this city has sworn that he saw the Treasurer close the dead Master's door on the night the Master fell."

He let the words stand. No one moved. The hall turned, slowly, the way a heavy door turns on its hinge — masters, wardens, clerks, and witnesses, all of them looking at last at the man who had sat among them, smooth and open-palmed, while his clerk's receipt and his signed loan and his double strike were laid out one by one.

The Treasurer's smile had not yet gone. But it had begun, very faintly, to cost him something.

Chapter 29: The Accusation

The hall had turned, but no one had spoken the name. That was the last service the room would do the Treasurer — the courtesy of waiting for someone else to say it. And Benet, who had carried the wax this far, found that he was the one the silence pointed at.

He had not meant to speak. He had meant the evidence to speak. But the evidence had done its work, and the name still hung unspoken, and a man does not carry a thing that far and then set it down at the edge of the table.

"Treasurer Veyle," Benet said.

The words were quiet. They carried down the hall like a single-strike seal — once, clean, no second blow.

"The tin was bought by your clerk, on the morning after the death, for a private sealing. A clerk seals nothing privately." He kept his eyes on the Treasurer. "The matrix that made the seal was signed out of the Treasury in your hand, a year ago, and never returned — Nessim's loan book does not lie. The seal was struck twice, a hair off — a clerk's hand, not a sealer's. And the two stains beneath it prove the letter was opened and read between the Master's death and the reading before this Guild."

He took a breath. "And on the night of the 12th of Frost, the bronze letter-press of this Guild was taken from the Treasury — the press engraved with the Guild arms, reachable only by the Treasurer's keys. And that night the Master died of a blow no stair-fall could have made."

Veyle rose. For the first time in twelve years, the smoothness was not smooth. The smile was there, but it was working — a muscle holding a position it was not trained to hold.

"You accuse me," he said, and the voice was still silver, but the silver had begun to tarnish. "A journeyman accuses the Treasurer of this Guild of murder, on a clerk's receipt and a boy's dream."

"Coben did not dream it," Benet said. "You told him he dreamed it, and he believed you, because a boy believes what a Treasurer tells him. He does not believe it now. I have seen your double strike with my own eyes. I have smelled your candle smoke in the wax. I have read the two stains you never read, because you trusted the seal to lie for you."

Veyle laughed.

It was a good laugh — Benet would give him that. It rang through the hall, easy and generous, the laugh of a man dismissing a child's tale. And then it failed. It ran on a beat too long, and the hall heard the join, the place where the laughter had become a performance and the performance had run out of road. The smile held a moment longer, and then it did not, and the room saw, underneath, what twelve years of silver had been covering.

"The press," Veyle said, and his voice had gone flat. "You have no press."

"We have your hand," said Benet. "We have your clerk. We have your matrix. And the canal is a long water — everything that has ever gone into it has come out again."

Chapter 30: The Treasurer's Fall

They found the press on the morning of the third day.

Ruhm had taken the canal in lengths, from the Weir down to the salt flats, dragging with hooks and nets and the patience of a man told a thing is there who intends to prove it. The divers went down in the black water where the bridge shadows lay and the silt holds what the current wants to keep. They came up on the third morning with the Guild's bronze letter-press, wrapped in a heavy hemmed linen, and on the corner of the press a fresh chip where the metal had struck stone.

The linen was brought to the Guildhall, and the chip matched against the stair-slate of Aldric's house, where the fall had been supposed to happen. They fitted the bronze corner into the scar in the slate the way a man fits a key, and it went in, and no one spoke.

Physician Rosk had been waiting for them. He had examined the dead Master on the morning of the 13th, when the city still said apoplexy, and had kept his doubts to himself because doubts were not evidence. Now the evidence had come to him, and he set down the wound in plain words: the blow to the temple was no stair-fall. A fall bruises at the angle of impact and the body's own falling; this was a single heavy blow, struck from above, with the hardness of bronze. A letter-press could have made it.

And then the linen did its last work. It was Veyle's household linen — the Treasurer's own mark worked into the hem, the mark every house in the city used at the common washing troughs. It had wrapped the press that killed the Master and gone into the canal with it, and it had come up again weighted with bronze, holding the truth in its folds.

Ruhm brought the Treasurer out of the Guildhall that afternoon. Veyle went without resistance, the last and most honest thing he did — a man who has built his life on smoothness does not struggle; he lets the room do the believing. He walked between the watchmen down the long hall, and the Guild stood, and no one moved to help him, and at the door he stopped and looked back.

"Seals are for keeping secrets," he said. "And wax is only wax."

Benet stood at the bar and did not answer.

The door closed. The watch took him down, and the hall was quiet, and Nessim sat at the end of the long table and began, with terrible exactness, to write the record of the day.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The canal ran black and patient under the Frost sky, and somewhere above the leather quarter, in the dusk, the lantern of the Wax Light was being lit — the honest flame that never went out, burning over the lane while the whole city drew breath again.

The wax had given up its last lie.

Chapter 31: The Confession

The Guildhall vault was a cellar of kept things. Along one wall stood the retired seals of dead offices, bronze faces wrapped in oiled cloth; against the other, the ledgers of a hundred years in iron racks. They set a chair in the middle of it, and a single lamp, and they brought Veyle down the steps to sit in it. His coat was still pressed from the morning of his arrest, and nothing in his bearing had surrendered since.

Ruhm left them alone at the table with Nessim's pen and a blank page, and the Treasurer looked at Benet as a man looks at a ledger he has never had to balance. You know what I am, he said. Not what the Guild thought. I was its convenience, not its favorite.

He told it as a man tells a thing he has long rehearsed. The Salt Venture had been his first year in office, a consignment of dyestuffs pledged on the Guild's credit, lost to the Weir when the barge went over. The loss was real and it was his, and so he had covered it, one line at a time, one false entry, one shaving of the Seal Fund, until covering had become a trade in itself and the trade had lasted twelve years.

Aldric had found it in the autumn. Thirty years of reading men, and one long night with the Fund's ledgers. The letter, the figures, the denunciation — Veyle had read it with a hot knife, the night of the eleventh, the seal lifting clean off the paper as if it had never meant to stay. And on the twelfth he had walked up Gilders' Row with the Guild's bronze press wrapped in a cloth, because a dead accuser is a paper you can set aside.

He did not deny the re-seal. He almost seemed proud of it. His hand was a clerk's, he said, and the matrix was a borrowed thing, and yet the seal had held — the whole city had walked past it for a fortnight and read only what it was meant to read. Seals are for keeping secrets, he said, and wax is only wax. That was the vanity at the center of him, undimmed by the vault or the lamp or the pen: an unbroken seal is proof no one has read it. No one questions an unbroken seal.

Benet sat with his hands flat on the table. He could have said what he knew: that wax remembers, that the two stains beneath the amber were the scar under a bandage, that a seal is a signature no thief can borrow and return. But the Treasurer was already confessing to a chair and a lamp, and a man who trusts the seal to lie for him will not hear the truth of what the seal keeps.

When Veyle had done, he asked for the wax. A small thing, he said, for a man who will not see winter. Benet looked at the blank page, at Nessim's pen, at the long rack of ledgers that would outlast them both. The ledger will, he said. He signed as witness and left the vault with the smell of the lamp in his clothes, the city above him gray with Frost.

Chapter 32: The Guild's Shame

The Guildhall had never been so full and so quiet. They crowded the benches that had stood empty for a decade — journeymen and masters, the tallow-men from the lane, the chandlers, the wardens of three quarters — and the silence had a weight to it, the particular weight of a room where everyone has arrived to be counted. Warden Haverel's voice, when he opened the reckoning, was lower than Benet had ever heard it.

They read the confession aloud, Nessim's copy, because Veyle's own pen was in the canal. They read the levy entries and the false totals, the twelve years of the Seal Fund skimmed line by line, and each figure fell into the hall like a stone into still water. The wardens who had signed the audits did not look at one another. Steward Sorn kept his eyes on his folded hands. The Guild had trusted the seal instead of reading it, and the seal had been a lie in amber, and the cost of that trust sat in a chair in the vault below.

What shamed them, Benet saw, was not the money. It was the smallness. Twelve years and no one had asked who bought the wax. The letter had lain unbroken on the table and the whole Guild had treated an unbroken seal as a sealed conscience. Haverel said it plainly, in the end: we praised him for his smoothness and called it generosity. We called a thief affable and a murderer our friend.

The empty place at the Treasurer's seat was the shape the shame took. No one had taken it; it had been empty since the twelfth, and now it was an accusation in oak. The Guild voted, that day, to open the Fund's books to the light, and to a stranger's audit, and to read every charter's fee against every charter's seal. It was not forgiveness and it was not reform yet. It was the first honest thing the room had done in years, and it was done in the fear of what else the ledgers might still be holding.

Benet walked back down Tanner Lane that evening with the dusk coming on, and the lantern above the Wax Light was already lit, a small gold eye against the gray. Marta met him at the door and did not ask what the Guild had decided. She only looked at him a long moment, and nodded, and went back to her fire. The shame was theirs, the learning was theirs; the shop's work was only to keep its own ledger true, and to be ready.

Chapter 33: The Reform

The reforms were not passed in a speech. They were passed in ink, clause by clause, through the short gray days that closed Frost — and Benet, who had never spoken before the Guild except under summons, found himself at the long table with a pen of his own, drafting what the wardens only knew they wanted.

The first clause was the blue ribbon. Every official seal of the city and the Guild would henceforth be struck over a ribbon of dyed silk worked into the wax before the blow — so that a seal could not be lifted with a hot knife and remade, because the lifted seal would leave the ribbon's fibers torn and the remade seal would show them absent. Marta's father had been ruined by a forged seal; the ribbon was that ruin's answer, and the Guild accepted it with a gravity that surprised even Marta.

The second clause was the public ledger. Every lot of Guild wax — every pour, every dye, every shaving — would be entered in a ledger kept at the Guildhall, open to any citizen, and certified by the shop that poured it. The lot ledger of the Wax Light had shown the city the truth once; now the truth would be a matter of public record, and the shavings would be the city's memory, kept in paste and in ink where no one could borrow them.

The third clause was the audit. The Seal Fund would be audited every year, in the open, by examiners chosen not by the Treasury but by lot from the quarter guilds — the very men who paid the fee. The levy books would be balanced against the arrival ledger every season, and Nessim, whose ledger had never once lied, was named keeper of the reconciliation. He accepted with the precise, quiet pride of a man whose conscience has been made official.

The fourth clause was the shop. The Wax Light was chartered the city's wax-maker, its lot numbers the only wax the Guild would seal with, its standards the city's standards. Marta signed for it herself, in the hand her father had taught her, and she did it without a tremor.

Benet copied the clauses into the fair book in his best hand, and when he was done he sat back and looked at what the Guild had made of its shame. It was not a monument. It was a set of rules that an honest man could keep and a dishonest man could not hide from. He thought of Aldric, who had died to uncover the need for it, and of the vault below them, where the Treasurer's chair stood empty, and of the blue ribbon he would thread into the first seal of his own career. The paper smelled of the shop's fire. It was the first law he had ever written, and it read like a recipe: honest measure, honest heat, honest blow.

Chapter 34: Aldric's Name

It was the Warden who asked it, a week after the reforms were signed, and it was asked quietly, in the small antechamber where the Guild kept the dead Master's effects: will you speak for him, journeyman? The letter-books lay open on the table between them, Aldric's hand in every line, thirty years of single-strike seals reduced to a sheaf of pages.

Benet read them as a man reads a ledger. The audit of the Seal Fund was all there, figure by figure, in Aldric's close, careful numerals — the false entries flagged, the shortfalls totaled, the year of the Salt Venture loss marked in the margin with a single firm line. The denunciation had not been a dying man's malice, as Veyle had whispered across the hall. It had been a working paper, a case assembled over months, and its only error had been to trust the seal to carry it to the Guild alive.

So Benet spoke before the Guild, and he did not speak of the murder. He spoke of the craft of the man, because it was the only eulogy Aldric's pride would have accepted. He told them what the letter-books showed: that the Master had audited the Fund not once but twice, that he had checked his own figures against Nessim's arrival ledger, that he had written the denunciation in a hand steady enough to be read as type. He told them of the single clean strike — how Aldric had sealed every letter of his life once, and once only, because he believed a seal should be a signature and a signature should be a promise, and a promise made twice is a promise half-made.

He told them what the case had taught. The city had feared Aldric for his reading of men; the case had shown there was something else to fear for him — that he had read the city, and gone on loving it, and had written down the truth of it in a hand that would not lie. Fear had been the city's way of honoring him. Benet asked them, in Aldric's name, to try the harder thing and listen.

When he sat down the hall was still, and then Steward Sorn rose, and then the tallow-men, and the Guild recorded, by the pen of Nessim, that the Master's name was cleared — that the audit was confirmed, that the denunciation was proved figure by figure, that Aldric of the Seal Tower had served the city better than the city had known, and that his seals, every one, had been true. The exoneration was entered on the eleventh day of Frost, a year of shame and reparation to the hour. Benet took a shaving from the box of the Master's own dark red wax, the lot the shop had poured for him every year, and put it in his pocket. Some records, he thought, a man keeps for himself.

Chapter 35: The Memorial

The Guildhall was lit as the city lit it only for its dead: a candle at every window, and a low table at the head of the hall where the matrix of the Seal — the old one, retired now — rested on a cushion of blue silk, the bronze still bearing its thirty years of small honest dents. The Guild stood in its orders, and beside the table, in the front rank, stood Coben.

He had grown a hand's breadth since the winter, and he held himself straighter than the boy who had found the body on the stairs. Benet watched him from the journeyman's bench and thought of the night of the twelfth, of the man at the door and the sound of the fall and the lie that had told him he dreamed it. The boy had carried that dream for a year. The Guild had learned, at the trial of the wax, to thank him for the courage of waking.

Marta stood at the other side of the table with a taper. The memorial was her making, in the old way of the craft: the candles all poured from one lot, one fire, so that the whole hall burned with a single honest flame. She had wanted it that way, she said, because a man is remembered in one light, not a hundred borrowed ones.

Benet had brought the wax for the great candle, and he set it in its holder and lit it, and the flame steadied over the bronze matrix and the cushion of silk. The light fell on Coben's face, and on Marta's, and on the long benches where the Guild stood and remembered a man most of them had feared. In the quiet, Benet took the shaving from his pocket — the dark red lot, the Master's own — and held it a moment over the flame, letting the wax soften, and then pressed it gently onto the rim of the great candle, where it caught the light as a small dark seal against the gold.

Afterward, when the hall had emptied and the candles burned down to their cups, Marta found him banking the last of the flames. You kept it, she said, meaning the shaving. He nodded. I keep it because wax remembers, he said, and because the dead should have something of theirs that is not a ledger. She was quiet a long moment. Then she said, my father's name had no such candle, and she said it without anger, and she took his arm, and they walked back down the lane together through the first snow of the coming Thaw.

Chapter 36: The Seal, New

The new matrix was cut by the city's finest graver, a slow man named Taras, who worked bronze the way Marta worked wax — by feel, by patience, by the refusal to strike twice. He had taken the design from the old seal and altered only what must alter: the city arms held now by the wax-light, the candle, the small honest flame where the old device had borne the Treasury's key. When he was done he brought the matrix to the Guildhall wrapped in wool, and the Guild came to look at it the way men look at a new foundation stone.

The Warden read the grant in the hall, and the words were simple and the silence around them was not. By this seal, the city binds itself; let the office pass to the hand that has kept its own honor, and let the Wax Light be the city's wax. And the Guild, which had taken three hundred years to name its Masters of the Seal from among the eldest of its clerks and wardens, broke that custom in a single sentence, and offered the Mastership to a journeyman.

Benet stood at the journeyman's bench and did not move. He thought of the letter in its amber seal, of the two stains beneath it, of the night he had stood before the hall with his own craft in the dock and his own name nearly with it. He had learned that office is a trust, and trust is a thing wax can be made to hold or to betray. He had not asked for this; he had only asked that the shop remain honest.

He accepted on one condition, and he spoke it before the Guild as a journeyman speaks to his trade: that the Wax Light remain the city's wax. No other shop, no other lot, no other fire — the lot ledger open, the shavings pasted, the blue ribbon in every official seal. The Guild would seal with his hands or not at all. It was not ambition he was bargaining; it was the one guarantee he knew how to make, that the craft which had unmade a murderer would not be unmade in its turn.

The Warden looked at him a long moment, and then he looked at Marta, and she nodded once. So it is written, the Warden said. The Wax Light is the city's wax, and you are the city's seal. He set the matrix on the table between them, and Benet took it up, and it was heavier than he had expected, and warmer, as if the bronze had already been handled by the dead Master's hand. He carried it down to the shop that evening and set it in the window, and the lantern over the sign threw its gold across the new arms, and he stood a long while looking at the candle it bore, which was the same candle, he thought, that had burned over the shop all his life and would burn, God willing, after.

Chapter 37: Benet's Mastership

The oath was sworn in the Seal Tower, in the round room at the top where the matrices of the city were kept in their iron chests, and the winter light came in through the high windows and lay in bars across the floor. Warden Haverel read the words, and Benet repeated them, and his voice was steadier than he had expected — I will strike no seal but in true wax, and no wax but in true measure, and no measure that I would not set my name to. The keys of the Tower were handed to him on a ring of iron, and the weight of them was the weight of the city.

He had thought, in the nights after the trial, that the Mastership would feel like a burden. It did not. It felt like a return — to the one thing he had always been able to trust, which was his own hands. He went down to the shop the same morning and stood at the bench where he had stood as a boy, and he poured the first lot of his office: the standing order for the new Guild-year wax, dark red in the old Master's style, because some things should not change with an office, and the color of an honest seal was one of them.

Marta watched him pour. She did not give him advice, and he did not ask for any; the partnership had been agreed in the week of the charter, and what passed between them in the shop was the trust of two people who have read the same ledger and believed it. When the lot was cooled she took the shaving for the ledger — the new book, the official one, her father's hand and hers and now Benet's — and pasted it in, and wrote the number, and the date, and the name of the buyer, which was the city.

The first seal of his mastership he struck that afternoon, alone in the Tower, on the charter that named the Wax Light the city's wax. He worked the matrix into the hot dark red, and he set it, and he struck once — a single clean blow, the way Aldric had struck for thirty years, the way he had practiced ten thousand times on scraps of tallow in the back of the shop. The seal came out perfect on the first strike, and he held the charter up to the light, and the dark red wax caught the winter sun, and there was no second edge anywhere on it.

He stood a moment with the charter in his hands, thinking of the man who had taught the city that lesson with his own life. Then he put the matrix away, and the keys on their ring, and he walked back down the stairs into the winter afternoon, and the smell of the shop's fire reached him on the cold air of Gilders' Row before he had turned the corner. Aldric's single strike, inherited by a better student of wax, had come home.

Chapter 38: The Shop

The shop went on. That was the first thing Benet learned about the Mastership, and he had to learn it again every morning: the city could be ruled by seals and ledgers and blue ribbons, but the Wax Light was ruled by the fire. The rendering pans had to be scoured, the tallow weighed, the dye ground fine. The standing orders came in with the dawn and the walk-in trade came all day, and none of it waited on the office.

Marta was beside him at the bench, as she had promised. She had made the partnership her own in a dozen small ways — she kept the receipts now, she charmed the tallow-men, she had taken to sitting with the ledger after hours, running her thumb down the pasted shavings as other women run their hands over mended linen. Her father's name had been ruined by a forged seal, and her own name was now the city's warranty, and Benet thought she wore that weight the way she wore her apron, without vanity and without once taking it off.

Piet was growing into the trade. He had filled out in the shoulders and his hands had begun to know the wax the way a musician's hands come to know an instrument, and he could pour a standing order now without supervision, and strike a practice seal that was nearly — nearly — single. He asked questions constantly, and Benet answered them the way Marta had once answered his own, with patience and with the truth, and he watched the boy test his first lot of amber and hold it up to the light with the exact look of wonder Benet remembered wearing himself, a lifetime ago.

Across the lane, in the evenings, the candle burned in Nella's window. She came to the shop with her father's orders, the tallow-chandler's daughter who had watched the trial from the back benches and never once looked away, and she stayed a little longer each time, until staying had become a habit and the habit had become a promise. They had not spoken of it. There was no need; the shop was small, and the winter was long, and some things take the time they take, the way a lot takes the time it takes to cool.

At dusk Benet climbed the step and lit the lantern over the sign, and the gold flame came up behind the painted candle, and the light fell across the lane to the chandler's door. The Wax Light burned on, the craft went on, the life went on — more honest now, and with the city's trust resting on it, but otherwise exactly what it had always been: a warm room, a true ledger, and a fire that never went out.

Chapter 39: The Craft

The Mastership, Benet found, had not changed the craft; it had only given him an excuse to be strict about it. He rendered in the morning, when the fire was new and the air of the shop was cold, because the heat of the day spoiled the fine distinctions of scent. He weighed the tallow and the beeswax on the old iron scales, and he entered the weights in the lot ledger before he melted anything, because a pour is a promise and a promise should begin in ink.

The dye he ground himself, the madder and the weld and the pinch of iron vitriol that had made Tanner Amber, and he ground it finer than the recipe called for and measured it to the grain. The lot ledger was official now, the city's memory in paste and ink, and Benet treated it with the reverence he had learned from Marta — every number, every shaving, every buyer's name, set down in a hand a stranger could read. The shavings were the point. Wax remembers, and the ledger was the place where the city could go to remember with it.

The pour was the sacrament of the trade, and Benet had made a ritual of it without ever meaning to. He heated the lot slowly, stirring against the grain of the pot, watching for the first fine bubbles and skimming them as they rose, because bubbles were the little lies a careless fire left in the wax — a hollow that would open under a seal and let a knife in. He poured hot and let it cool in the stillness of the shop, and he read the bloom the way he had learned to read it: the cloudy surface pattern a lot cools into, as individual as a hand, as honest as a date.

The striking he practiced every evening, an hour of it, long after the shutters were up and the fire was banked. Single strikes, once and done, the matrix set and the blow landed and the seal lifted clean — the habit of a man who believed, with the dead Master, that a signature is a promise and a promise is made once. Piet watched, and tried, and failed, and tried again, and Benet corrected his wrist with the same two words Marta had used on him for years: again, and clean.

It was, he thought one evening, a strange work for a man of the city — to spend his days in wax and his evenings in bronze, and to measure out his whole life in heats and blows and dates. But the charter hung in the window, and the blue ribbons lay coiled in the drawer, and every lot that left the shop was a small piece of the city's trust made solid and struck true. He banked the fire, and put up the shutters, and the craft that had unmade a murderer went on quietly making honest seals, one single clean strike at a time.

Chapter 40: The Wax, Honest

The charter hung in the window, in a frame of dark wood, and the morning sun came through the painted candle behind the glass and laid a band of gold across the words: the Wax Light, chartered the city's wax-maker, its lots the only wax the Guild seals with, its ledger the city's memory. Passersby on Tanner Lane stopped to read it — the tanners in their aprons, the clerks from the Guildhall, the tallow-men who had once looked at the sweet-smelling shop and called it strange. The sign meant nothing to them a year ago. Now it was a public fact, like the bridge or the river.

Benet stood at the counter that morning with the honest measure in his hands. The measure was a small bronze cup, struck with the city arms and the date of the charter, and it was the law of the shop now: every lot poured to the cup, every pound weighed to it, every buyer given what the cup held and not a grain less. The price of honesty, Marta had said, was that you could no longer be cheated by accident. It was, Benet thought, the only trade in the city where the customer and the craftsman shared the same fear of the short measure.

What it bought, in return, was harder to weigh but simpler to see. The clerks came with their letters, and the seals went on dark red and true, and no one in the city doubted the wax on a charter anymore. The Guildhall's books balanced against the arrival ledger every season, and Nessim's pen had grown almost cheerful with the monotony of nothing to find. The blue ribbon held. The lot ledger held. The city trusted its own letters again, and the trust was a small thing, seal by seal, and it was everything.

At dusk, as he always did, Benet climbed the step and opened the lantern. The old candle within it was nearly spent, and he took it out — a stub of wax that had burned through a murder, a trial, a reform, a year of snow — and he replaced it with the first candle poured under the charter, the honest lot, the new fire. It caught, and steadied, and the light of the Wax Light shone out over the quarter as the dark came down the lane.

Nella came to the step as the light went up, and she stood with him a moment watching the flame. It will be the last thing the quarter sees at night, she said, and the first thing in the morning. He nodded. The lantern of the Wax Light had never gone out in his lifetime, nor in Marta's, nor in her father's. The sign shone over the tanners and the river, the honest flame against the dark canal, and the shop at the far end of the lane kept the city's trust the only way it knew how — one lot, one ledger, one single clean strike, and a candle lit at dusk that would burn while there was wax to feed it.

Chapter 41: The Winter

The river froze in the second week of Deepwinter, silently, over one night. Benet stood at the Tower window and watched the water stop moving — the dark canal first, then the broad river beyond the Weir, until the whole city lay between two sheets of white, and the air that came off them was the cleanest the quarter ever breathed.

The cold was the great lesson of his trade. Wax is a patient creature in warmth and a brittle one in frost, and every journeyman learns the difference the way a boy learns not to touch the stove. In the Seal Tower that winter Benet taught it again, to himself as much as to the shop. A cold lot will not take the matrix. It cracks along the cooling lines before the strike is half made, or it shatters outright, a hundred shards on the flags and the letter ruined and the wax wasted. The trick is not to fight the cold but to court it. Bring the tin to the fire slowly; let the heat rise through it like a tide; stir with the wooden paddle until the bloom on the surface melts and the amber runs clear; warm the paper at the flame, not to scorch it but to soften the fibers, so the wax sets against them and not against the ice. A master's hands in winter are slow hands. He had learned slowness from a dead man who struck once because he had learned to wait.

The mornings in the Tower began the same way. Benet opened the vault, took down the day's matrices, and laid them on the ledge above the brazier to come to room warmth before a single letter was sealed. Marta scolded him across the lane for it — a sealed letter's wax needs the matrix warm as well as the lot, or the impression comes up cold and dull, and a dull seal is an honest seal gone grey. "Warm the die, warm the wax, warm the hand," she said, counting on her fingers, "and the seal will be true. There is no fourth thing." He kept the rule by heart and wrote it in the margin of the lot ledger, where the shavings sat pasted like pressed flowers from a summer no one could remember.

Nella found him there one dusk, blowing on his knuckles over the ledger. "You look like the wax," she said. "Cold as a seal."

"Warm on the inside," he said, and it was almost a joke, and almost true.

The couriers still came, frost or no frost, their letters in leather wallets under their coats. The city did not stop sealing because the river had stopped moving; it sealed more, as if to keep itself warm with promises. Benet struck the seals of winter slowly, one by one, watched the cold amber bloom into its frosted pattern under the lamp, and thought that even the frost could not teach wax to lie.

Chapter 42: The City

The morning was a knife of a morning, blue and hard, and Benet walked the city the way the old Master had walked it — with his eyes open, reading the seals. He had gone out as a journeyman without ever seeing the craft; now he went out as Master of the Seal and saw nothing else.

Every door of the city was a letter. The charter on the Guildhall wall, behind glass, sealed with the city's great matrix in the year of its founding — he knew the lot, a wine-red tallow he could taste in memory. The deeds of the Salt Market stalls, folded in their brass boxes, each with the tiny seal of the market's clerk. The tanners' contracts with the curriers, waxed and counter-waxed, the two seals pressed side by side like two men shaking hands. The baker's license, the vintner's bonds, the marriage lines the priest kept in the chapel chest, sealed in wax older than the couples and bound to outlast them.

And the love letters. Nella had told him of those, laughing, when she learned what he did all day. Half the lane wrote them in winter, when there was nothing else to do, and sealed them with whatever their mothers kept in the kitchen — it was a wonder, she said, that any two people ever matched. Benet had smiled, and not said that he looked at such seals every day, and that even a kitchen seal was a promise made visible, and that the city ran on promises.

He stopped at the Weir, where the ice had piled like white masonry, and watched the water argue beneath it. A year ago he had stood on this bank in the dark while Ruhm's divers brought a bronze press up out of the mud. The canal that had hidden a lie now held nothing but ice.

The walk was a ledger, he realized, if he cared to read it that way. Each seal a line: who pressed it, when, in what wax. The honest ones he could date by bloom alone — a year of frost still in the grain. The new ones were sharp and pale and would darken as the months did their honest work. Not one, in the whole city, had been lifted and set again. He could not have said how he knew, and he did not need to; he was the Master of the Seal, and the city's wax was at peace.

In the afternoon he came home by the long way, past the tanners' pits where the smoke hung blue in the cold, to the far end of the lane, where the Wax Light burned. Marta had the lantern lit early against the short day. He stood a moment in the street, a man among his city, and let the warmth of the sign reach him across the frost.

Chapter 43: The Letters

The seal office received the letters, but Benet never read them. That was the first law, older than the Tower: a man's letter is a wound in wax, and the Master of the Seal does not look at another man's wound. He held them, weighed them, warmed them when the cold had made the wax shy, struck them, gave them back — and in all that he came to know the city's letters the way a physician knows the city's bodies, without ever being told the complaint.

The merchants wrote small and pressed small, their seals neat as sums, and their wax was the city's wax, his own, from the Wax Light — he would have known it anywhere, the honest amber with its frosted bloom. The guilds wrote large and struck their seals large, as if size were proof of standing. The poor wrote rarely, and sealed with whatever they had, and their letters were the ones he warmed the longest, because a poor man's letter was the whole of his hope in one fold of paper, and he would not have it cracked.

Aldric had taught him that, without a word, by being read wrong. The old Master's letters were dark red, single-struck, and the city had feared them because it read the color and not the care. Benet wondered sometimes whether any man had ever known what Aldric's letters said — not the words, which were law, but the care, which was love of the truth. He thought now that he did.

There was a letter he remembered from Hay, the season just past. A currier on the Lane had died, and his daughter wrote to the Guild to say her father's trade was honest and his books true, and would the Guild take his son as apprentice. The seal was kitchen tallow, grey, badly struck — twice, a hair off. Benet had smiled at the double strike and warmed it anyway, and it had held, and the boy had been taken. The double strike of a grieving girl had done more than the single clean strike of a dead Treasurer's vanity.

That, he thought, was what the craft guarded: not the great charters, not the state's word, but the small letters of small people, each carrying its freight of hope or grief or love, each sealed against the world's prying, each arriving whole. The Tower kept those. He struck the seals slowly, in winter, and let the letters go, and trusted.

Chapter 44: The Seals

Nella asked him once, in the warm clutter of her father's shop, what a seal was for. She had grown up among tallow and knew the common kind of the trade better than he did, but the question was not a tradeswoman's question. It was a person's. Benet turned a lump of his own amber in his fingers before he answered.

"A seal is a signature," he said. "Whoever presses it declares who he is — the matrix is his hand, the wax is his ink. An honest man's seal says him truly, and a dishonest man's says him falsely, but either way it says him. The city runs on that."

She waited. "There's more."

"Wax remembers when it was poured," he said. "The lot, the date, the bloom, the cooling lines. A seal is a clock that cannot be set back. You cannot make wax younger than it is. Veyle thought an unbroken seal was proof no one had read his letter. He was wrong. The wax told us when it was made — after the letter, after the death. The time was in it, the way the year is in a tree."

"And the dye?" she said, because she had heard the story and was learning it.

"The dye is a fingerprint. No two lots are alike. I can read a lot the way another man reads a face — by its color, its scent, its bloom. The amber was mine, Lot 14 of the Wax Light. I knew it the way I know my own hand."

He set the lump down. "And the letter is the wound. Every sealed letter is a cut made in the world and then closed. The seal is the scab. If the letter is opened and re-sealed, the scar is still there underneath — the old stain under the new. That is what convicted him. Not the words. The scar."

She was quiet a long moment, counting over tallow in her head. "That's a heavy thing," she said at last, "to sit inside every envelope in the city."

"That's the last part," he said. "An honest seal is the city's smallest law. It is not grand and it does not threaten. It only says: this is mine, this was written when it says it was, this is who I am. A hundred honest seals, a thousand, and the city can trust its own word again. That is why I keep the craft." He smiled at her. "That, and because the shop smells better than the tannery."

Chapter 45: The Trust

He came in the Thaw, when the river broke and the ice went down past the Weir in grinding white. A small man with a clerk's hands and a pleasant face, and he wanted, he said, a seal pressed onto a paper of apprenticeship — his dead father's seal, the tanner's own, to carry on the trade.

Benet looked at the paper. The wax was dark red, and the seal upon it was pressed once, clean. A good forgery, by an honest eye. Then he looked again, and the warmth went out of the morning.

"Where did you get this wax?" he said.

The small man blinked. "My father's chest. It was his."

"The color is the Master's," Benet said, "but it is not the Master's wax." He held it to the light. "Look at the bloom. Aldric's red was made yearly, by my master's hand, in this shop. This red is newer than the paper — and the ink is older than the seal. A seal cannot be younger than its wax, and its wax cannot be younger than the hand that bought it. This was struck last night, by you, in a tin from a chandler who does not ask questions." He set the paper down. "You pressed a dead man's name in a live man's wax."

The color drained from the small man's face the way it had from the paper's. He began to bluster, and Benet cut him off with the ledger.

Marta brought it, heavy, and laid it open on the counter. Every lot, every number, every buyer, every shaving pasted in. "This lot," Benet said, running his thumb along a line, "is the red I poured for the Master last year. It went to the Tower and nowhere else. The Wax Light keeps no more of it than this." He touched the pasted shaving, a dry curl of red. "Every lot is accounted for, to the scrape. You bought no lot here. So your father's wax is a stranger to me — and the stranger, and the tin, and the night, are the parts of your story that will not hold."

The small man stood very still. Benet closed the ledger, gently, like a door that need not be slammed.

"Go home," he said. "Teach your son to make an honest mark, and press it in his own wax, and I will seal it for you myself. But do not press dead names again. The city reads its wax now."

The man left, and he did not come back. In the evening Marta said, "You let him go."

"The ledger caught him," Benet said. "He will not try twice against a book that remembers." He had not needed the watch, or the Guild, or a trial. The honest wax and the registered ledger had done the whole of the work in a single morning, and the city went on, and the trust held.

Chapter 46: The Anniversary

A year, to the day, from the night on the stairs. Frost had come round again, and the city was cold, and Benet had not forgotten the date because the shop had not let him forget it — the standing order for the new Guild-year lot was due on the morning of the thirteenth, as it had been on the morning after the old Master died.

He had planned it quietly, the way he did everything. In the dusk he climbed to the Seal Tower with a taper and a lump of the Master's own dark red, the last of the lot made the year before, kept back for this one purpose. Marta came up behind him, breathing hard on the stair, and did not speak.

He set the candle in the window that faced down Gilders' Row, toward the house where Aldric had fallen. He warmed the red wax in his hands until it gave, pressed the old matrix — Aldric's own, retired now, on its cushion in the vault — once, clean, and set the seal on the sill beside the flame. A single strike, in the old Master's style, in the old Master's red. The bloom came up frosted and true.

"Now he's sealed," Marta said. She was crying, and would not have been seen to be, and was glad of the dark. "That's what he was, Benet. That was his whole craft. A thing struck once and true."

They heard steps on the stair, and Coben came up, taller than he had been, a year of good feeding in his face. He had come to the Tower on this day every month since, to stand in the room where the truth had been kept, and Benet had never turned him away. The boy looked at the candle and at the red seal and nodded, as if an account were settled.

"He was good to me," Coben said. It was all he ever said, and it was enough.

Nella was waiting at the foot of the Tower when they came down, her lantern lit against the frost, her breath silver. She did not ask what they had done; she took Benet's cold hand in both of hers and walked with him up the lane to the Wax Light, where the sign burned and the smell of oak and rosemary came out to meet them.

Late, in the shop, Benet stood at the window and watched the candle burn in the Tower, a small gold point in the dark, and thought that Aldric had been honored with the one thing he had loved — a clean seal, struck once, in a wax that remembered him. The flame held. It would hold till morning.

Chapter 47: The Dye, Kept

The shaving was still in the ledger, where it had always been. Amber, dry now, curled like a petal pressed between leaves, its bloom faded to a frost the color of old honey. The book lay open to the page, as it had lain open to that page for a year — Lot 14, the date of the pouring, the standing order, the witnesses. Marta's hand had written the entry; Benet's hand had poured the lot; the shaving was all that was left of the morning that unmade a murderer.

He did not read the page. He knew it by heart. He touched the shaving sometimes, in the quiet of the evening, when the shop was closed and the lantern burned and the whole city was sealed for the night. It was the dryest thing in the shop, and the heaviest.

Piet had asked him once why he kept it, why he did not let the page close and the book move on, as books should. Benet had not answered then. He answered now, in his own mind, the way a man settles a debt with himself.

Because wax remembers. That was the whole of the mystery, the whole of the craft, the whole of the truth he had nearly lost his standing to speak. The dye remembers its lot; the lot remembers its date; the seal remembers its maker's hand. Veyle had trusted wax to lie for him — to stand silent and smooth and unbroken over a wound. And the wax had not lied. It had remembered, and told, and been believed.

He kept the shaving so that no one would forget. A hundred years from now some journeyman would open the ledger, find the pasted lot, ask what Lot 14 was, and be told the story, and understand that an honest ledger is the city's memory and an honest seal its conscience. Benet did not need to be remembered. He needed the shaving to be remembered, and the lesson in it.

He closed the ledger gently, so as not to crush the amber, blew out his candle, and went to bed, and the book lay in the dark, remembering.

Chapter 48: The Story

The new apprentice was called Tomas, and he was Piet's cousin, and he had come to the Wax Light in the autumn with the same look Benet had worn at that age — awe and fright and a determination not to show either. He was quick with the paddle and slow with his tongue, and in the evenings, when the shop was swept and the fire banked, he sat on the stool by the ledger and waited.

He was waiting for the story. Benet knew the look. He had waited for stories himself, once, at a journeyman's knee.

So on a frost evening, when the last of the day's sealing was done, Benet sat down across from the boy and told it. He told it the way the craft taught it, piece by piece, each clue in its order, none withheld. The letter the old Master had written, sealed in his dark red. The seal upon it that was not dark red — amber, his own work, Lot 14. The two stains beneath, the old ghost and the new, the scar under the scab. The ledger that dated the lot to the day after the death. The receipt that named the proxy. The double strike, a hair off — a clerk's hand, not a sealer's. The matrix borrowed from the Treasury. And at the end, the bronze press in the canal, wrapped in a dead Treasurer's linen, the chip of slate on its corner matching the stair.

Tomas sat very still through all of it, his hands folded, his eyes round. When Benet finished, the boy was quiet a long time, turning it over.

"How did you know?" he asked at last. It was the question of the book, the one Benet had been waiting a year for a boy to ask.

Benet reached over and opened the lot ledger to the page that never closed. He took out the amber shaving, dry as a pressed leaf, and held it up between his fingers.

"The wax told me," he said. "Every lot remembers — when it was poured, where it went, whose hand pressed it. You have only to learn to read what it remembers. That is the whole craft, Tomas. The whole craft is paying attention."

The boy looked at the shaving a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, the way Piet had nodded, the way Benet had nodded once, and said, "Will I learn it?"

"You'll learn to pour," Benet said, and put the shaving back. "The rest comes when you learn to listen."

Chapter 49: The Candle

The shop was dark when Benet came down to it, the fire banked, the lantern burning at the sign outside, the street asleep. He had done this once a year since the Mastership came to him — made a single candle, by the old methods, alone, for no buyer and no purpose but the making.

He did it slowly, as the winter demanded. He weighed out the good beeswax, the honest pale gold of it, and set it to melt in the tin over the low fire, stirring with the wooden paddle. He hung the wick in the mold — a length of the spun tow Piet had twisted that afternoon — and weighted it with a nail. He poured when the wax ran clear, a thin stream, steady, no bubbles, no hurry, the whole craft of a year in the steadiness of one hand.

Then he waited. A candle is not made in the pouring but in the cooling, and Benet had learned patience the way other men learned trades. He sat on the stool by the ledger and watched the surface go from clear to milky, the bloom creeping in from the edges like frost on a window. He did not touch it. The candle cooled at its own pace, and he let it, and the shop ticked around him.

He trimmed it when it was set, cutting the wick square with the knife Marta had given him on his journeyman's day. He drew it from the mold and turned it in his hands — a single perfect candle, pale gold, smooth, true, the sort of thing his master would have set on the counter and said nothing about, because there was nothing to say. It was right.

He lit it, and the little flame stood on the wick, and the dark shop filled with warm gold light and the smell of beeswax and oak and rosemary. He held it up, and it was the whole book, the whole craft, the whole year: the honest thing, made slowly, by hand, in the dark, and brought to light.

He let it burn till morning. No one saw it. That was the point.

Chapter 50: The Wax Light

Dusk came down Tanner Lane like a fall of grey water, and the tanners' smoke was already blue in it, and the lamps of the quarter were coming out one by one — the chandler's, the currier's, the cooper's — all except one. The Wax Light burned its sign behind glass, but the lantern above it had gone out at noon, when the wind tore the glass, and Marta had spent the afternoon in a fury about it, because the light above the Wax Light had not been out in her lifetime, nor her father's.

Benet climbed the step with the new glass in one hand and a taper in the other. He was Master of the Seal; he could have sent for a glazier, could have had any man in the quarter up the ladder. But this was the Wax Light's lantern, and its keeping had passed to him, and he would not have it tended by any other hand.

He fitted the glass, worked the catch till it seated, and opened the door of the lantern. The wick was cold and clean. He trimmed it square, the way he had trimmed his candle in the dark shop, and raised his taper.

The flame caught, hesitated, caught again, and steadied. It stood in the lantern like a small amber bloom, and the light spread out over the sign — the painted candle behind glass — and down the step and along the wet stones of the lane, a warm gold pool in the cold dusk.

He stood on the step a moment and looked down the lane at the city beyond: the dark of the canal, the cold stars over the Weir, the thousand small lights going on in the windows. The night would have them all in a little while. But this light would not go out; it had not gone out since before he was born, and it would not go out after he was gone. It was the honest flame over the honest craft, and the shop's sign shone over the quarter, as it had shone over the man who had killed for a seal, and as it would shine over the boys not yet born who would come to learn the trade.

He stepped down, and Marta's voice came from the door behind him, gruff and satisfied: "There, now. That's it lit."

"That's it lit," Benet said. And the lantern held, and the city darkened, and the Wax Light burned.
