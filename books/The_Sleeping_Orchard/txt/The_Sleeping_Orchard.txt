The Sleeping Orchard
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Wall

The wall had stood a hundred years and would stand a hundred more. Hazel knew this the way she knew the roof would hold the snow and the well would hold the water — as a fact of the world, older than her, older than her mother, older than anyone's memory of a door having opened in it.

From her window she could see a stretch of it, grey and moss-eaten, twelve feet high, running behind the village like a held breath. The village rules about it were few and old. Do not climb it. Do not touch the gate. Do not call over the top, in case something calls back. The key to the iron gate hung in Elder Bramble's house, and everyone said it was rusted, and that it fit no lock, and that the gate had not been opened in living memory, and that was that.

Beyond the wall, everyone agreed, was a curse. An orchard in a hundred-year sleep. Trees full and green and heavy with fruit that never fell — an eternal midsummer held inside the winter. It was strange and it was sad and it was forbidden, and the village had made its peace with all three.

Snow was falling now, thick and slow, the first of deep midwinter. Hazel pressed her hand to the cold glass. All day the village had gone about its business — mending harness, banking fires, telling the same stories — and the quiet had settled over everything like snow settling over roofs. She liked the quiet. The quiet was when she heard it.

There it was. Low, under everything, like distant bees and strings, a sound no one else seemed to hear. It came from beyond the wall. It had come for as long as she could remember, and when she hummed back, it seemed to listen.

The snow fell. The grey stone stood. And then — Hazel blinked, leaned closer, pressed her face to the pane.

Over the top of the wall, where no green thing had ever moved in a January, there was a speck of red. Small, bright, unmistakable, hung like a lantern on a branch of the sleeping trees.

It should not have been there. A hundred years of frozen summer and never a change, and now, in the deepest cold, a red speck no bigger than her thumb.

Hazel's breath made a fog on the glass, and she wiped it away to see again. It was still there.

Curiosity, in a season of stillness, is a small fire, and she felt it catch.

She did not tell anyone. Not yet. She watched the red speck until the light failed and the snow hid the wall, and all the while the humming went on beneath the winter, patient and low, as if it had been waiting.

Chapter 2: The Humming

The humming had always been there, like the wall. Hazel had been hearing it before she could name it, a low song under the day's noise, and when she was small she had asked her mother if the orchard sang.

Maren had looked at her a long moment. "It's the wind, love," she had said, and turned back to the bread.

But it was not the wind. The wind came and went. The humming stayed. It was not quite a note and not quite a voice — something between bees and the drone of a distant string, so low it was almost a feeling in her chest. When the village was quiet, it rose. When she hummed back, it settled, as if it had been answered.

No one else could hear it. She had tried, carefully, to find out. She hummed the tune under her breath at the winter market and watched the faces — no one turned, no one lifted a head. Only Pip, who was twelve and could always be counted on to say the true wrong thing, caught her at it.

"You're doing it again," he said. "Humming at the wall."

"I'm not humming at the wall," she said, which was not true.

"You're going to hum the curse awake one day, Hazel. Then where will we be?"

"Inside a curse," she said, "probably."

He snorted and threw a snowball at the wall, where it burst on the grey stone. "There. That's what your humming's worth."

But he did not mean it. He never meant it. And when she hummed again, softer, he did not laugh; he just watched her with his head cocked, as if he half-heard something after all.

At night her mother sat by the fire and mended, and Hazel sat with her, and the humming came up through the floor and the walls like the memory of summer. She hummed it in her sleep. Maren had told her so — once, gently, in the dark, thinking she was asleep: "You hum even when you're sleeping, love. Like you're answering something."

Hazel had not answered. But she had lain awake a long time, listening to whether the humming answered back.

It did. It always did. It was not noise to her, had never been noise. It was a voice on the other side of the wall, and it knew her, and when she hummed, it listened — the way you listen when someone you know begins to speak.

The trouble was she had never told anyone that part. Because how do you say to a village that a curse is talking to you, and that it sounds less like a threat and more like a song, and that you have started to look forward to it?

Chapter 3: The Hundred Years

The midwinter meeting was held in the meeting house, which was the only building in Greengate with room for everyone at once. They gathered by the light of a dozen lanterns, stamping snow from their boots, and Elder Bramble stood at the front in his long coat with the great rusted key hanging at his belt, where it had hung for as long as anyone could remember, fitting nothing.

He told the story as it had always been told, and the village listened as it had always listened.

"A hundred years ago," Bramble said, "this was an orchard town, and the orchard behind the wall was the best of them all. Its apples were famous, and the witch, the old one, came to the valley and asked for apples. She was a gardener, they say, and greedy for fruit, and the folk refused her. You do not give the best of your trees to a stranger who will not give her name."

Hazel, at the back with her mother, felt the familiar shiver at the word. Witch. The whole village spoke it the same way, low and careful, as if saying it too loudly might summon her.

"And being refused," Bramble went on, "she cursed the orchard. She stole its fruit — every apple, every seed — and she locked the trees in a sleep that would not end. A hundred years of frozen summer. That is what hangs over the wall. That is why the fruit never falls and never rots and never feeds a single child of this valley."

The lanterns guttered. Snow hissed at the windows. And Bramble stood there, the keeper of the key, the keeper of the story, and the story was as solid as the wall itself.

It was then that Hazel asked her question. She had not meant to. It came out of her the way the humming did — without asking permission.

"But Elder," she said, and the room turned to look at her, "if the witch cursed the fruit to steal it — what did she do with it? A witch cannot eat a cursed apple, can she? And if she could, why curse the tree that gives it? A curse that feeds nobody steals nothing. It just waits."

The silence that followed was very long. Bramble looked at her, and she saw his face go from startled to puzzled to closed, like a door being shut against the cold.

"She is a witch, Hazel," he said, and his voice was kind and final, like a latch. "We do not have to understand the wicked. We only have to keep them out."

The meeting moved on. The village went home. But in the corner of the room, over the dying lanterns, a woman who had made cider all her life — Mrs. Quince — was looking at Hazel as if she had just heard a stone speak, and was turning the question over in her own mind, and finding it would not lie down.

And Hazel, walking home through the snow beside her silent mother, heard the humming rise beyond the wall, and it seemed to her, for the first time, less like a curse — and more like someone waiting to be asked properly.

Chapter 4: The Witch's Curse

The full story was told to the children, and Hazel, though she was sixteen, stayed to hear it again, because the story had changed for her now and she wanted to see it plainly.

It was told in Mrs. Quince's cider house, on the night the snow let up, while the smallest children sat in a ring and the older ones leaned on the barrels. The charms were mentioned — little bundles of dried things that used to hang on every door, until the village decided the witch was too far gone to notice them, and let them moulder. The iron gate was described, how it had never been opened, how the key had grown too rusted to turn even the lock it was made for. And the trees: full, green, heavy, frozen in a perfect summer, their fruit forever out of reach.

"A hundred years," Mrs. Quince said, "and not one apple has fallen over that wall. Not one has been tasted. The trees neither grow nor die. That is what a curse does, children — it holds a thing in its fist and will not let it be alive, and will not let it die."

The children shivered deliciously. Hazel shivered too, but her shiver was different, and she knew it.

That night she dreamed of the orchard from the inside.

She was standing in golden light. The air was warm on her face, warmer than any January, and the snow of the world outside had fallen away at some invisible threshold. Trees rose around her in long green rows, heavy with fruit, their leaves motionless, and over everything lay a stillness so deep it was like standing inside a held breath.

And there was a voice. Not speaking — humming. Low and wordless, like a woman humming to a child, or to a garden, or to nothing in particular because the sound itself was the point. It came from ahead of her, between the rows.

She tried to move toward it, and her feet would not go, and the humming did not stop, only swelled, as if it had felt her listen.

Hazel woke in the dark of her own room, her heart going, her breath loud. The fear was there — the old fear, the village fear, the cold hand on the back of the neck. The witch. The curse. A hundred years in a fist.

But under the fear, like the humming under the winter, there was something else. A wonder. The orchard had not been cold in her dream. It had been warm. And the voice had not sounded like rage, or greed, or anything a curse would sound like.

It had sounded, she thought, lying in the dark with the snow-light on her ceiling, like a lullaby. Like someone singing to a sleeping thing, and hoping, in the endless middle of the song, that someone would one day come to answer it.

She did not tell her mother about the dream. But before she slept again, she hummed the tune back, softly, into the dark — and whether it was the wind or the orchard or her own heartbeat, she could have sworn it answered.

Chapter 5: The Midwinter Ripening

The red speck grew.

Hazel had watched it alone for two days, thinking it a trick of the snow-light, a bird, a dead leaf — anything but what it plainly was. But on the third morning, when she pressed her face to the window, the speck had not only stayed; it had multiplied. Where there had been one, there were a dozen, and then a score, scattered red against the green of the sleeping trees like berries on a frozen bush.

By midday the whole village had seen it.

They gathered below the wall in the deep cold, breath white, hands in sleeves, and looked up. And up there, over the grey stone, the impossible thing was happening: the apples were ripening. In January, in frost so hard the well chains rang when the wind touched them, the orchard was turning red. The trees that had not changed in a hundred years were changing now, and the frost could not touch them. Frost lay white on the top of the wall itself, but the fruit above it shone warm and deep as summer, as if the cold had no jurisdiction there.

Fear and wonder argued in every face.

"It's the curse ending," said the miller's wife, her voice high. "When a curse ends, everything it held rots at once. We should be boarding our doors."

"Rots?" said old Tom the herdsman. "Does that look rotten to you, woman? That looks like the best apples this valley ever grew, and I have waited ninety years to say so."

They argued. Some wanted to climb and throw stones at the fruit, to knock it down before it could fall. Some wanted to burn the wall — you can always burn a wall, they said, if you want to badly enough. Some, the quiet ones, only looked and said nothing, and Hazel watched them and knew they were feeling the same crack she had felt: that the world had broken its rules, and no one knew which side that favored.

Elder Bramble came down to the wall with the key at his belt, and he stood a long time, and the village fell silent, waiting for him to know what to do. But he only looked, and his face was the face of a man watching a story he had believed all his life begin to change its ending.

"It means nothing yet," he said at last, and his voice shook a little. "It is a witch's work. It can only mean ill."

But no one went home. They stayed until the short day died, staring up at the red fruit in the cold, and the humming sang underneath them all, and Hazel stood apart from them with her hands in her pockets, and she felt it — the turn of the year, the turn of everything — not fear, not quite hope, but the strange excitement of a door she had believed locked, moving, just a little, on its hinges.

Chapter 6: The First Apple

It happened on the fourth day, at dusk.

The village had gone home, worn out by wonder, and Hazel had stayed, as she always stayed, standing below the wall in the falling snow with the humming in her chest. She was not waiting for anything. She was only listening, the way she always listened, humming back the low tune she had hummed for sixteen years.

And the apple fell.

Not dropped. Not tossed. Fell — though it had hung there frozen in a hundred-year summer, and falling was the one thing the fruit of the orchard was not supposed to do. It came over the top of the wall as if the wall were not there, as if it had simply let go and given itself to the winter air, and it turned once in the failing light, red and perfect, and landed in her apron.

She caught it without thinking and stood very still.

It was warm. That was the first thing, stranger than anything — warm as a hand, warm as bread just out of the oven, in the middle of a frost that would freeze the breath in your mouth. It was ripe, deeply, richly ripe, and unblemished, not a spot, not a bruise, as if it had spent its whole hundred years waiting to be this perfect and no insect and no weather had ever been allowed to touch it.

And it smelled of summer. Not the memory of summer, but summer itself — hay and green and warm rain, the whole golden season folded into one apple.

She did not mean to eat it. But it was warm in her hands and the cold was at her back and the humming had gone very quiet, as if the orchard itself were holding its breath to watch her, and she raised it and bit.

It tasted of summer. It tasted of a hundred summers, of orchards her grandmother's grandmother had walked, of the light that had been held behind that wall for a century. It did not freeze in her hands. It did not rot at the bite. It tasted of — she could not name it, but she knew it — of being finished. Of a long waiting, come to its end.

At the center, where the core had been, was one seed. One perfect, unblemished seed, darker than the others should have been, like a bead of polished wood.

She should have told someone. The village was aflame with the ripening; the first fallen apple was a portent they would argue over for a generation. But she looked at the seed in her palm, and the humming rose, warm and unmistakable, and something in her — the listener in her — said, not yet.

She hid the apple in her apron. She hid the seed in the tin at her windowsill, with her small treasures. And she went home through the falling snow, warm all over, feeling like a thief.

It was the first wrong thing she had ever done, she thought. The first small theft.

She could not have known then — not for many days of the winter — that it was the one theft that would save the valley.

Chapter 7: The Village Trembles

The council was called at noon, and the whole village came, because nothing like it had happened in anyone's lifetime.

Bramble stood before them with the key in his hand, and the key had been taken from its hook, and that alone was enough to silence the room. He set it on the table, and it rang against the wood like a bell struck once.

"The ripening is a sign," he said. "The question is of what. I have asked every old person in this valley, and they all agree on the tradition, and the tradition is this: when the spell of a curse breaks, the thing it held is released — and a thing released from a hundred years of waiting does not wake gently. It rots. It turns. It poisons. The orchard has been ripening for four days. When it finishes, the rot will come down over this valley like a pestilence, and our own orchards, our own cider, our own children — they will drink the poison in the fruit and in the water that runs through the roots."

A murmur went around the room like wind through dry leaves.

"So we prepare," said Bramble. "We close the wall to us. The key will be locked in the strongbox, where no hand can take it and no temptation can open the gate. We set guards at the wall, day and night. If the fruit falls, we do not touch it. If the air turns foul, we burn lime in the streets. And we wait for the curse to spend itself."

He locked the key in the strongbox himself, there before them all, and the lock gave a single click that the whole village heard.

Hazel sat with her hands in her lap, and in her pocket the apple-core was wrapped in cloth, and in the tin at home the one seed waited, and she said nothing.

She thought of the apple's warmth. She thought of the taste of summer in the deep of winter. She thought of the perfect, unblemished seed. None of it had felt like rot. None of it had felt like poison. It had felt, in a word she was afraid to use even to herself, like mercy.

But she was sixteen, and the whole village was afraid, and fear, once it arms itself, does not like to be asked to put the weapons down.

So she nodded with the rest of them when the meeting broke. She watched the guards take their posts at the wall with their shovels and their lanterns, watching the reddening fruit as if it were a watch-fire of the enemy. She went home, and she did not tell her mother about the apple in her pocket, or the seed in the tin, or the way the humming had swelled when she had caught it, as if the whole orchard had let out its breath.

She lay awake a long time that night, listening to the guards stamp their boots in the snow, and the humming went on, patient and low, under all of it.

It did not sound like a curse to her.

It sounded like a question.

Chapter 8: Hazel's Gift

It was Maren who told her, and she told her badly, the way you tell a thing you have been putting off for years.

It was the fifth night of the ripening, and the guards were at the wall, and Hazel had come in from the cold with snow on her shoulders, and her mother was sitting at the table with her hands folded, and Hazel knew at once that something had been decided.

"Sit down," said Maren. "I have to tell you about your great-great-grandmother."

Hazel sat. The name came out of the dark of her childhood: a woman whose picture no one had kept, whose face had been lost, but whose story had been told — the way such stories are told — in pieces, at funerals, at the cider press, in whispers.

"She was a gardener," Maren said. "The best this valley ever had. She knew the trees by name. She spoke to them, and they listened, and it is said that when the witch came — a hundred years ago, the witch that cursed the orchard — your great-great-grandmother did not run from her. She walked out to meet her. They were friends."

"Friends?" said Hazel. It was not a word you used about the witch.

"Friends," said Maren. "The witch was a gardener too, you see. That was her trade, before she was anything else. And your great-great-grandmother knew her name — her true name, the one the village lost when they became afraid to speak it. When the orchard was cursed and the wall was built, your great-great-grandmother went on telling her name to no one. She said: the day a gardener of this family hums at the wall and the orchard hums back, you send that child in. The gate will be open for her."

Hazel's heart had gone very still.

"That was three generations ago," Maren said. "I thought it was an old woman's tale. I thought you would grow out of the humming. But you did not grow out of it, Hazel. You grew into it. And now the fruit is ripening in January, and I have watched you stand at that wall every night, humming, and the humming answering you."

She reached across the table and took Hazel's hand, and her grip was tight, and her face was the face of a woman afraid for her only child.

"The gift is yours, love," she said. "It was always going to be. She was the listener, and she passed the listening on. And I am afraid — I am afraid to ask you — but I have to know. When the orchard hums, does it know you?"

Hazel looked at the tin on her windowsill, where one seed waited in the dark.

"Yes," she said. "It knows me. It has known me my whole life."

Her mother was silent a long moment. Then she said, very quietly, "Then I think you are the one it has been waiting for. And I think that is the most frightening thing I have ever had to say."

The gift had been a private wonder for sixteen years. Now it was a duty, heavy as a pot of orchard earth in her arms.

And it was hers.

Chapter 9: The Choosing

On the seventh day, the ripening had spread to every tree that showed over the wall, and the guards reported a new thing: the orchard was glowing at night. A faint golden light, like a banked fire, lay over the tops of the trees. The village took it for the approach of the rot.

And the village, for the first time, spoke of going in.

It happened at the meeting house, at dusk. Bramble stood before them, and the key was not on the table. It was locked in the strongbox, in his own keeping.

"We cannot wait for the curse to break over us," he said. "We have to know what is happening behind that wall. Someone must go through the gate and learn the truth of the ripening — whether it is the rot come early, or —" he paused, and his voice dropped, as if the word cost him, "— whether it is something else. One person. Quick and quiet. In and out before the witch wakes to them."

The room was very still. No one moved. No one met anyone's eyes.

"I have heard it said that the key fits nothing — that the gate was barred from within by the spell, and only the spell can undo it. If the spell is ending, the gate may open for the one the orchard —" he swallowed, "— the one the orchard expects."

He let the silence stretch, waiting for a volunteer. And no one volunteered. They shuffled. They studied the floor.

And then Bramble's eyes came up and moved across the room, and they found Hazel, standing at the back with her mother, and they stopped there.

"Hazel," he said. "You are the one who hums at the wall. All of Greengate knows it. The orchard speaks to you. If it will open for anyone, it will open for you."

"No," said Maren, and her voice cut the room in half. "She is a child. I will not send my daughter into a curse."

"I am not sending her," said Bramble, gently. "I am asking. There is a difference, and it is the only difference that matters."

And Hazel stood in the humming silence, and the whole village looked at her, and she felt it turn inside her — the moment when a thing you have been given becomes a thing you choose.

Maren was gripping her hand, hard.

Hazel looked at the tin at home, where the seed waited. She looked at her mother. She looked at Bramble, who was afraid, doing the best a frightened man could do.

"I will go," she said. "I will ask the orchard what it has been humming all these years."

The room let out its breath. Maren's grip tightened, then loosened, and she did not cry, but her face was the face of a woman letting go of something.

And Hazel took the great rusted key from Bramble's hands — it did not matter that it was locked in the strongbox, for it was also, somehow, in his hands, and it weighed a hundred years, and it fit nothing.

"Take it," he said. "It has never fit a lock. Perhaps it was meant for you to carry."

She held the key, and beyond the wall the humming rose like a welcome.

Chapter 10: The Gate

She went at dusk, alone, as the guards stood back at a nod from Bramble.

The snow had stopped. The air was so cold it hurt to breathe, and the wall stood grey and enormous before her, twelve feet of moss-eaten stone, and the iron gate in it was black with age and hung with frost like a beard. The red fruit glowed beyond it in the failing light, and the golden light lay over everything, and the humming filled her chest until she thought it would lift her off her feet.

She carried the great rusted key. It was too big for the lock — anyone could see that. The key was the size of her forearm, and the lock, what was left of it, was a small dark mouth the size of her thumb. They did not match. They had never matched. The key had been carried to the gate a hundred times in a hundred years, by a hundred worried hands, and it had never turned, because there was nothing in it to turn.

"It fits nothing," Hazel whispered to the gate. "Bramble says it was never meant to fit. He says perhaps it was meant for me to carry."

She lifted the key. She touched its rusted teeth to the black mouth of the lock, the way everyone had always done, and nothing happened, of course — the key did not go in, did not turn, did not catch on any mechanism, because there was no mechanism, there never had been.

And then she did what no one had ever done. She did not try to unlock it. She took the key away, and she set her hand flat on the iron bars, and she pushed.

The gate swung open.

Not with a groan, not with a shriek of a hundred years of disuse — it swung open silently, smoothly, on hinges that had never rusted, as if it had only been waiting for a hand that did not fight it. Some said later that it chimed. Hazel could not say. The humming rose so loud in that moment that the whole world was sound, and she stepped forward into the cold air that met her like a held breath released —

And the cold stopped.

Her foot came down, and the snow vanished at the threshold, and warmth came up to meet her like a tide. Warm air, golden light, leaves hanging motionless, apples heavy on the boughs — an eternal midsummer held inside the winter, and she stood at the edge of it, and her breath did not steam.

She looked back. The gate stood open behind her, and it did not close. It did not swing shut on its own, the way gates in stories do. It simply stood, open, patient, as if it had always been that way and would always be.

The key was heavy in her hand. She set it down on the stone inside the gate, where it would not be lost.

"The door was never locked," she said to the humming, and the humming swelled like an answer.

"Only unopened."

Chapter 11: The Orchard Inside

The gate swung shut behind her with a sound like a chime, and the snow ended at her heels.

Hazel stood on green grass. Grass. She had almost forgotten the word for it — the low, soft, breathing green of a summer meadow — and beneath her boots the earth was warm, not frozen, the way earth is only in July. Above her the golden light came down from no sun she could see, gentle and even, as if the air itself were lit. The wall behind her had vanished into the brightness. The cold had vanished with it. She had stepped through an iron gate in the middle of winter and found the summer that winter had been holding.

The orchard lay before her in rows like the lines of a song, deep and still and green. The trees stood full, heavy with apples, every leaf in place, every apple shining as if it had been polished that morning and no morning after. There was no wind. The leaves did not move. Hazel held her breath and watched them, and saw that they were not frozen — frozen things have a stiffness, a death about them — but waiting, the way a held breath waits, the way a bell waits before it is struck.

She put out her hand. A leaf lay against her palm, cool and alive, and beneath the warmth of the air the trunk beneath her fingers was cold — not the cold of winter, but a deeper cold, patient and slow, the cold of a long, long sleep. Somewhere above her the hum had grown so clear she could almost name the instrument. It came from everywhere at once, from the grass and the bark and the hanging fruit, and it was not a noise. It was a voice. It was the orchard talking to itself in its sleep.

Hazel walked the rows. She had expected to be afraid. She had brought the great rusted key like a talisman, and her mother's warnings like armor, and she found she did not need them. The apples hung over her head, red and gold, so close she could have reached up and taken one, and she did not. She walked. The grass whispered. The hum went with her, ahead of her, around her, like a guide showing her the way without once turning back to see if she followed.

Alone, she thought, and then corrected herself. Not alone. There was the orchard, and the orchard was listening. She could feel it in her teeth, the way you feel music in a stone floor.

She stopped at the foot of the first row and looked down its green length to where the trees met the brightness. The summer was holding its breath for her. Whatever the witch had done, the orchard had not wanted to be winter. It had wanted to be here, waiting.

Hazel hummed. The orchard hummed back.

Chapter 12: The Music

The hum had been with Hazel all her life, a low song like distant bees and strings, so faint the village called it nothing and she called it company. But here, inside the wall, the hum was no longer distant. It was in her. It had climbed in through her ears and settled under her skin, and now it beat with her heart, and the orchard heard it and answered, and Hazel understood that she had never once been humming alone.

She stood in the green middle of the first row and listened. Each tree was a note. The old gnarled trees at the far end carried the low part, dark and patient, the way a cello holds the bottom of a song; the young ones, lighter, nearer, sang higher, like flutes and the wind in a pipe. Between them the whole orchard moved in a music that had no beginning, because it had been going on for a hundred years, waiting for her to walk into it and take her part.

She took a step. The music did not stop, but it changed — one note grew louder, one tree leaned its attention toward her, and Hazel felt it the way you feel a room go quiet when you enter. She took another step, and another, and the music opened before her like a path, the loudest part of it drawing her down the rows, past apple trees and one small twisted tree at the row's end that hummed a single clear note and held it.

She hummed back. The note was in her throat before she had chosen it, the same low note she had hummed at her window all winter, and the tree heard it and answered a fifth above, and Hazel laughed — a real laugh, the first since the snow began — and the laughter went into the music too, bright, and the trees took it up and passed it along the rows like a ripple passing a pond.

That was when she knew they recognized her. It was not that they had seen her before. It was that she had been humming their song for sixteen years without knowing it, and they had been listening all that time, the way you listen for a voice you have almost forgotten. The orchard was not afraid of her. It was glad. It had been waiting for the one who hummed back.

Hazel walked on, into the loudest part of the music, toward the center of the orchard where the rows crossed and a stone cottage stood among the trees. And all around her the orchard sang, and she sang with it, and she was not an intruder. She was expected.

She had known it from the moment the gate chimed shut. She had only been waiting to be sure.

Chapter 13: The Sleep

At the crossing of the rows, where the path opened and the cottage stood, Hazel found the sleep made visible.

A leaf was hanging in the air at her shoulder, mid-fall, stopped so perfectly between branch and ground that she could see the tiny hinge where it had let go. She reached out and touched it, and it was warm, alive, not frozen — simply waiting to finish a fall that had begun a hundred years ago. Beside her, caught between two branches, a small grey bird hung with its wings half-spread, its bright eye fixed on some seed in the grass it would never reach, and Hazel felt her throat close with a strange and sudden pity.

The spell, she thought. This is the spell. Not a curse that hurt anything, but a hand that had come down on the world and said, wait. Wait, and do not finish. Wait until I can save you. The leaf had been falling for a hundred years. The bird had been meaning to eat for a hundred years. Nothing was dead. Everything was paused, held, breathing slowly in the golden air, and the holding itself was so gentle that Hazel could not call it wicked. She could only call it careful.

She walked to the oldest tree in the orchard, a great broad trunk covered in moss, its bark ridged like a closed and sleeping face. She laid her palm flat against it. Beneath the warmth of the air the tree was cold — not the cold of snow but the cold of depth, of a cellar, of something kept safe a long way down — and patient, impossibly patient, like a heart that had learned to beat once a century and was not done yet.

The tree dreamed. Hazel could feel it dreaming, the way you can feel a sleeping person dreaming if you sit close enough in the dark. It dreamed of fruit, she thought, and of rain, and of children climbing, and of the particular weight of apples at the end of summer. It dreamed of waking.

And that was the moment Hazel understood, dimly, the way you understand things that are too large for speech: sleep was not death. She had been taught that the orchard was cursed, that the witch had stolen its life. But nothing here was stolen. It was all here, held safe, waiting. The trees were not dead. They were only dreaming their way through a long, long night, and she had been sent, or had come, to tell them that morning had come at last.

She took her hand from the trunk, and the tree's dream did not break. It held her, warm and cold at once, and Hazel bowed her head and whispered to it.

"I see you," she said. "I see you sleeping. I'll come back."

The leaf, at her shoulder, had still not finished its fall.

Chapter 14: The Witch

The cottage at the center of the orchard had a door of grey wood, and on the door a wreath of last autumn's apples, dried and rosy, and before the door a woman was waiting.

Hazel stopped. All her life she had heard the witch told as a hag — crooked, raven-voiced, with a hook of a nose and a mouth that smiled to steal. The woman before her was none of that. She was tall, stooped, old in a way that was not frightening but simply long, like a tree that has stood through everything weather can send. She wore a gardener's apron over a dress that had been good a hundred years ago, and her hands hung at her sides with earth under the nails, and her face was lined and tired and unsurprised, as if she had been standing at that door a very long time and had known exactly when the gate would chime.

"So," the woman said. Her voice was low and dry, like wind through an old apple tree. "You've come."

Hazel could not find her words. The witch did not seem to need them. She looked at Hazel with eyes the color of bark in winter, and something in her face softened, and she said, very quietly:

"You've been humming for sixteen years. I've been listening."

The world went still around Hazel, the way it goes still when a thing you have carried alone is named aloud. She had never told anyone about the humming. It was her secret, her shame, her comfort. And this woman had heard it, all of it, every night at the window, every hummed reply, for sixteen years.

"Who are you?" Hazel whispered.

"I am the gardener of this orchard." The old woman's mouth moved in what might have been a smile. "The trees have a name for me. You will learn it when you have seen the truth."

Hazel felt the fear drain out of her — not all at once, but the way snow melts, from the edges inward. The hag of the village's story had earth under her nails and a wreath of dried apples and hands that looked like they had held a hundred summers. Nothing about her was a monster. Everything about her was a woman who had been working too long alone.

"Why?" Hazel said. "Why did you do it? Why did you curse them?"

The old woman looked past her, down the green rows to the sleeping trees, and her face was very old and very tired and not at all cruel.

"Come inside," she said. "You have carried my key across the snow, and I have kept the fire for you. Come inside, and I will show you — and you will see that the word you used is not the word that was done."

She turned and went into the cottage, and Hazel, without deciding to, followed her across the threshold.

The door was warm. The house was warm. And the hum, for the first time since the gate, had gone quiet, because the trees were listening too.

Chapter 15: The Gardener

The cottage was small and full of light, and it smelled of apples and honey and the warm dust of a house lived in a very long time. On the table lay a loaf of dark bread, a crock of honey, and two cups. Against the wall stood a jar of bees, slow and sleepy, thawed from their long winter; on the shelves, in neat rows, jars of stored fruit, sealed and rosy, the work of a hundred summers.

She moved slowly, the way a very old person moves, saving every gesture. She poured from a pitcher of water that smelled faintly of mint, and Hazel understood that this woman had kept house here, in this golden stillness, for a hundred years — that the stored fruit was hers, and the honey, and the bees, and through it all she had been alone.

Her hands, when she passed Hazel the bread, were the thing Hazel could not stop looking at. They were old hands, knotted, scarred — small pale scars across the palms like the map of a hundred years of work — and they moved with the terrible patience of someone who had never once been hurried. She caught Hazel looking and turned her hands over, showing the scars as one shows a garden.

"Knives," she said. "Twine. A hundred years of cutting and binding and waiting. They heal." She turned her hands over again, like pages. "My name is Yarrow. The trees gave it to me; I have kept it for them alone."

Hazel ate. The bread was good, dark and warm, and the honey was like light you could taste, and between bites she asked the question she had carried through the gate and across every green row.

"Why did you curse them?"

The old woman set down her cup. For a long moment she did not answer, and Hazel saw something pass across her face — not anger, not grief, but the weighing of a person deciding whether the teller is ready.

"Every child in Greengate for a hundred years," Yarrow said, "has been told that I cursed the orchard to steal its fruit. That I am a thief and a hag and the enemy of your village." Her eyes were kind. "It is not true. But I have had a hundred years to practice not defending myself, and I will not begin now with a girl who has eaten my bread. The story is wrong," she said. "And the correction is not a thing to be told. It is a thing to be shown."

She stood, and Hazel stood with her, and Yarrow led her out into the golden air and down the rows, past the sleeping trees, to a low shed at the far edge of the orchard with a door of iron, sealed and still.

"When you have seen this," Yarrow said, "you will understand why I did what I did. And you will understand," she added, very quietly, "why I was glad, these sixteen years, every time you hummed."

Hazel looked at the iron door, then at the old woman, and the fear that had melted in the cottage was gone. She was not afraid of the witch.

She was beginning to be afraid for her.

Chapter 16: The Truth

The shed door was iron and had not been opened in a long time. Yarrow took a key from her apron — not a great rusted thing like the one the Elder kept, but a small bright key worn smooth with use — and turned it, and the door swung inward on a smell that made Hazel step back.

Damp. Old. And something else, grey and faintly sweet, like fruit forgotten in a cellar. In the dim light, a rack of sealed glass jars stood against the far wall, and in the jars were pieces of wood — branches, bark, a small grey apple — covered in a pale fur that seemed to move, faintly, as Hazel watched.

"This is the Grey Blight," Yarrow said. "The thing that almost killed every orchard in the valley. It feeds on waking fruit. It spreads on the harvest wind, in a spore so fine you cannot see it. A hundred years ago it came to Greengate, and it was killing these trees, and there was no cure. Only one thing would have saved the valley: the axe."

Yarrow's voice did not change, but her hand on the door frame tightened.

"I would not let them be cut down. They were not mine, but I had watched them all my life. I was a gardener. I had bred them and pruned them and known their names. So I chose the only other way I could find. I laid a spell of sleep on the orchard — not to steal its fruit, not to curse your village, but to hold time still. The sleep held the blight at bay. It cannot feed on fruit that never ripens. It cannot spread on a wind that never blows. I made the orchard a cocoon, and I have been inside it, alone, breeding a wood the rust cannot kill, for a hundred years."

She turned to Hazel, and her face was naked in a way that hurt to look at.

"Your village's story was wrong in every detail but one. They said I took the fruit. I did take it. I took every apple that would have ripened, to keep the rust from it, and they fed me, and they kept the orchard alive. I took the fruit to keep it safe. I have been taking it and keeping it for a hundred years, so that one day the trees could wake and give it back."

The grey fur in the jars moved again, and Hazel understood the shape of it at last — the wall, the fear, the curse that was no curse. Not a spell of stealing. A hundred-year lullaby, sung over sleeping children by the one who loved them best, so that morning could come.

"Then it is breaking," Hazel said. "The apples ripening in the snow — it means they are ready."

Yarrow looked at her, and for the first time her tired face broke into a true smile.

"Yes. It means my wood is ready. It means the orchard can wake. It means," she said, and her voice was very soft, "that you and I have been working toward the same morning, all these years, without ever once meeting until today."

Chapter 17: The Blight

Yarrow reached into the shed and brought out one of the glass jars, and Hazel looked at it closely for the first time.

The piece of wood inside had been a branch once, smooth and grey, and now it was covered in a fur the color of ash, so fine it seemed to breathe. Where the fur met the bark, the wood beneath was soft, gone spongy and dark, and there was a smell off the jar — faint, sweet, like cider gone wrong — that made the back of Hazel's throat close.

"This is what it does," Yarrow said. "It does not strike like a storm. It comes quietly, on the wind, in the autumn when the fruit is heavy and the spores are loose. One orchard catches it, and the next, and the next. The rust finds a ripening apple and makes its nest in it, and the apple rots from the inside out, and every apple the tree makes after is poison. The trees do not die quickly. They die the way the valley would have died — slowly, orchard by orchard, until nothing is left but grey wood and a smell on the wind that you learn to dread."

She held the jar up to the light, and the fur glowed faintly, almost beautiful.

"The village did not know this. How could they? All they saw was a witch, and a wall, and fruit they were forbidden to touch. They made a story they could carry, because a story is a thing you can carry. Fear is a heavy thing, Hazel. A hundred years of it is heaviest of all. I did not blame them then, and I do not blame them now."

Hazel thought of the village — Bramble's locked box, the charms hung on doors, her mother's afraid eyes at the gate. She had thought, stepping through, that she was leaving the fear behind her. But it had been here all along, in a different shape: the fear of what would come if the sleep ever broke.

Yarrow had feared the blight coming true.

"Waking fruit," Hazel said slowly. "That is why it had to sleep. If the trees woke too soon, the rust would..." She stopped. "It would eat everything."

"Everything," Yarrow agreed. "The orchard, and then the valley's orchards, and then the village's cider and its winter stores. There was no waking them safely until the wood was ready to live in the open air. And for a hundred years, the wood was not ready." She set the jar back in its rack, her hand lingering on the glass. "Fear explained is fear half-disarmed. You know now what I was holding back. Not the fruit, but the rust."

She looked at Hazel, and her eyes were clear.

"The orchard in a mother's arms."

Hazel looked from the jar to the golden trees to the old woman's face, and she understood why the story had been wrong. It was told by the frightened, and the frightened tell the simplest story they can.

The true story had been told by no one, for a hundred years, because the only one who could tell it had been too busy keeping everyone safe to be heard.

Chapter 18: The Graft

In the back of the shed, away from the jars, Yarrow kept her workbench. Hazel had not noticed it at first — her eyes had gone to the grey fur, the sealed glass, the smell of the rust. But now Yarrow drew her past it, and Hazel saw what the shed had really been for all these years.

The bench was scarred wood, worn soft in the middle by a hundred years of leaning. On it lay a knife thin as a feather, a roll of twine the color of straw, a pot of wax, and a dozen small pots of earth, each with a stick of living green rising out of it. Hazel counted the pots. Fifteen. Sixteen. Each held a tiny tree, no taller than her hand, with leaves the color of new summer, and on each small trunk was a neat binding — a place where one kind of wood had been made to grow onto another.

"This is the graft," Yarrow said. "The only art I had to answer the rust with. You take living wood — a shoot from the old wild rootstock, the wood the valley's first trees grew from, the wood that has always lived in the open air — and you cut it clean and bind it onto a young orchard tree, and let the two grow into one. The wild wood gives its strength, its patience, its way of living. The orchard wood gives its fruit. And if you are patient, and you keep trying, and you fail a thousand times..."

She picked up one of the little pots and turned it in her hands, her face, in the dim light, soft as water.

"...one day you get a tree that bears as an orchard tree bears, and lives as the wild wood lives, and that the rust cannot touch. One tree like that, and you have won. From one tree you can take a hundred grafts, and from a hundred, ten thousand. You only have to save one tree, and then the next."

Yarrow's hands — scarred, knotted, patient — moved over the little tree as if it were a sleeping child.

"The grafts are my children too. I have no other. I raised each one from a cutting, watered it, sang to it, bound it to its new life. Most of them died, and I burned them, and I kept the ones that lived, and I tried again." She set the pot back among its fellows. "A hundred years of knives and twine, Hazel. A hundred years of waiting for one branch to grow true."

Hazel looked at the little pots and understood that the spell was only half the work. The sleep had held the orchard still. But the graft — the hundred years of binding new life onto old wood — that was the part the sleep had made possible. The sleep had given Yarrow time. And time, given to a gardener, becomes a kind of prayer.

"Why did you keep them here," Hazel asked, "in the shed, with the rust?"

"Because the rust is what made them. I keep them beside it to remind them — and myself — what they are for. They are not a garden. They are an answer."

Chapter 19: The Breeding

There was a book on the bench, bound in dark leather, and Yarrow set it before Hazel as if it were the most precious thing in the orchard.

"The orchard-book," Yarrow said. "A hundred years of entries. Every graft I made, every one that failed, every one that lived. You cannot breed a wood that can live in the open air without keeping a record, and a record is only honest if it keeps the failures too."

Hazel opened it. The pages were close-written, in a small neat hand that did not change from the first page to the last — a hand that had been steady for a hundred years. She read the early entries and felt her heart go cold.

"Grafted wild plum onto seedling, spring. Took, then died in the first frost. Burned." "Twelve cuttings, none took. The rust took the parent before harvest. Burned." "The pear is lost." "The west row is gone to the rust."

Page after page of failure, written without complaint, and then, slowly, the entries changed. "Took, and held through two winters." "Held. Fruit small but sound." "Held. The rust touched the leaves, and the leaves threw it off." And at last, half a century old, an entry in the same steady hand:

"The wood is ready. But the world is not."

Yarrow had written it fifty years ago. Hazel looked up at her.

"Fifty years," Hazel said. "You knew fifty years ago. And you waited."

"I waited," Yarrow said. "The wood was ready to live, but the village was not ready to receive it. Fifty years ago they feared the witch too much to take anything from her hand. A gift offered to frightened people is not a gift; it is a threat in a coat of kindness. So I kept the wood, and I kept it true, and I waited for a morning when someone would come through the gate who could hear the orchard hum — who could carry the wood out and be believed."

She turned the pages of the book with a finger, and Hazel saw the last entry, dated, impossibly, this winter:

"The frost does not touch the Ready Tree's apples. The ripening has begun. She is coming."

Hazel's breath caught. "She?"

"You," Yarrow said simply. "The midwinter ripening is not the spell breaking by accident. It is the graft's answer to the frozen years — the wood telling me, at last, that it is done waiting. And the apple that fell to you, Hazel, the one you hid in your apron and have carried in your coat ever since, fell to you because the orchard chose you."

The book lay open on the bench between them, a hundred years of patience inked onto paper, and Hazel understood that she was not standing in a cursed orchard with a witch.

She was standing in a seedbed, at harvest time, and the hundred years had ripened at last — readiness and the world, meeting for the first time.

Chapter 20: The Ready Tree

Yarrow led Hazel out of the shed and down the rows to a tree that Hazel had not noticed before, because it had not wanted to be noticed until now.

It stood near the well, a young tree among old ones, no taller than her mother's kitchen door, and it was — Hazel could not find another word — ready. The other trees slept, heavy and still. But this one was awake. Its leaves moved in a breeze that touched nothing else, its branches were bent with fruit, and its apples were deep red, shining as if they held a light inside them.

"The Ready Tree," Yarrow said. "The grafted descendant of the Old Queen — the oldest tree in the orchard, the one you put your hand on and felt dreaming. I bound her granddaughter onto wild stock and bred her through a hundred years, and she is the first of them all to be done." She reached up and took an apple, warm and red, and held it out. "Take it."

Hazel took it. It was warm in her hand, ripe, unblemished, and she turned it over and knew it before she looked — the weight, the particular curve, the deep red glow. She had held this exact apple before, in her apron, on a winter's day, on the other side of the wall.

"It is the same," she said. "This is the apple that fell to me."

"The same tree, at least," Yarrow said. "It fell over the wall to you, Hazel, and to no one else, because it was the Ready Tree's first gift to the world, and the orchard gave it to the one who hummed. You are holding its brother now. The orchard has known for sixteen years who was coming."

Hazel looked down at the apple in her hands, and she thought of the seed — the one perfect seed she had taken from the first apple, wrapped in a cloth, and kept in the pocket of her coat every day since, afraid she had done wrong. It was not wrong. It was the whole purpose. Her small theft — the village would have called it theft — was the orchard's gift, sent over the wall to the only hands that would know to keep it.

"Open it," Yarrow said.

Hazel broke the apple in two with her thumbs. In the pale heart of the fruit lay one seed, perfect and brown and shining, exactly like the one in her coat.

"That seed," Yarrow said, "is a Ready Tree seed. And the one you keep is the same. Do you understand now, Hazel? Everything you hid, everything you feared you had stolen — it was never theft. It was a gift, carried in the only way the orchard could reach you. You have been carrying its future, all winter, in your coat."

The Ready Tree moved in the windless air, and a single petal — impossible, in January — fell and settled on Hazel's hand, light as a blessing.

Hazel closed the fruit in her hands, seed safe in its heart.

"Then it is time," she said. "It is time to wake them."

"It is time," Yarrow said. "And you are the one who will carry it out."

Chapter 21: The Carrying

Yarrow chose her branch slowly, the way a person chooses a last word. She walked the Ready Tree three times round, touching the bark, listening the way Hazel listened, and at last she stopped at the low branch that faced the well, the branch that had ripened first.

"This one," she said. "This one has been waiting longest."

She cut with a knife worn thin as a leaf, and the tree did not flinch. For a moment the whole orchard held its breath with her; the hum sank to a thread, then rose again, deeper, as if the tree had said yes. Yarrow bound the cut with twine from her apron, wrapped the end in damp cloth, and set the branch into a pot of warm orchard earth.

"It is heavy," she said.

It was heavier than any branch that size had a right to be. Hazel lifted it and felt something pulse against her palms, once, twice, like a heartbeat. It was alive. It had only been waiting to be carried.

"Now listen," Yarrow said. "Water it with winter rain, never well water. Sing to it. Hum the way you hum to us; it knows your voice, it heard you in the apple." She touched the cloth at the cut. "And carry it always in the orchard's own earth. The earth remembers what the wood is learning."

"Will it live out there? In the cold?"

"Cold is not its enemy," Yarrow said. "Only doubt. And fire. And the rust." She said the last word softly, as a mother speaks a child's fever. "Keep it from the rust, and it will teach the whole valley to do the same."

They stood a moment under the Ready Tree, and the hum rose and fell around them, and Hazel understood that the pot was the small part of the burden. The song was the large part.

"The gate will open for the burdened," said Yarrow. "It always has. Go now, while the light holds."

At the rows she put her hand on Hazel's shoulder. She smelled of earth and apples and winter honey. "Bring them home," she said. "I will be here."

Hazel walked toward the gate. Behind her the orchard hummed a farewell, a long low note held under all the others, the way a mother hums to a child she does not want to watch walk out the door. Hazel did not look back. If she looked back she would set the pot down and never leave, and the pot had to leave.

The gate stood open. The warm air met the cold at the threshold and turned to mist, and through the mist the grey winter waited like the other side of a held breath.

She stepped through.

Chapter 22: The Return

The cold hit her like a second body. Hazel gasped, and the snow came down on her shoulders, and for a moment the winter was so grey and huge after the golden air that she thought she had dreamed the orchard, until the pot in her arms steamed.

There was a watch-fire at the wall, and men around it with lanterns and pitchforks. An hour inside, she thought, and the fire had burned down and been rebuilt.

"Girl!" Elder Bramble came striding through the drifts, coat undone. "What happened? What did you see?"

And then Maren was there, catching Hazel before she could answer, her mother's cold hands on Hazel's face, her voice saying her name over and over like a charm. "You're whole."

"I'm whole, Mum."

"The gate," said Bramble. "It was open, and you were gone, and we..."

"I'm back," Hazel said. The pot steamed in her arms; the branch stood green and stubborn against the snow. "And I've brought..." Her tongue was full of warm air and honey and the deep hum of a hundred trees, and none of it fit the cold words of the night. "The witch is a gardener."

The silence was worse than any shouting. Bramble's face did what she had seen faces do at the meeting when a truth too strange came in. It closed.

"A gardener," he said.

"She's called Yarrow. She's old and kind, and the trees aren't cursed, Elder, they're sleeping. She put them to sleep to save them. There's a blight."

"You've been enchanted," said Bramble. It was not anger; it was certainty. "The witch has sung to you, and you've come back carrying her twig, her spell."

"It's a graft! It's what will wake them."

But the cold was in her voice now, and her words came out thin, and the hum had gone quiet inside her, muffled by the snow and the firelight and all the faces that did not believe. She looked down at the pot and suddenly it looked like what they saw: a twig, in a pot, carried by a girl the witch had sung to.

"Put it down," said Bramble.

"No." Maren's voice, clear as a frost bell. "She's walked out of a hundred years of that wall and you will not make her drop what she carried out of it."

Bramble looked at Maren, and something in his face bent. "Tomorrow, then. The council decides tomorrow. Tonight we all get warm, and you'll tell us the whole of it, Hazel. And we will listen."

It was a door, Hazel thought, held open a crack. But as she turned toward the village with her mother's hand in hers and the pot's warmth against her chest, she knew what waited on the other side: they would listen, and they would not hear.

Chapter 23: The Teaching

The meeting house was full to the door. Every lamp was lit, and the heat of a hundred bodies pressed against the frost on the windows, and Hazel stood at the front with the pot of orchard earth at her feet and the green branch behind her like a witness.

She told them everything.

The golden air and the still leaves and the bird frozen between two branches. The apple that fell to her and the seed she had kept. The cottage and the old woman in the gardener's apron, and the hum, always the hum. And then the part she had not known how to say: the blight.

"It came a hundred years ago," she said. "It rode in on the harvest wind. It fed on waking fruit, on the ripening, on the sweetness itself. Every orchard in the valley went grey and then black. There was no cure. Only felling, or the sleep."

"The sleep," said Elder Bramble. His voice was careful, the voice he used for the old stories. "The curse."

"It isn't a curse. It's a lullaby. She put them to sleep to keep the blight from feeding. She didn't steal the fruit; she kept it alive. She's been breeding a graft that resists the rust for a hundred years. Under the frozen years, the seasons still turned in the wood." She lifted the pot. "This is it. This is the Ready Tree. It ripened in midwinter because it was done."

The word went around the room and changed as it went. Wake. Wake.

Then Bramble stood, and the room went still the way it went still for him.

"A hundred years of closed gates and hung charms and a fear we were born into," he said. "Our fathers' fathers locked their doors against her. And you come back from one afternoon with a twig in a pot and tell us we were wrong about all of it. Which is likelier: that she cast a spell to steal our fruit, or that a girl who hears singing where the rest of us hear silence walked into a spell and came out carrying it?"

The room murmured. Hazel felt the pot against her ankles, felt the branch listening, and she had no answer, because she could not make anyone hear what she heard.

But she saw two faces.

Her mother, Maren, whose hand lay still on her lap, whose eyes were wet and who was not shaking her head.

And Mrs. Quince, at the cider press in the corner, who had said nothing all night and had leaned forward, very slightly, the way a person leans toward a fire she suspects will catch.

The teaching was done. The believing had not begun. But in two hearts, Hazel saw, the seed had been dropped into earth.

And she knew what a graft must feel like, bound to wood that does not trust it, waiting for the sap to rise.

Chapter 24: The Elder's Doubt

The council met without her. Hazel heard them from the cold room above the cider press, their voices carrying across the square like the thrum of a struck string: Bramble's low and level, the others rising and falling around it. She sat with the pot behind her on the shelf, the branch hidden under her mother's winter blanket, and she listened.

"The witch sings to the girl," Bramble was saying. "We have all heard her humming. Humming to nothing. Humming to a wall. And now she walks out of that gate with a green branch and calls it a gift."

"It was the apple," someone said. "The first apple fell to her."

"Exactly my point. Why her? Why a girl who hears songs in stone? Because the witch chose her. Everything Hazel says is a song the witch put in her mouth."

Hazel pressed her hand to the wall. She wanted to go down and make them touch the green leaves in January, hold the pot of warm earth. But she remembered the look on Bramble's face at the gate, certainty, not anger, and she knew that a proof carried by an enchanted girl was no proof at all.

Then Bramble's voice, dropping: "Tomorrow the twig goes on the fire. The gate goes back to the key. And the girl does not go near the wall again."

Hazel's heart went cold. She sat a long time in the dark, the hum faint and far, like a song heard through a locked door. And then came the knock, soft, three times, the way Pip knocked.

Pip's face appeared around the door, snow in his hair. "I heard them. They'll take it at first light." He looked at the branch under the blanket. "The cider cellar. Mum's gone to the storehouse. There's a cold corner behind the old casks where the frost doesn't reach. I'll help you carry it down."

It took the better part of the night, the two of them, because the pot was heavy and the branch caught on every doorway, and Pip kept whispering, "It's alive, Hazel, it's warm," and Hazel kept shushing him, and the whole time the village slept the sleep of the unconvinced, and the great rusted key, the key that fit no lock and had guarded nothing at all, hung on the meeting-house wall.

They set the pot in the cold corner. Pip piled sacks of dried apple over it. Hazel put her hand on the branch one last time and felt it listening, patient, alive, a held breath.

"Tomorrow I'll go to the wall," Hazel said, "and I'll make them look at what's really there."

Pip's eyes went wide. "They'll stop you."

"Maybe." Hazel wrapped the blanket back around the branch. "But a graft doesn't take unless someone binds it and waits. I know what waiting is now." She looked up through the grate at the cold stars. "I've had a hundred years of it taught to me."

Chapter 25: The Proof

The graft bloomed on the third night of its hiding.

Hazel found it in the cellar by smell, apple blossom impossibly sweet in the winter dark, and when she lifted the sacks there were five small white flowers on the carried branch, open and perfect, the color of the snow outside the grate. She stood a long time with the lantern held still, because she had seen apple blossom in January and there was no word for that except the truth.

She did not hide it this time. She carried the pot up the cellar stairs and across the square in the grey morning, and the people who saw her stopped, and by the time she reached the meeting house there was a crowd, and the crowd was silent.

Mrs. Quince came out first. She stood in her door with her apron on, looked at the blossom, looked at Hazel, and said, "Well." It was the only word, and she said it like a bell.

Elder Bramble came last. He stood before the pot a long time, and Hazel saw his hand move toward the pocket where the key rode, and stop. "Blossom," he said at last. "In January."

"Midwinter ripening," Hazel said. "The wall-side apples have been on the trees a hundred years. Take them down. Keep them a fortnight in your own cellar. They don't rot. The rust can't eat them."

Bramble did not take an apple. But the crowd did. The crowd did everything Hazel asked, because the blossom had done what no speech could do: it had made the strange thing visible.

That fortnight was the proof's slow turning. The wall-side apples came down by the basket, heavy, red, perfect, and lay in the village's cellars and on its windowsills, and they did not soften, did not spot, did not wrinkle. The children ate them and were not poisoned. The birds ate the windfalls and flew away fat and loud. And Widow Cole, who had locked her door against the curse for seventy years, bit into a hundred-year-old apple and said, "It tastes like my mother's orchard," and began to cry.

But the proof that broke the council was Mrs. Quince's.

She pressed a whole basket of the wall-side apples, ran them through her press, and stood in the square with a cup of the cider in the winter light and drank it down, slow, in front of everyone. Then she held the cup up to Elder Bramble.

"Not poison, Elder. Not curse. It's the best apple I've pressed in sixty years of pressing, and I'll stake my casks on it." She wiped her mouth. "If this is what the witch kept safe for us, then we owe her something more than fear."

The council did not vote that night; it could not agree. But the wall-side apples were not returned to the wall, and the order to burn the branch was never spoken again. And when Hazel went to sleep, the hum of the orchard was louder than it had been since she came out of the gate, as if through all the stone and the winter and the doubt, the trees could feel the world leaning, at last, toward waking.

Chapter 26: The Waking

It began in the middle of the night, and it began as a hum.

Hazel woke to it in the dark, but this was not the hum she knew, the low distant song she had carried all her life like a second heartbeat. This was louder. She heard windows opening up and down the lane, and voices calling, and the sound was everywhere, rising: a low enormous note, the way a hundred bees would sound if they had a hundred years of spring to sing.

She ran barefoot through the snow.

The square was full. People stood in their nightclothes, with lanterns and without them, and everyone was looking at the wall. The wall, twelve feet of moss-eaten grey stone that had stood a hundred years, was humming. The stones themselves were humming, the frost on top shaking loose in small bright flakes. The iron gate vibrated, a shiver running up and down the bars like a note struck on a harp.

"Open it," someone said. "For love's sake, open the gate."

"The key," began Bramble, and stopped, because the key was in his pocket and he knew, they all knew now, what the key had never been for. He took it out, held it up, and the whole village watched him walk to the gate that fit no lock, and press his hand to it, and the gate swung open on its own, as it had opened for Hazel, as it had always opened.

The light came out like a held breath let go. It was golden and warm, and it smelled of apples and of earth after rain, and it spilled over the snow and the white faces of the village like a slow summer pouring out of a door.

The leaves shivered first, every leaf at once, the sound of it like rain coming fast over a field. The trees stood full and green and heavy, and then they moved, truly moved, the first real movement in a hundred years, and the apples deepened where the light touched them, and the air above the wall filled with light in rings, the way a pond fills when a stone is dropped.

"The spell is gone," Hazel whispered. She was still holding the pot; she had run with it, barefoot through the snow, and she had not spilled a single grain of its earth.

Behind her the village stood silent. An old man fell to his knees in the snow, and Hazel did not know if he was praying or weeping, and it did not matter.

She set the pot down at the threshold of the gate, in the warm light, where the branch of the Ready Tree could see, for the first time in a hundred years, the orchard it had been bred for. And the orchard, waking, leaned toward it like a mother toward a child's hand.

The hum became a song. It had been a hum for a century, and now it was a song, and the song had words in it, though no one could have told you what they were. But Hazel could. She heard it clearly, the way she had always heard it, and the words were: come home, come home, come home.

Chapter 27: The Night Bloom

The bloom came with the moon.

All night the orchard worked. Hazel walked its rows with the pot under one arm, and the trees worked around her, visibly, impossibly, the way the whole of spring works in a single breath. She watched buds break on branches that had held the same green fruit for a hundred years, watched them swell and split and open, the blossom coming up pale in the moonlight like snow falling backward into the sky.

She had thought the winter orchard was beautiful. She had not known what beauty was.

The blossom covered the trees to the tips. The air was thick with it, white and gold and faintly blue, and the petals came down on her shoulders and on the pot, and under the blossom the apples were forming, green knobs that swelled as she watched, that reddened at the top first and then down, like cheeks taking a blush. The orchard hummed with the work. Not a hum now, a chorus, each tree a note, the notes bound into a chord so low and so full that Hazel felt it in her chest, in her teeth, in the roots of her hair.

She came to the Old Queen first. Its blossom was the thickest of all, a cloud of white so dense the branches bent under it. Hazel touched its trunk, and the hum went quiet in a circle around her.

"You waited," she said, very softly. "You waited the whole time."

And she thought she felt, under her palm, the answer: not words, but what a tree has instead of words, a deep and patient gladness, the way a held breath feels when it is finally allowed to end.

Beyond the wall, the village had gathered. They stood at the edge of the dark, silent, wrapped in blankets and sheepskins, and they watched the impossible thing happen in their valley: a hundred years of spring let out all at once, blooming by moonlight over the wall their fathers had built against it.

Pip was at the gate. He was too small to see over; she saw him jump, and jump again, and then a man lifted him onto his shoulders, and Pip went still, and she heard his small clear voice across the hummed dark: "It's pretty, Hazel. It's so pretty."

She laughed. She had not laughed in the orchard before; there had been no room for it. And the chorus changed, opened a little, took the laugh in, made a place for it.

The trees, she understood, had missed laughter.

She walked the rows until the moon went down, and when she came to the gate at last, with the first grey of dawn in the east, she looked back and saw the orchard whole: green and white and heavy with apples, awake after a hundred years, done with waiting.

The gate stood open.

"Come in," Hazel said to the silent village on the other side of the wall. "It's safe. It was always safe."

And the first person across the threshold was Pip, who had been waiting his whole short life for an open door, and who ran in laughing under the apple boughs as if he had been expected all along.

Chapter 28: The One-Night Harvest

The orchard was heavy with morning.

No one who saw the bloom had slept. When the sun came up the orchard stood in full summer light, the fruit so thick that the boughs bent nearly to the ground, each apple red enough to seem lit from inside. The air smelled of ripe fruit and warm earth.

At the gate the village gathered, timid, with baskets and ladders and carts, and yet they stood at the edge of the open gate and did not come through. A hundred years of fear does not dissolve in a night. It stands at the gate in its boots and holds its baskets and waits.

Maren was the first grown one. She walked through the gate as if through deep water, and Hazel met her, and her mother put out a hand and touched a hanging apple, then picked it, slowly, and bit into it with her eyes closed.

Maren began to cry. "It's summer," she said, her mouth full. "It's, Hazel, it's summer in my mouth."

And the village came through, like water through a broken dam: old men and small children, women with aprons full, everyone laughing or crying or both. The first ladders went up against the oldest trees. The first baskets filled. And the laughter, that was the strangest thing and the truest: the orchard had not heard laughter in a hundred years, and now it heard all of it at once.

Pip organized the children like a general. He shook the low branch of the Little Twig and the apples came down in a red rain, and their noise was the loudest thing in the orchard, louder than the hum, and Hazel found she did not mind. The hum, she thought, was enjoying itself.

Mrs. Quince stood in the middle of it all with a basket on each arm, like a woman who had found buried treasure. "These are the best apples in the history of apples. These will be wine. These will be legend."

"Eat first," said Maren, laughing. "Wine later."

They ate first. Everyone ate first. The apples tasted of summer, of the one perfect day of July kept in the memory for the whole year, and of something else the village could not name but could feel: forgiveness, heavy and sweet as a fruit that had waited a century to be picked.

By noon the carts were full. By evening the orchard stood bare and the village lingered in the long light, tired, stained with juice to the elbows, unwilling to leave. The gate stood open behind them, and for the first time in anyone's memory, the village and the orchard were the same place.

Hazel stood at the well, beside the pot of the Ready Tree, and watched them. She put her hand on the green branch that had saved them, and the hum came up through her palm like water through roots.

"You did it," she said. "You woke them."

And she was not sure whether she spoke to the branch, to herself, or to the old woman in the cottage at the orchard's heart, who had kept the light in so that the village could walk into it on a single morning.

Chapter 29: The Village Sees

It took the village three days to see the orchard.

The harvest came first, the work, the joy, the full carts. But on the third morning, when the picking was done, Hazel led them on the walk she had been waiting to lead, from the gate to the cottage at the heart of the rows, and the village followed in a loose, uncertain line, the way people follow a guide through a place they have been taught all their lives to fear.

She showed them the frozen bird first, or she tried to. It was not frozen anymore. It perched in the full light on the topmost branch of the Old Queen, and as the village watched it lifted its head and sang, one long note, and flew. A hundred years had passed for that bird in an instant, and it sang as if it had been promised this.

The shed was next. It stood at the back of the rows, small and sealed, and the village drew back from it by instinct, the way they had always drawn back from the wall. But Yarrow was there, waiting by the door, the first time most of them had seen her, and she was not what the stories had made of her. She was tall and old and tired, with earth under her nails and an apron over a dress a hundred years old, and she looked at the village with patience, and a kind of worn-out hope.

"This is the reason," she said. Her voice was low and rough, and the village leaned in to hear. She opened the shed, and the smell came out, grey and dry and wrong, and inside, under glass, were branches gone grey with fur, the rust that fed on waking fruit.

"The blight," Hazel said. "It came a hundred years ago and killed every orchard in the valley. It would have killed ours. She chose the sleep instead of the felling."

Yarrow looked at the grey wood a long time. "I was not a witch," she said, to the branches, to the village, to the years. "I was a gardener. I could not cure it, and I could not bear to cut them down. So I put them to sleep. And I bred a wood that could live in the waking world again." She closed the shed. "And I kept your apples for you. All of them. There was never a theft. Only a keeping."

The silence was enormous. Hazel saw shame move through them like a cold wind, saw Widow Cole cover her mouth. One man, the cooper they called Old Sorrow, went down on one knee. It was not worship. It was the body doing what the body does when it discovers it has been afraid of a mercy.

"Get up," Yarrow said softly. "Get up, all of you. I did not keep them for thanks. I kept them for the waking."

And the village, awkward, ashamed, beginning to be grateful, did not know what to do with their hands. But they put their hands on the trees, palm flat against the bark, and they felt the hum come up through the wood, and whatever they had believed, something in them opened.

Chapter 30: The Gratitude

The gifts began the next morning and did not stop.

They were not grand; the village had nothing grand. They were the small honest things of a farming people apologizing with their hands: a jar of honey from the thawed bees, a loaf of new flour, a coat sewn in a night. Widow Cole brought the last skin of cider she had pressed in the year of the curse, kept sixty years in her cellar, and set it on the cottage step without a word, and Yarrow did not make her speak.

The village came in ones and twos all week, the strangeness wearing thin; they learned the cottage, the low hearth, the hanging fruit. They learned that she took tea and did not mind children, and that she sang to the trees at dusk in a voice like a worn-out bell.

At the end of the week, Elder Bramble came.

Hazel saw him from the rows, walking alone through the gate he had never entered, good coat, polished boots, as for a funeral or a festival. He carried nothing. At the cottage he stopped, and Yarrow came to the door, and the two stood a long moment in the autumn light.

Bramble took the key from his pocket. The great rusted key that had hung in the meeting house, that his father had hung there, carried a hundred years against a lock that had never existed.

"It never fit," he said, his voice not steady. "I have known it since I was a boy. But it was the thing we had. It was the shape of the fear. We polished it. We hung it on the wall. We carried it to the gate and back and called it safety." He held it out to her, both hands. "It is yours. It was always yours. I am sorry. We are all sorry."

Yarrow looked at the key. Then she reached out and took it, and turned it over, and the rust came off on her fingers like old dried earth.

"It was never locked," she said gently. "It was only unopened. The gate has opened for every hand that tried it, child; it opened for yours the day you were brave enough to carry this key to it." She pressed the key back into his palms. "There is nothing to forgive, Elder. There is only the door. It is open, and has always been open, and you have walked through it at last."

Bramble wept. He stood in the middle of the orchard he had feared his whole life, the rusted key in his hands, and wept the way a man weeps when a weight carried so long that he forgot it was heavy is finally lifted. The orchard hummed around him, soft, the way it hummed around all of them now, and Hazel understood that the hum was not for her.

It was the orchard forgiving the village for a hundred years of fear.

It was the garden telling the gardener: you were right to wait.

And Bramble, who had believed the curse because it was the only story he had, stood at the heart of the true story, the one about a woman who had kept the light in, and let it hold him.

Chapter 31: The Witch's Rest

The spell broke, and something in the witch broke with it. For a hundred years the sleep had held Yarrow as surely as it held the trees — the same held breath, the same long patience — and now that the breath was out, she was nothing but her age. She slept till noon. She moved slowly, the way the frozen bird had moved when it first came awake, blinking at a world that had gone on without it. She was not sad.

"I only kept them warm for you," she said one morning, and said it as simply as a woman saying good morning.

Hazel found her at the cottage table with the orchard-book open before her, the great leather volume of a hundred years of entries. "Come," said Yarrow, "and I will show you the keeping." She showed Hazel the knots — how the twine was tied so that the graft would not strangle itself as it grew. She showed the watering — winter rain, never well-water, because the rain carried the sky's patience. She showed the singing, the low hum that kept the trees company, the lullaby that told them someone was awake and watching.

"Will you remember?" Yarrow asked.

Hazel remembered. She tied the knots until her fingers learned them. She carried water from the rain-barrel. And in the evenings, when Yarrow slept, Hazel walked the rows and hummed, and the trees hummed back, and she thought she understood at last why the orchard had called her — not to wake it, but to keep it.

The days passed. Yarrow slept more and walked less. She began to leave things for Hazel to find — a knife laid across a folded apron, a coil of twine, the orchard-book left open to the first page, where a hand that was not Yarrow's had written, in ink gone brown with age: Begin here. This is the keeping. Whoever hums, holds.

Hazel picked up the knife. It was warm from Yarrow's hand. She did not know she was crying until the tears fell on the open page, and she wiped them away with the edge of her sleeve so that they would not touch the writing.

That night she dreamed she was a tree, and in the dream she heard herself being sung to sleep by a voice she had known all her life, and she woke knowing the song. She went to the window. The orchard lay white under the moon, and at the cottage door stood Yarrow, wrapped in a shawl, not asleep, watching the rows as a shepherd watches sheep.

"They are yours now," said the old woman, not turning. "I only held them for you."

Hazel said nothing. She came and stood beside her. For a long time they watched the sleeping trees together, two gardeners in the cold, and the orchard hummed its long low note, like a breath held, like a promise kept.

Chapter 32: The New Gardener

Yarrow gave Hazel the knife on a morning of thin grey light, and the twine, and the small pot of orchard earth. "The tools are yours," she said. "The wood will teach you the rest."

Hazel's first graft was a disgrace. She cut the scion too long, bound it too loose, tied the knot the way she had been shown and watched it come undone at once. The wood slipped. The twine tangled. She was nearly crying with it when Yarrow's hand, still and gentle, covered hers.

"Again," said Yarrow. "The tree does not count your failures. It counts only the one time you hold."

So Hazel held. She cut, and bound, and tied, and every knot held longer than the last. By the third day her hands were scarred like Yarrow's, small pale lines, a map of beginning. On the seventh day she made a graft and did not need to undo it. She stepped back, and the Old Queen's hum rose a little, a note of approval, or what Hazel had come to know as approval.

"Listen," said Yarrow. "They are telling you it is right."

"Did they tell you that, a hundred years ago?"

Yarrow looked at the trees. "They told me I was patient," she said. "It was all the praise I needed."

The graft was a branch of the Ready Tree, bound onto the old wild rootstock at the orchard's edge. It was the first graft Hazel had made with her own hands, and it would be the first of hundreds. She watered it with winter rain. She hummed to it. And in the evenings Maren came to the orchard gate and watched her daughter work, and did not speak, because there was nothing to say that the trees were not already saying.

"She's good," Yarrow said once, when Maren stood in the doorway of the cottage. "She has the gift."

Maren looked at the rows, at her daughter's small figure moving among the trunks, and her pride came up in her like a tide. "She was always listening," Maren said. "I was afraid of what she might hear."

"She heard the orchard," said Yarrow. "There is nothing in this world better to hear."

That night Hazel slept with the knife under her pillow, as Yarrow had slept with it for a hundred years, and dreamed of a graft that grew into a tree that grew into a forest, and woke with her hands remembering the knots.

The fear of failing had gone out of her. What remained was the work, and the work was endless, and the work was hers.

Chapter 33: Hazel's Role

Hazel's days had two shapes now. At dawn she was a gardener. She rose before the light, walked the rows with the knife at her belt, checked the knots and the watering, hummed the trees awake. The orchard knew her step. The Old Queen's branch inclined toward her as she passed, the way a sleeping mother turns toward a child's voice.

At noon she was still a girl. Pip dragged her sledding on the hill above the wall — the wall that was no longer a wall to her, only stone. They had snowball fights. They stole sips of Mrs. Quince's cider. Hazel laughed with her mouth full of snow and was twelve and sixteen at once, a girl and a keeper, and for a few hours the two selves were the same person.

At dusk she was a gardener again. She walked the orchard with Yarrow, or with the book, and the village's new respect was a thing she felt on her back like a hand. They nodded to her now. They saved her a seat at the meeting house. But they did not invite her to their firesides, and she understood: the gardener belonged to the orchard, and a thing that belongs to the orchard is not quite one of them.

It was the loneliness she had not expected. Her mother felt it too, and sat with her longer in the evenings, and did not fill the silence. "You chose it," Maren said once, not unkindly.

"No," said Hazel. "It chose me. I only stopped refusing."

At night, when the girl-self was asleep, the gardener-self lay awake and listened. The humming came up through the floor, through the cold, through the snow — the orchard singing to itself in the dark, patient as weather. She hummed back, and the two songs met somewhere between her bed and the trees, and the loneliness thinned until it was only the shape of the song.

She learned the cost of the gift: the early mornings, the scarred hands, the distance. And she learned its wages: the moment at dawn when the first light found the orchard, and the trees turned gold, and she was the one who was there to see it.

"Every keeper pays," Yarrow said. "The trees pay too — they pay in years. But they would rather pay than not bear."

Hazel pressed her hand to the Old Queen's trunk, felt the deep cold patience under the warmth. "Is it worth it?" she asked.

The Old Queen did not answer in words. But the hum rose, and Hazel heard in it the one-night bloom, the hundred years of held spring let go at once, and she thought: yes. Yes, it is worth it.

Chapter 34: The Orchard Open

The gate was left open. Not on purpose, at first — Hazel simply forgot to close it one evening, and the next morning she found the snow by the threshold crossed by footprints, small and hesitant, as though the village had come to look and then been afraid to be seen. But nothing had been taken. Nothing had been cursed. The orchard had simply been visited, and the orchard had not minded.

So the gate stayed open. Then a door was cut in the wall, a small arched door, low enough that a child had to duck — cut not to let anyone in, but to let the wall know it was no longer a border. Bramble stood at the cutting and wept, though he said it was the cold wind.

The visits began slowly. First the bold ones, the children. Then the curious. Then, one clear afternoon, old Father Cinder came hobbling through the gate with a stool under his arm, and sat under the Old Queen, and fell asleep there, and woke at dusk saying the dream had been the kindest he had had in seventy years. After that, the old people came.

They came to walk the rows — the men and women who had spent their lives afraid of the twelve feet of grey stone. They touched the trunks. They read the names Hazel had taught them, the Whisper Tree, the Little Twig. Some of them cried. Most of them sat. The orchard held them the way it held the frozen bird, with a patience that asked for nothing.

"All those years," said old Widow Thistle, standing where the wall's shadow had once fallen, "I would not have come through that gate if the world was burning. And look — it was only an orchard. It was only ever an orchard."

The orchard became the village's heart. Lovers walked it at dusk. Children played at its edges. The well at the center, where the Ready Tree grew, became a place to meet, to talk, to be still. On Sundays after the meeting house, the whole village drifted through the door in the wall as naturally as water finds a channel.

What had been forbidden became home. The wall was still standing, but no one looked at it anymore. They looked at the trees.

Hazel watched it happen and understood, dimly, that this was the second waking — not of the orchard, but of the village. The trees had woken in a night. The people woke more slowly, one small visit at a time, and each visit was a stone taken from the wall.

"Give them time," said Yarrow, watching the children run through the rows. "A hundred years of fear is a heavy thing. It cannot be lifted in a day. It can only be set down, stone by stone."

Chapter 35: The Children

Pip was the first child through the open gate, of course. He had been waiting for permission all his short life, and the moment the door in the wall existed, he was through it, whooping, with a pack of smaller children behind him like a wake behind a boat.

The orchard changed how they played. You could not have a war among the sleeping trees — the trees did not allow it. The hum rose when they shouted, a low note like a mother clearing her throat, and the children lowered their voices without knowing why, and the war became a procession, and the procession became a game of hide-and-seek among trunks that seemed to hold their secrets. Pip hid in the Whisper Tree. No one found him for an hour, and when they did, he was asleep against its roots, smiling.

Hazel taught them the names. "This is the Old Queen, the oldest. This is the Whisper Tree — she tells the wind what to say. This is the Little Twig, who was small and is small no longer." She taught them how to touch a tree — palm flat, patient, like a greeting — and the children pressed their hands to the bark and went quiet, feeling for the hum.

When the first thaw came, the children gathered the seeds from the one-night harvest by the handful. They planted them in a row at the orchard's edge — a crooked row, because children do not plant straight, and the orchard did not seem to mind. Hazel showed them how to press the soil, how to water, how to sing a little, off-key, and not to care.

"Why sing?" asked little Trill, who was six and believed everything.

"Because the tree likes it," said Hazel. "And because you will want it to know your voice. One day you will be old, and it will still be here, and it will remember you."

The children planted, and watered, and sang. And the orchard hummed for them — a different hum than the one it gave Hazel, lighter, higher, almost like laughter. Hazel heard it and stood still a long moment. The trees were not just tolerating the children. They were glad.

Pip came and stood beside her. "It's a good place," he said, with the air of a general approving a battlefield.

"It is," said Hazel.

"I'm going to be a gardener when I grow up."

"You're going to be something," said Hazel, and ruffled his hair, and the orchard hummed its agreement.

That night she told Yarrow about the children's row of seeds, and Yarrow smiled — a smile like the sun coming out on a grey day. "The gift is passing," she said. "I thought it might. It moves through hands, like a song. You cannot hold it. You can only sing it on."

Chapter 36: The Festival

The village had festivals before — for the first snow, for the hay, for the turning of the cider. But it had not had a harvest festival since the wall went up, because the orchard's harvest had been cursed for a hundred years, and a village does not celebrate a curse.

Now the orchard had borne, and the orchard was open, and Mrs. Quince said, "We will have a festival, or I will know the reason why," and no one could give her a reason.

So the lanterns went up in the trees — paper lanterns, gold and red, hung from the low branches by the children, who were the only ones tall enough to be trusted with ladders and too short to reach the highest. The light fell on the rows like a second harvest. The cider came out of the cellars, the good cider, the proof-cider that Mrs. Quince had made from the first unrotting apples, and it was poured in cups, and it was good.

There was music. Old Father Cinder played his fiddle, and the tune he played was the orchard's hum, or as close as a fiddle can come, and the children danced until their shoes wore thin. Yarrow came to the edge of the light and stood there, and no one quite knew what to do — the witch, in their festival, at their lanterns — and then Bramble walked across the grass and raised his cup to her.

"To the witch-gardener," he said, and his voice cracked, and he said it again. "To Yarrow. To the woman who kept them warm for us when we could not keep them ourselves."

The village raised its cups. Yarrow, ancient and tired and wrapped in her shawl, raised her own, and the lantern light caught her face, and she was not a witch then, only an old woman at the edge of a party, smiling.

Hazel watched the story change. She had heard the curse story told at a hundred midwinter meetings, always the same: the witch came, was refused, cursed, stole. And now here was the same village telling a different story, the new story, the true one — the gardener, the blight, the hundred-year lullaby. The words were still settling, still awkward on the tongues that had held the old ones so long, but they were being spoken, and every speaking made them more true.

"Memory is a field," said Yarrow, when Hazel came to stand by her. "You can grow any story in it you choose. But you must plant it, and water it, and dance on it, or the old weeds come back."

She was tired, and Hazel walked her home through the lantern-lit rows. Behind them the music played on. The festival was the first of a hundred, though no one knew it yet. It was the night the village began to remember its orchard as a gift instead of a theft.

Chapter 37: The Apple Wine

One cold night, when the last of the cider was gone, Mrs. Quince brought out the wine. She had made it from the first harvest's smallest apples, the ones too small to eat, and she had kept it in a dark cask since the harvest, and it had turned the color of late afternoon.

She poured it at the meeting house on the night of the first hard frost. There was not much — a cup each, then a second for the elders. And something strange happened, the way strange things happen quietly, at the edge of a room, when no one is looking.

The drinkers began to dream.

It was not the ordinary dreaming. It was the orchard, but the orchard as the spell had made it — golden light, warm air, leaves motionless, the deep summer held inside the winter. They dreamed they walked the rows. They dreamed they were children, or the trees themselves, or the snow falling softly on the wall. And in the dreams there was a feeling that none of them could name on waking, a feeling of being held, of belonging, of being kept warm by something that asked for nothing.

Old Father Cinder said, "I dreamed the orchard was my mother's kitchen."

Widow Thistle said, "I dreamed I was a tree, and the frost was only a blanket."

Even Bramble, who had not dreamed kindly in years, went quiet and said, "I dreamed the gate was open, and I was not afraid to walk through it."

Mrs. Quince listened, and her face did the thing it did when a batch came out right. "The sleep gave us dreams," she said. "A hundred years of them. We thought the orchard was stealing from us. It was giving us something to dream about — a place where we were safe, and warm, and not afraid."

The village sat with this. It was a hard thing to hold: the curse had not only been a saving, it had been a comfort. Every child who had ever woken frightened had dreamed the golden orchard. Every cold night had carried that light behind its eyelids. The sleep had wrapped them, too.

"There is one thing we owe her," said Mrs. Quince. And she carried the first bottle — the first bottle of the wine — down to the cottage, and set it on Yarrow's table, and said, "This is for you. For later. For the grave, when it comes."

Yarrow looked at the bottle for a long time. Then she looked at Mrs. Quince, and her eyes were bright. "I never thought to taste the waking," she said. "They told me the waking was all fear and forgetting. You have shown me it is wine, and dreaming, and a hundred years of being forgiven."

They shared one cup, the two of them, in the warm cottage, while the orchard hummed outside in the cold. And the wine tasted of late afternoon, and of summer, and of everything the sleep had kept.

Chapter 38: The Blight Fought

The cuttings went out from the Ready Tree like messages from a long siege. Hazel cut them in the first thaw — small branches with living wood, one bud each, bound in wet cloth and packed in pots of orchard earth. There were enough for every orchard in the valley, and Yarrow said, "There were enough a hundred years ago. But you cannot give what no one will take."

The valley's orchardmen came with their carts, suspicious and hopeful at once. Their trees had been dying for a century — the grey rust creeping up the bark, the fruit rotting before it ripened — and they had cut, and burned, and prayed, and nothing had helped. They came to Greengate to see the fabled orchard, and they left with cuttings, and with a story.

Hazel went with them, orchard to orchard, the pot of earth in her arms. She showed them how to bind the graft, how to tie the knot, how to water with winter rain and sing a little and not care if they were laughed at. Some of them laughed. Most of them planted. And the ones who planted watched the grey rust die back.

It was not a quick thing. The rust fought, and the old dying trees fought, and there were days when Hazel thought the hundred years had been for nothing. But the graft was patient wood — a century of patience bred into its grain — and patience always wins the long war. Wherever a Ready Tree cutting was bound, the grey fur on the bark stopped spreading, then dried, then fell away, and underneath was clean living bark, the color of a healed scar.

By the time the leaves fell, every orchard in the valley had a graft of the Ready Tree, and every graft was holding. The blight was not gone — it would never be gone, Yarrow said, only held, the way the sleep had held it — but it was held, and it was dying, and the trees were living again.

Yarrow's patience, proven in a hundred orchards. The hundred years redeemed in one season, one graft at a time, one orchardman who stopped burning his trees and started binding them.

The orchardmen came back, some of them, to thank her. Yarrow stood at the cottage door and received them with the same calm with which she had endured the accusations, and she said to each of them the same thing: "I did not save the trees. I only kept the answer until you were ready to hear it."

And Hazel, standing among them, understood that the blight had been fought in the way all great fights are won — not by a battle, but by a hundred years of someone refusing to give up while everyone else slept.

Chapter 39: The Graft Spread

In the spring the grafts took everywhere. Hazel traveled the valley with the pot of orchard earth and a basket of cuttings, and she went further than the valley, over the grey hills to villages that had only ever heard of Greengate as the place with the cursed orchard.

She carried two things: the graft, and the story. "The witch did not curse the orchard," she told them. "She slept it, to keep it from dying. The sleep held the blight at bay while she bred the answer. The answer is in my hands. Will you plant it?"

Some believed her because the graft was in her hands, green and alive. Some believed her because she hummed, and the hum was hard to disbelieve. Some believed her because their own orchards were dying, and hope is a seed that will grow in any ground.

The story traveled faster than Hazel did. It went ahead of her, carried by the orchardmen who had come to Greengate with carts and left with cuttings. It changed in the telling, as stories do, but it changed toward truth: the witch-gardener, the hundred-year lullaby, the girl who walked through the unopened gate. By midsummer, children in villages Hazel had never visited were singing the song of the gardener, and she was a small legend, a girl with earth-stained hands who carried a tree in a pot.

Greengate felt it — the shame and the pride mixed together. Shame, that the village had feared for a hundred years what the valley now honored. Pride, that the answer had come from their orchard, their Hazel. Bramble said, at a midwinter meeting that no longer told the curse story, "We were the walled village. Now we are the village that kept the answer. Let us be that, and be glad."

Hazel came home in the autumn, tired and sunburned, and found the pot of orchard earth waiting for her by the cottage door, refilled, ready. Yarrow was asleep in her chair, and the orchard hummed its greeting, and Hazel set the pot down and put her hand on the Old Queen's trunk.

"The story is out," she said. "It will travel as far as the fear did. Further."

The Old Queen did not answer. But the hum rose, deep and satisfied, and Hazel knew that the truth had outgrown the wall at last. The wall still stood, but it was only stone now. The story had gone over it like weather, and nothing built of fear can stop the weather.

Chapter 40: The Spring

The spring came, and it was the orchard's first real spring in a hundred years. The blossom was so thick the air was white with it — not snow, this time, but petals, falling and rising and turning the light soft as linen. Hazel walked the rows and breathed it in and could not stop smiling, because the trees were doing what trees did: blooming. In time. On time. Alive.

The frozen birds had woken, and their descendants sang in the branches, and their songs wove with the hum into a music the orchard had not made for a century. The children's crooked row of young trees had grown green and quick, each with a name, each with a child who sang to it. The whole valley came, on the best days, to walk the white air and stand under the Old Queen, who was draped in blossom like a queen in her wedding clothes.

But Yarrow could not walk the rows. She had grown too weak — the hundred years had caught up with her, and the spring that woke the orchard had taken the last of her strength. She sat in her chair by the cottage door, wrapped in her shawl, watching the blossom fall, and she was not sad.

"Bring me out," she said. "Into it. I have waited a hundred years to see this."

So Hazel carried her. She lifted the old woman in her arms — she was light, light as a dry branch, light as a thing that had been mostly air for a century — and carried her through the rows, through the falling white, past the Whisper Tree and the Little Twig and the children's new trees, to the Old Queen, where she set her down in the grass and sat beside her.

Yarrow looked up through the blossom at the sky. The petals landed on her shawl, on her hands, in her white hair. She was silent a long time. Then she laughed — a real laugh, the kind that comes up from the roots — and the petals shook on the branches, and the Old Queen's hum rose, and the frozen birds' descendants sang on.

"I forgot," said Yarrow, "what it looks like from underneath. All those years inside the spell, I watched the trees bloom in my mind. I never thought I would see it with my eyes again."

"Do you want to go back to the cottage?" Hazel asked.

"Not yet," said Yarrow. "Let me stay until the petals are done. Let me be buried in blossom, like a queen."

So they stayed, the girl and the old woman, under the white trees, while the spring went on around them — the orchard blooming, the birds singing, the children laughing at the gate — and Yarrow, who had held a hundred years of spring inside her, let it fall at last, and laughed, and was glad.

Chapter 41: The Blossom

The Old Queen stood at the far end of the rows, the oldest tree in the orchard, its blossom so thick that the air around it was white. Hazel carried Yarrow to it one morning in the early summer, the old woman light as a child in her arms, her apron smelling of earth and apples and the last of the winter honey.

"Here," Yarrow said. "Set me where I can see her."

Hazel settled her on the grass at the Old Queen's foot, propped against the great ridged trunk, and sat beside her in the warm light. The orchard hummed around them, low and full, the way it hummed when it was glad.

Yarrow talked that morning. She talked more than she had in all the months since Hazel came through the gate, as if the hundred years of keeping had finally loosened its hold on her tongue.

She told Hazel of her life before the orchard — a girl who heard music in growing things, who made the village's roses bloom a week early and its seedlings rise in a single night. Small magics. Harmless ones. But the village had feared them, the way villages fear what they cannot explain, and so she had come to the empty orchard at Greengate's edge, where the trees were dying, and had loved them, and had stayed.

"I did not choose to be hated," she said. "I chose that they should live. The hatred was the price, and I paid it because there was no one else to pay it. A tree cannot be asked to wait alone." She looked up into the white blossom. "They could not know it was kindness. They could only know the wall."

Hazel took her hand, the scarred gardener's hand. "They know now."

"Yes," Yarrow said. "You taught them."

A wind moved through the orchard, the first wind Hazel had felt here that did not belong to a spell, and the Old Queen's blossom came down around them, a slow white snow falling in the green light.

Hazel made her promise then, kneeling in the petals.

"I will keep the names," she said. "The Old Queen, and the Whisper Tree, and the Little Twig, and the Ready Tree, and every one. I will water them and sing to them and bind them when they need binding. And when you are done resting, I will be here."

Yarrow's eyes closed. A single petal let go of the Old Queen's highest branch and fell, turning slowly, and settled on Yarrow's open hand, light as a blessing, the way a petal had once settled on Hazel's own hand at the Ready Tree.

Yarrow did not open her eyes, but her fingers closed over the petal, holding it.

"Good," she said. "The names are yours now."

And the orchard hummed, so low and so full that Hazel felt it in the roots of her hair, a note held under all the others, the note that meant: we are listening, and we are glad, and you are one of us now.

Chapter 42: The Harvest

The harvest came in autumn, and it was ordinary, and that was the wonder of it.

The year's rhythm had established itself. Hazel woke before dawn and went to the orchard while the mist lay in the rows, checking the grafts she had bound in the spring, humming to the trees the way Yarrow had taught her. She was still a girl at noon, at lessons and sledding with Pip, and she was a gardener at dusk. The village had grown used to it, the way it had grown used to the open gate.

And in October the apples came in — not the impossible one-night fruit of the waking, but the patient fruit of a real year: red and gold and heavy, ripened slowly, tasting of sun and rain and the long ordinary work of growing.

The whole village was in the trees. Ladders against every trunk, baskets everywhere, the children on the lower branches shaking the last apples down in a red rain. Pip had a whistle now and used it like a general, and even Bramble, who had not climbed a tree in fifty years, stood on a low branch with his coat off and dropped apples into a basket and laughed at his own clumsiness.

"Not cursed," he said, catching Hazel's eye. "Just apples." He looked at the fruit in his hands, and his voice was quieter. "Yarrow would have been proud of this."

Hazel set aside her share for the stores, the way Yarrow had shown her — the sound ones in the loft, the windfalls for the press. Mrs. Quince had her casks going, and the smell of cider hung over the village like a second harvest.

That evening Hazel walked the rows alone. The trees stood stripped and satisfied, their leaves going gold, and the orchard hummed a low contented song, the song of a thing that had done its work.

She understood, standing there in the last light, what the waking had given them. Not just the one-night bloom, the one-night gift of forgiveness. This — a season that came when it was meant to, a harvest she had worked for and waited for, fruit that had ripened in its own time. The miraculous had become the daily. And the daily, Hazel thought, was the deepest miracle of all.

The orchard was alive in time again. It would sleep, and wake, and sleep again, and she would be there for all of it.

She hummed, and the orchard hummed back, and the two notes went up together into the gold and the quiet.

Chapter 43: The Witch's Grave

Yarrow died under the Ready Tree, in the deep of the same autumn, as she had asked.

Hazel found her there on a morning of low gold light, sitting against the trunk of the tree she had bred through a hundred years, her hands folded on her apron, a small smile on her face, as if she had simply fallen asleep in the middle of being glad. The Ready Tree had dropped its leaves around her, a ring of gold at her feet, and the orchard's hum had gone very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when something precious is being carried out.

Hazel knelt and took her hand. It was still warm.

"You kept them for me," she said. "You kept them warm. Thank you. I will keep them now."

The village came in a long quiet line, and they did not need to be told. They knew. They had known the old woman was resting toward this, and they had learned, in the year of the open gate, to carry their grief as they carried their harvest — together.

They buried her under the Ready Tree, where the roots could keep her the way she had kept them. Hazel laid the gardener's knife in the grave with her, the knife thin as a feather, and the roll of twine, and the dark leather orchard-book with its hundred years of entries. Mrs. Quince set the first bottle of her apple wine beside her, the one she had saved from the first harvest, for the grave she had always known she would be making.

And Hazel took a cutting of the Ready Tree — a small branch with roots, bound in a pot of the orchard's own earth — and planted it at the head of the grave.

"A graft," she said to the village, "is what you bind when someone must live on. She bound us. Now she will."

The dirt went in by handfuls, everyone taking a turn, the way the whole village had taken its turn learning the truth. Bramble spoke last, standing in his good coat at the foot of the grave, and his voice did not hold steady.

"We called her curse," he said, "and she gave us a century of care. We feared her, and she kept our trees safe, and our children, and our future. We have no word to thank her that will not sound small." He looked down at the earth. "But we have a tree, and we have her name, and we will keep both."

The mourning belonged to the whole village. It was not a dark grief; it was a grief with bloom in it, like the leaves falling in the orchard's gold. Hazel stood at the grave until the light went, and then she stood a while longer, and the hum of the orchard came up through her feet, soft and patient, the way it had always come up through her palms.

Yarrow was not there. But the trees were there, and the trees were her children, and Hazel was their gardener now, and the orchard went on.

Chapter 44: The Humming Kept

The winter came down on the orchard, and Hazel was alone with it, and the humming continued.

She had thought, in the first days after the burial, that the hum might go quiet — that it had been Yarrow's song, and without her it might falter. But it did not. She woke to it, she carried it through her rounds, and when she stood at the grave-tree in the snow and put her hand to the small bound wood, the hum came up through her palm as steady as ever, the way water finds its level.

"I hear you," she said. "I'm here."

She watered the grave-tree with winter rain, the way Yarrow had taught her, catching it in a jar on the eaves. She sang to it, the songs Yarrow had sung, and the small tree stood green against the snow as if it had been promised this.

The village had learned to let her be. The orchard was hers now; they came for the open gate and the daily visits, but they knew that a gardener's morning was a private thing, and they left her to it. Only Maren came, some days, and stood at the edge of the rows and watched her daughter move among the trees, and did not call her in.

And one evening, when the light was going blue and Hazel stood at the grave-tree humming the low note she had hummed all her life, she heard a second voice.

Her mother, off-key, laughing at herself, humming along.

Hazel turned. Maren stood in the snow at the end of the row, wrapped in her winter shawl, and she was humming — badly, cheerfully, without shame — the same note.

"I don't know why I'm doing this," Maren said. "It just comes out of me when I stand near the trees now. Is that all right?"

Hazel laughed. It was the first real laugh she had given since the burial, and it went into the evening and the snow, and the orchard took it in and held it.

"That's exactly right," she said. "That's how it starts."

They stood together in the cold, mother and daughter, humming, one in tune and one in a happy wilderness of tune, and the orchard hummed between them, and Hazel understood that the song was not hers to keep locked away.

It was a gift, like the graft. You carry it, and then you pass it on, and the carrying is what keeps it alive.

She went home that night with her mother's arm in hers, and the hum went with her, patient as weather, and she knew she would hum it all her life.

Chapter 45: The Winter Again

A second midwinter came, and the trees stood bare and sleeping, and there was no fear in it.

That was the wonder. Hazel stood at the gate that no longer had to be opened, for it stood open always now, and looked at the orchard under its first true winter: the branches black and clean against the snow, the grass gone to brown, the whole place resting the way a field rests, full of what it had done and ready for what came next.

A year ago she had stood at this same gate with a rusted key and a stone in her heart, and the village had gathered at the wall in terror, watching apples ripen in the frost and calling it an ending. Now the same frost was only frost. The children hauled sleds past the open gate and up the long hill, and their laughter came down through the bare trees, and no one crossed themselves, and no one spoke of curses.

The village knew the difference now. Hazel heard it in the way they said the word winter — a season, not a sentence. They had learned that the trees could sleep without being trapped, that a held breath could be let out, that the orchard could be still and still be theirs.

She remembered last winter's terror and could not quite fit herself back into it. It had been so large, that fear. It had filled the square and the meeting house and every doorway; it had armed the village with pitchforks and lanterns and a key that fit nothing. And now it was gone, and the world had forgotten it so completely that it was hard to believe it had ever been there at all.

How quickly the world forgot to be afraid, she thought. How quickly the true story wore the old one smooth.

Pip came skidding through the gate on his sled, cheeks red, shouting for her to come and pull him up the hill. Hazel laughed and went.

She looked back once at the orchard — the bare branches, the grave-tree standing green at its head, the small cottage at the heart of the rows with warm light in its window where she had lit the fire that morning. It was hers now, all of it. Not because she had been chosen, though she had been, but because she had stayed, and learned, and loved it into a second year.

The ordinary winter was enough.

She turned and ran after Pip, and the orchard slept on behind her, easy and unhurried, dreaming its ordinary dreams.

Chapter 46: The Orchard Sleeps Softly

Snow fell all one night, thick and quiet, and when Hazel could not sleep she pulled on her boots and her mother's shawl and walked out to the orchard.

It was a different orchard than the one she had first stepped into. No golden light, no warm air, no leaves stopped mid-fall. The trees stood in the dark and the snow, bare and still, and the cold was a real cold, the cold of an honest winter, and Hazel loved it for that.

She walked the rows, her breath steaming, the snow coming down in the lantern light. The grave-tree stood at the Ready Tree's foot, its green crown dark against the white, and she touched its bark and felt the cold, patient cold, the cold of a thing keeping.

She had known the other sleep — the spell-sleep, the hundred-year holding, the breath that would not be let out. This was a different thing. This was the season-sleep, the ordinary night of the year, the sleep a tree has always slept and will always sleep. Not a cocoon. A bed.

She came to the Old Queen and laid her hand flat against its trunk. It was cold, but it was not the deep unnatural cold of the spell — it was the cold of January, the cold of the ground and the sky, a cold the tree wore like a coat and did not fear.

And that was when she heard it.

The orchard was humming in its sleep.

Not the waking song, not the answer to her voice. A softer thing, low and slow, a lullaby, the sound a mother makes without knowing she is making it. It came from every tree at once, from the bare branches and the sleeping roots and the snow, and Hazel stood still in the middle of it and understood that she had never heard this before.

The spell-sleep had been silence. Yarrow's sleep had held the orchard so still that it could not even dream aloud. But this — this was the orchard dreaming ordinary dreams, and the dreams had a music, and the music was a lullaby, and the lullaby was the most peaceful sound Hazel had ever heard.

She did not hum back. For once she only listened, standing in the falling snow, letting the song hold her the way a blanket holds a child.

When she was warm enough to walk, she answered it at last — one low note, soft as the snow, the note she had hummed all her life. The lullaby opened a little, took it in, made a place for it.

Then she went home, past the sleeping trees, and the orchard slept on behind her, easy in its first true winter.

In her own bed, with the snow coming down beyond the window, Hazel heard it still, faint and far and kind, and she slept the sleep of a thing that is loved.

Chapter 47: The Old Fear

The peddler came at the turn of the year, when the roads were passable and the village was restless with midwinter.

He set his cart at the edge of the square and told stories for his supper, the way peddlers do, and the children gathered around him in the snow, and Hazel did not notice anything wrong until she heard the word.

Witch.

She was crossing the square with a basket of winter apples for Mrs. Quince, and she stopped. The peddler had the children in a circle, and he was telling them the old story — the witch who cursed the orchard, a hundred years of stolen fruit, the grey wall built against her malice. He told it well, the way fear is always told well, and some of the smaller children had gone wide-eyed, glancing at the open gate behind him.

Hazel set down her basket.

"Friend," she said, and her voice carried, and the children turned. "You have the story a little wrong. Let me show you."

The peddler looked at her, surprised. But he was a good-natured man, and the village had come out at the sound of her voice, and there was no way to refuse a girl who spoke like that and carried it off.

She took him through the open gate into the orchard. She showed him the rows and the cottage and the shed where the blight was kept, the grey fur still sleeping under glass. She showed him the grave-tree at the Ready Tree's foot, green in the snow, and the smooth stone at its head.

"Her name was Yarrow," Hazel said. "She was a gardener. The blight came a hundred years ago and would have killed every tree in the valley. She put them to sleep to save them, and she bred a wood the rust cannot touch. She took the fruit to keep it safe." She picked an apple from the winter store and held it out. "Here. Taste the curse."

The peddler bit into the apple. He chewed. He looked at the orchard, and at the grave, and at the children who had followed them through the gate and were now climbing the low branches of the Little Twig, laughing.

"Well," he said at last, around the apple. "I have told that story in a hundred villages, and it was never once true."

He told the true one after that. He told it that night in the meeting house, and the village listened with the pleasure of hearing their own story spoken by a stranger's mouth, and he told it on the road out of Greengate, and Hazel watched him go, carrying the true tale where the false one had ridden.

The old fear died from being seen. It had only ever lived in the not-looking, and now the whole valley had looked, and the whole valley had seen a gardener and her grave, and the fear had nowhere left to stand.

Hazel went back through the open gate and put her hand on the grave-tree, and the orchard hummed, soft and low, the sound that meant: it was only a story, child, and stories are doors. Yours is open now.

Chapter 48: The New Story

The new story took root in the village like a graft, and it grew the way a graft grows — slowly at first, then all at once.

It began with the singer. Old Fern, who had kept the village's songs for forty years, came to Hazel in the spring and asked her to tell him the whole tale — not the telling she gave at meetings, but the private one, the one with the humming in it. Hazel told him, and he listened without speaking, and when she finished he said, "It is a song. It has been a song all along."

He wrote it that summer. "The Gardener's Hundred Years," he called it, and the village learned it, and it spread faster than the fear had ever spread — because a song is lighter than a fear, and it travels on the same roads without the same weight.

It was not a long song. It told of a girl who heard music in growing things, and a village that feared her, and a blight, and a choice: to be hated, or to let the trees die. It told of the sleep, and the hundred years, and the graft bred in the dark, and the midwinter apple that fell over the wall to a girl who hummed. It told of waking, and of bloom, and of the open gate.

The children acted it out at the festival. Pip was the witch — he insisted — and marched through the square with a paper knife and a crown of twigs, and the children booed him mightily, and then the Little Twig's branch was handed to a small girl with a serious face, and she carried it to the meeting-house steps and the children cheered.

And in the middle of it, Hazel heard her own name.

She had never heard her name in a song before. It was a strange feeling, like looking in a mirror and finding a tree looking back. The song called her the Listener, the one who hummed, the girl who carried the branch out of the snow — and it was true, all of it, and the children sang it without knowing how true.

She stood at the back of the square and let it wash over her. Yarrow had been hated in a song — the song the village had sung about a curse. And now a different song was being sung, in the same square, by the same voices, and it told the truth.

Hazel put her hand on the grave-tree that night, and the first green shoots of the spring were on it.

"Did you hear?" she asked. "They sing your name now. The way they used to sing the fear."

The orchard hummed, low and glad, and Hazel understood that the truth had begun to travel as far as the fear had traveled.

A story is a door. And this one, at last, was open.

Chapter 49: The Wall Removed

In the spring, a year after the waking, the village took down the wall.

It was Hazel's idea, or Bramble's — they never quite agreed, and in the end it was everyone's. The wall had been the shape of the fear, and the fear was gone, and a wall that guards nothing is only a pile of stones in the way of the light.

They did it in the spring, when the orchard was in blossom and the work of the year was still ahead. They did not tear it down in anger. They took it apart in use, the way you take apart an old barn for its timbers, stone by patient stone, the whole village at it: the children hauling the small stones in their sleds, the men at the top handing down the big ones, the women sorting them into piles by what they would become.

And the stones became things.

The best ones lined the village well, so that the water came up clean and cold. Some made a hearth in the meeting house, so that the winter gatherings were warm. Some bordered the gardens, holding back the earth the way the wall had never been able to hold back anything. A long flat one became a bench at the foot of the Old Queen, where the old people sat in the long light and listened to the orchard hum.

The iron gate was the last thing. It had never locked anything, and it had stood for a hundred years as the symbol of a door that was never closed. The village took it down and set it at the head of a row of apple trees, and roses and wild grape grew up through the bars, and it became an arbor, a door you walk through because you want to, on the way into the orchard.

The last stone of the wall was laid as a doorstep.

It was the great stone from the bottom of the wall, the one the gate had hung from, and Bramble and Hazel carried it together, one at each end, and set it at the threshold of the meeting house. Bramble was breathing hard, and his hands were shaking, and he was not crying — or he was, a little.

"A hundred years," he said, standing on the stone, "and this is what the wall was for. A doorstep. A way in."

Hazel looked down the hill at the orchard, open now to every road, its blossom going white in the morning light. The wall was gone. The stones were hearth and bench and well and doorstep, and the orchard was open to every road, and there was nothing between the village and its heart but the walking.

She put her hand on Bramble's arm.

"We built it out of fear," she said. "And now it's a way in. I think that's what the gardener wanted all along."

The orchard hummed, loud and glad, and the sound of it carried over the village like the first note of a song everyone knew.

Chapter 50: The Open Orchard

The orchard opens in the morning to the light, and it stays open all day, and it is the village's pride.

Hazel walks its rows at dawn, before the mist lifts, the way she has walked them every morning for a year. She carries the pot of the Ready Tree's earth — she keeps it still, refilled each spring, because a gardener remembers where she came from — and she has the knife thin as a feather in her apron, and the roll of twine, and in her pocket the one perfect seed she has carried since the first apple fell to her, wrapped in its cloth.

She knows the names. The Old Queen, oldest and clearest. The Whisper Tree, that sings the softest in the wind. The Little Twig, where the children climb. The Ready Tree, by the well, that ripened first. And the grave-tree at its foot, green and growing, that keeps Yarrow's name for anyone who will hear it.

In the spring the grave-tree flowers, white as the blossom of the orchard it grew from, and the village brings the first cider of the year and sets a cup at its root, the way it has learned to.

The seasons turn. The orchard lives in time now — blossom, and green, and harvest, and the honest sleep of winter — and the village lives with it, and the fear is a story told to children about the old days, and the true story is a song everyone can sing.

Hazel is the gardener. She is also still a girl. She races Pip down the rows and loses on purpose, and she teaches the small ones the trees' names, and she hums, always hums, the way her great-great-grandmother hummed, the way Yarrow hummed, the way the orchard hums back.

And on an evening in the deep summer, when the light is gold and the apples are beginning to color, she sees a child at the orchard's edge.

A small child, a girl, stopped at the last row where the grass meets the road. She is not climbing, not picking, not running. She is standing very still, with her head tilted, listening.

Hazel goes to the edge of the rows and watches her. The child does not move. Her face is serious and wondering, and she is listening the way Hazel had listened, all those years, at a window in the snow.

Hazel hears it too. The hum. The low song like distant bees and strings. It is coming up out of the orchard, and it is louder than it has ever been, and it is singing to the child.

She does not call out. She does not want to break it. She only stands at the edge of her rows, and the child stands at the edge of the orchard, and the song goes between them, a bridge of sound a hundred years wide.

The child smiles. It is not the smile of someone who has understood; it is the smile of someone who has been greeted.

Hazel smiles too.

The song will be kept.

The orchard hums.
