The Copper Canyon
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Strike
One spring past, an old man walked out of Cañon del Cobre carrying a stone the size of his fist, and the canyon was never the same again.
Hugh Gantry had come through a winter on bad beans and less hope, prospecting ground the sheep people had grazed for four generations. He was the kind of man the canyon had always shed — white-faced, loud, a shovel where a shepherd would carry a crook. The canyon's own people knew the green in the walls the way they knew the weather. It was the green eye, the malachite outcrop in the upper wall where the rock went soft and green as new leaves, and below it a seep that ran bitter, and the sheep would not drink there. No one had ever thought to dig. Sheep country is not ore country. That was the canyon's secret, and the canyon kept it.
Gantry's stone went south to the assay office, and the word came back faster than any rider could have carried it: copper, high grade, more than a man could spend in ten lives. The word reached Arroyo Seco first, thirty miles out, where the railhead drank the business of everything around. From there it spread like a rumor of water in a dry year — men sold what they owned and bought what they needed and pointed their horses at the red canyon on the horizon.
The first strangers arrived on the sheep trail. Rosa Aguilar, twenty-eight that spring, stood on the ridge above the canyon mouth and watched them come — four men on horses, then a wagon, then a string of mules carrying packs, all of them following the old path worn by hooves and generations, the path she had walked since she could walk. They looked up at the walls, at the green fire in the rock. They did not see her.
Her father Benito stood with her in the wind, one eye weak, watching with a patience that told her nothing. That evening, by the fire in the adobe, he said only this, and it stayed with her for the rest of her life: "The sheep never drank at the green seep. We should have asked why."
The canyon had held sheep and silence and the spring that looked up at the sky. Then a man carried a green stone out of it in his hand, and everything that came after — the tents, the shanties, the hundred men, the fever — was already in the balance of that one walk out of the narrows. The canyon told the truth, and the canyon would remember. Rosa watched the world arrive, and she did not yet know it had come to live in her canyon, or that she would spend her life keeping the peace between the canyon and it.

Chapter 2: Cañon del Cobre
The canyon was a red room in the earth, and Rosa knew every wall of it by heart.
Cañon del Cobre ran northwest out of the mountain, its walls rising in layered reds — rust, blood, brick — with the green of ore seaming through where the rock had gone mineral. A river had cut it once, a river of the old world, and left a floor of sand and gravel and the patience of water. The water was still there, hidden: el ojo del agua, the spring at the canyon head, the oldest landmark, where the water's eye looked up at the sky from a stone basin older than any name. Herders had watered flocks there for generations, and their herds before them, and the canyon remembered each of them in the depth of the hoof-cut trails.
The anatomy of the place had names in Spanish, given by the sheep people, kept by the sheep people. The narrows, where the walls closed to a knife's width and the wind came through with a sound like a voice — the old ones said the canyon sang there, and the young ones learned not to argue. La cueva, the shallow cave above the narrows, where herders had sheltered from weather and wolves since the first man drove sheep up the gorge. The sheep trails, worn deep as ditches by hooves and generations, cutting across the canyon floor like the lines of a hand. The green eye, the malachite outcrop, the canyon's copper, known forever and never dug.
The seasons had their own map. Winter snow on the red walls, the trails frozen and slick. Spring — the canyon's true year — when the water ran full in the spring and the lambs came and the creosote bloomed after rain, the smell of it lying low and green. Summer pasture high, the flocks moving up with the sun. Autumn, the herds coming down past the narrows, bells ringing, dust gold in the evening air. The canyon turned through its year the way it had turned through ten thousand years, and the herders moved with it, and were the only thing in it that could be said to hurry.
Rosa had learned the canyon before she learned letters — dust before books, as she would later say of herself. She could read a season in the color of the walls, a storm in the way the narrows went quiet, a stranger's passage in the dust of the wagon road. The canyon was the room she had grown up in, and she had grown up in every part of it.
Then the green eye was bought, and the canyon's long silence was sold with it. The wagons came, and the sound of iron on rock, and the canyon began to change whether it would or no. Rosa rode the sheep trail the morning the first claim was staked and looked up at the green eye in the wall and thought, without knowing she was thinking it: the canyon was whole before the fever. And she was the only one left who remembered.

Chapter 3: The Sheep Trails
Rosa's first memories were of hooves and dust and the bell of the lead ewe.
She was born to the herding year. Lambing came with the spring thaw, and her mother Serafina would be out in the lambing pens before light, warm hands in the cold dawn, and Rosa at her apron hem learning the names of the flock. Summer meant the high pastures, the flock moving slow as weather up the green benches, Benito ahead with his staff, Mateo — six years younger, always at Rosa's heels — trailing behind with the stragglers. The trails were their roads, the canyon their country, and the sheep were a river they followed through the year.
Her mother taught her the things the sheep people know and never write down. How to read the sky. How the narrows sounded before rain. How a ewe would turn her head to warn of trouble before any bell could ring. Serafina was the canyon's memory in those years — she knew the name of every spring, the ruin of every old homestead, the story of why the herders no longer camped at the foot of the green eye, where the water ran bitter and the sheep would not drink. "The water remembers," her mother said once, and Rosa asked what it remembered, and her mother only smiled.
Rosa was twelve in the year the water rose. It was a spring of strange weather — rain in the high country, the narrows running brown and loud — and her mother had gone up the gorge after a ewe that had wandered, the way she had gone a hundred times. Rosa was at the sheep camp when the flood came through, and she was old enough to understand, and young enough that understanding did no good. The water took Serafina in the narrows, where the walls closed to a knife's width and there was no high ground to climb. They searched for days, the herders and the men from the settlements, but the canyon keeps what it takes. Her mother's body was never found.
Her father's grief had no words. Benito went on with the flock because the flock would not keep itself, and he raised two children on the trails, and he never once blamed the water and never once mentioned Serafina easily. Grief lived in him like a stone in a streambed — it changed the current around it, but the water kept moving.
The trails were Rosa's inheritance and her education. She learned dust before she learned letters — to read a hoofprint, a rut, a man's passage, a lie. The canyon taught her what the schoolhouse could not: that the land remembers everything, and that the truth is written in what the land keeps. Every spring, at the place in the narrows where the water had taken her mother, Rosa left flowers. She did not know, that girl on the trails, that the canyon was training her to be its law. But the canyon knew.

Chapter 4: The New Town
A year passed, and the canyon mouth was not the same place Rosa had known.
The strike had been made in the spring. By summer there were tents — a dozen, then fifty, white against the red walls like new lamb's wool. By autumn the shanties came, board and canvas and scrap iron, and the first frame buildings rose like slow trees. A hundred men by winter, the town called Cobre, named for the green thing in the canyon's walls, squatting at the mouth of the gorge where the sheep trail met the wagon road. The canyon's first nation of work had been the sheep people; the second came down the trail in boots and dust, carrying picks and dreams and nothing else.
Rosa watched her home change the way you watch a wound heal wrong. The wagon road was cut through the lower canyon, scraping the sheep trail at the crossing where the old path had run for generations — the herds still used it, and now they shared it with ore wagons, and the two streams of traffic tangled and frayed like the canyon was learning a new way to move. The green eye was bought by the Fortuna Mining Company, eastern capital, and the mine went up against the upper wall — a headframe, a hoist, the sound of machinery where there had only ever been wind. The copper that had been the canyon's secret became the canyon's reason.
The sheep people watched it happen with the canyon's own patience. They did not leave. The flock still went up in spring and came down in autumn; Benito's sheep still watered at el ojo del agua, though now they shared the spring with men who did not know its name and never asked. The herders were used to being the only people in the canyon; they were learning to be a minority in their own home. Some sold their wool to the miners' store. Some sold their wagons to the mine. Some just watched, and waited, which was the canyon's way.
Rosa was not a girl on the ridge anymore. She was a woman who read dust and trails, who had buried her mother in the canyon's keeping, who spoke English and Spanish with the same tongue and moved between the two towns — the English town of boots and wages, the Spanish camp of hides and sheep — like a woman walking a single room. The two towns did not trust each other. They trusted her.
The wagon road had cut the sheep trail. The green eye had become a mine. And in the spring of that second year, when the town's one season had passed and the fever had not broken, the county sent a lawman to Cobre — and that lawman, tired and drunk and honest about both, looked at the canyon and asked for a deputy who knew it. Rosa did not yet know his name. She was about to.

Chapter 5: The Three Saloons
Night in Cobre had three hearts, and Rosa knew the pulse of each.
The Nugget sat at the head of the street, the loudest building in the territory — false front, painted glass, a piano that never stopped. It was the miners' palace, the house of wages and whiskey, where a man with a payday in his pocket could spend it faster than the canyon could take it. Jake Bodine was there most nights, a big man gone to fat and debt, laughing too loud and owing too much. Rosa stepped through the batwing doors into the heat and the smoke and the smell of men and dust, and the noise did not drop for her — it never did, and she never needed it to. She walked the room, read the tempers the way she read ruts, and the Nugget knew she was there and settled a half-degree for her.
El Refugio was the third building down, and it was her home ground. Doña Elvira's cantina, herder ground, Spanish voices, the smell of chiles and wool and the good wood of the bar. Here the sheep people drank and talked and remembered, and here Rosa was not the law in a badge but a woman of the canyon's own blood — Doña Elvira's was the one roof in town where nobody had to explain themselves. She stayed longest there, a glass of something warm in her hands, hearing the news the English town never would — which flock was ailing, which herder's daughter had taken a husband, what the company's wagons had been seen carrying on the road to Arroyo Seco. El Refugio was where the canyon spoke, and Rosa listened.
The Last Water was the dive at the edge of town, the last building before the desert, where the desperate drank — the men the mine had broken, the men who had lost the strike at cards, the men with nowhere else to go. The name came from the old trail joke: the last water before the dry country. It had no piano, no false front, just a low roof and a lamp that burned all night. Rosa was watched there in a way she was watched nowhere else. She went in slow, read the room, and went out again, and the men in the Last Water learned that the deputy did not need to draw to be known in a room.
Her night round ran from the Nugget to El Refugio to the Last Water and back, one peace kept in three languages of men. The town was a hundred strangers with nothing in common but the green thing in the walls, and Rosa was the thread that held them — welcome in two hearts, watched in the third, and walking the dark between them all.
But a town with three hearts and one lawman is a town with the balance hanging by a thread. The night she came off her round and found the sheriff's office dark and Grant asleep in his chair with the bottle empty beside him, Rosa understood for the first time that she might be the only one awake in all of Cobre. And she was not wrong.

Chapter 6: The Law
The law came to Cobre in the spring of the second year, and it came tired.
Sheriff Emmett Grant rode in on a big chestnut that had seen better days, a man in his sixties with a lawman's build gone soft and a lawman's eyes gone gentle. The county had sent him because he was the only man who would take the post — a strike town forty miles from anywhere, a hundred strangers with guns, a canyon full of copper and greed. Grant had been a good lawman once, before the booms wore him down, and he knew it. He said so himself, openly, which was the best of him.
The first thing Grant did was ride the canyon, not the town. He rode the old sheep trail to el ojo del agua, through the narrows where the wind sounded like a voice, up to the green eye where the mine was chewing into the wall. When he came back he asked the county for one thing specific: a deputy who knew the canyon.
The men he might have picked were the usual sort — a big man with a star's appetite, a tough with a reputation, somebody from the outside who could look at the town and see only the town. Grant saw what they could not: Cobre was a town laid over a canyon, and a lawman who did not know the canyon would be law over a place he could not see. He asked for the only local in the new law, and he got her.
Rosa Aguilar. The county clerk wrote the name down with a question mark. Hiram Brand, the mine's local agent, objected with the door shut — the company wanted a lawman it could count, and a herder's daughter with a badge was not the count it had in mind. "She's a woman," Brand said, "and she's one of them."
"Both true," Grant said. He had taken the cork out of the bottle, and then — the one thing Brand did not expect — he put it back in. "And the law in a canyon has got to know the canyon. She knows it better than any man alive. I'm the law here, Brand. I'll take her."
The star was pinned to her shirt that afternoon, in the office with the door open onto the street, the way Grant did everything honest — in the light. Rosa stood very still while the town's eyes settled on the badge, a hundred strangers measuring her. She had never asked for this. The star was the first thing she had ever been given that she did not know how to wear.
That night, alone in the office, she unpinned it and looked at it a long time in the lamplight, and pinned it back on — not for the county, not for Grant, not for the company, but for the canyon. The canyon was hers to keep, and the star was how she would keep it. She knew it. And it was about to be tested.

Chapter 7: Rosa
The deputy was a woman of the canyon, and the canyon was in everything about her.
Rosa Aguilar was twenty-nine, and she looked it — not old, but looked-at, the way a thing is looked at by weather. Her skin was canyon-dark, her hair black under the hat, her hands the hands of a herder's daughter, strong and quiet. She rode a bay mare she had raised from a foal, a horse that knew the trail better than most men knew their own names, and she carried her father's old rifle in the scabbard — a Henry, handed down, oiled and true. She was not tall. She did not need to be. The men who measured her by her height learned nothing; the men who measured her by the canyon learned everything.
Her sharpness was the canyon's teaching. She read tracks, weights, ruts, men's habits — the depth of a wheel in the dust, the set of a shoulder, the weight a man put on his feet. She had learned dust before she learned letters, and it showed: she could look at a wagon road and tell you what had passed, when, and why it was lying. She was rooted the way a cottonwood is rooted — home in a way no miner could ever be, the canyon in her bones. The miners owned their claims; she owned the place the claims were staked.
She was brave the way the canyon was brave — quietly, practically, without performance. She had never had to tell anyone she was brave, because courage in her was not a decision but a direction, the way water finds the low ground. She would corner a man and walk him out into the light before she would shoot him down. That was her law, and she had not yet had to use it.
Her daily ride ran from the adobe at the canyon's edge — her father's house, where she still kept a room and a hook for her bridle — down the wagon road to the office in town. She rode it every morning with the canyon under her eye, reading the dust of the night's passage, the new ruts, the strangers' tracks, the mine's smoke against the red wall. The town woke around her and did not see her reading it.
And under everything, like water under the canyon floor, was her mother. Serafina had been the canyon's memory, and Rosa had inherited the keeping of it — the names, the trails, the knowledge that the water remembers. Her mother's grave was the canyon itself, which is a hard grave to visit. The narrows, where the water had taken her twelve-year-old mother, was the one place Rosa had not been back to. She rode past its mouth most days, and she did not enter, and the canyon waited with its terrible patience.
The badge had been hers for a month. The canyon had been hers all her life. She did not yet know that the one would finish what the other had begun — that the law she carried would one day bring her, finally, back into the narrows, where the walls closed and the wind sounded like a voice and her mother had been waiting all these years.

Chapter 8: The Deputy
Her first month as deputy was a school the town did not know it was teaching.
The first test came on a Saturday night in the Nugget, payday, the room hot with a hundred men's money and temper. A knife fight broke out by the bar — two miners, a debt, a woman's name, the usual arithmetic — and the crowd made a ring and the room leaned in, hoping. Rosa did not draw. She walked into the ring, took the knife out of the first man's hand the way you take a toy from a child, and put him on the floor without raising her voice. The other man dropped his blade and looked at her. "That's all," she said. "Go home." And they went, and the Nugget's noise came back a half-degree lower, and Rosa walked out into the night with her rifle still in the scabbard, which was the point.
The second test was a claim dispute. Two men swore the same ground was theirs, one mule-cut rut's worth of boundary between them, and the town stood around waiting for the law to fail. Rosa walked the ground, knelt, read the rut — the depth, the set of the shoe, the drag of a worn hoof. Then she read the mules, both of them, standing patient and dusty. "That rut's a month old," she said, "and this mule's been shod since Tuesday. The boundary's his." The loser howled; the winner looked stunned; the town blinked and began, slowly, to understand that the deputy could see what other men had to be shown.
The third test was not one thing but a hundred small ones. Drunks walked home, their boots set on the trail instead of the street, their hats returned. A fight broken up without a gun. A widow's water barrel defended. The men tested her — they tested the badge on a woman the way they would have tested a new rifle, looking for the weak place. She did not give them one. She did not bluff, and she did not draw, and she did not need to, and the town learned that last thing slowly and never forgot it.
What the town came to feel for her was not quite affection and not quite fear. It was the way a town feels about weather — grudging, then full. They trusted her the way they trusted the canyon to be true: she did what she said, and she saw what was there, and she could not be bought, because a woman who reads dust has nothing to gain by being lied to.
But the town's trust was for the small things. The big thing was coming, and Rosa knew it the way the canyon knows rain — she could feel the weight of it building out on the road to Arroyo Seco, thirty miles of dust and wagon tracks and men who wanted what the canyon had. She did not know what the big thing was, or whose hand it would come from. She only knew her first month had passed, the old life was over, and the new life was dangerous — and that the danger had not yet shown its face.

Chapter 9: The First Theft
The first theft was small, and the town did not want to see it.
High-grade copper — the selected ore, the green-and-black rock ready for the railhead — came up short at the weigh in Arroyo Seco. One wagon's load, more or less, gone between the mine and the stockyard thirty miles out. The weighmaster's slip said what the scales said, and the scales said a load had vanished from the road, and there was no way to argue with a scale. Hiram Brand came down from the mine office white with fury, demanding to know who was watching the road. No one was. There had never been anyone watching the road. The road was the canyon's, and the canyon had always kept it honest.
Sheriff Grant heard the news in the Nugget and went very still, and then very drunk. Rosa found him in the office at midnight with the empty bottle and the door open, staring at the wanted posters on the wall like they were the faces of all his failures. The town had outgrown his nerve, and he knew it, and the knowing was in his eyes. "It's only started," he said, and Rosa thought he meant the theft, and later she understood he meant everything — the town, the fever, the canyon, the weight of it all.
The loss was measured in the assay office, where Abel Vickery's books told the story no one wanted to hear. Vickery was meticulous, mild, a man whose arithmetic was unassailable, and his books said what the scales said: the copper had been weighed at the mine, and it had not arrived at the railhead in the recorded amount. The gap was real. The gap was small. And a small, regular gap is a patient thing, Rosa thought, and she had lived her whole life among patient things — the canyon, the seasons, the slow turn of the herding year. A small theft that repeated would be a large theft by summer.
The town's confidence cracked a little, like ice in a spring thaw. The miners' talk turned suspicious; the herders' wagons were looked at sideways; the Nugget's speculation was loud and the Last Water's was mean. Brand wanted a hanging before the culprit was found, which was his way — hard, sharp, fair only after the fact. Grant wanted the bottle. And the town wanted a name, any name, to put to the thing that had stolen from them.
Rosa wanted the dust. She walked the road to Arroyo Seco the day after, alone, in the heat, reading the ruts of every wagon that had passed since the weighing — the deep ones, the shallow ones, the place where a wheel had left the road and come back, the place where a wagon could stop unseen. She read the canyon's memory in the dust, and the canyon told her a truth she was not yet ready to hear: the gap was not on the road. The thief was not a stranger. The thief was someone who knew the weighing, the loading, the road, the hides, the schedule — someone the town trusted. She did not know his name yet. But the canyon had begun, in its patient way, to write it.

Chapter 10: The Copper
What was being stolen, and what the stealing did to men, was the whole story of the fever.
The copper was malachite and chalcocite — the green and the black, the two faces of the canyon's ore. Malachite came out of the upper wall in bands of deep green, the color of the green eye itself, the color the sheep people had known for generations without ever naming its worth. Chalcocite was black as a moonless night, heavy, rich beyond the green. The two were blasted from the rock, sorted by hand in the picking shed, and the high-grade — the selected ore worth sending thirty miles to the railhead — was weighed, loaded, and sent down the canyon on mule wagons to Arroyo Seco, and from there to the world.
The mine was La Fortuna. The men worked the face in the bunkhouses' dawn and the shaft's lamplight, and their wages were weighed against their ore, and no one had ever read the company's books but the company. The copper bought what the canyon had never had — money, machinery, a hundred men, a town. It bought the frame buildings and the schoolhouse that was coming and the graveyard that was coming sooner. It put a fever in men's eyes, and Rosa watched it spread like a rumor of water in a dry year, the same rumor that had brought the first strangers up the sheep trail.
The green eye of the canyon was the same green as the malachite in the wagons. That was the cruelest thing about the fever: it had always been there, the canyon's copper, the place the sheep would not drink, known to the herders for generations and never once a temptation. The canyon had kept its green eye open for a thousand years and no man had thought to covet it. Now a hundred men had given their lives to it, and the green that had been only the canyon's was the color of the town's whole hunger.
Rosa remembered when the green rock was only the canyon's. She remembered the herders' year turning beneath the green eye — the lambing, the high summer, the bells coming down through the narrows in autumn — and the green in the wall that was just part of the canyon, like the red of the walls and the blue of the sky, no more remarkable than a stone in a streambed. The canyon had never been poor, because it had never known it was rich. The town knew it was rich, and was poor in the one thing the canyon had in abundance — the knowledge of what was enough.
The first theft had cracked the town's confidence. But the theft was not the danger, Rosa understood, riding home in the red evening light with the dust standing gold around the mare's legs. The danger was the fever itself — the patient, multiplying hunger that had put the green eye in men's pockets and the lie in their books. She had watched the canyon turn its seasons for twenty-nine years. She was beginning to understand that she was watching the town turn into something that could steal from itself — and that her badge would not be enough, unless she was.

Chapter 11: The Wagons
The copper left the canyon the way everything left the canyon — on mule wagons, down the road to Arroyo Seco.
Rosa rode the route the morning after the weigh came short, and she rode it slow. The road had been cut the year before, a raw scar across the sheep trails where the wagon wheels had worn through the old dust to the gravel beneath. She knew the trails it had cut across; she had walked them as a girl, and it troubled her, the way iron had sliced the canyon's oldest roads to make a road for ore.
The loading was done at the mine's weigh station. The high-grade was weighed on the platform scales, tallied on the slip, and loaded onto the wagons under the eye of the company clerk — at least, under the eye when the clerk was not at his dinner, which he often was. The weigh at Arroyo Seco came thirty miles later, at the stockyard scales, and the two weighings were supposed to agree. They did not. The gap was the road itself.
The road ran through the narrows' mouth and along the canyon floor where the walls went red and quiet, and for a long stretch it was a road no one watched. There were no guards on it. There had never been guards on it — a road through a canyon with nothing but sheep had never needed watching. A wagon could leave it at half a dozen places, where the arroyos opened onto the flat, and rest in a fold of the land with the walls standing over it, and no one behind would see it gone until it came back.
She found the place on the second day. A dry arroyo where the brush had been cut back, a space for a wagon and a team, screened from the road by a shoulder of red rock. The ruts were there — wheel ruts, fresh, deeper on the way out than on the way in. She knelt in the dust and read them the way she read everything, and the ruts told her a wagon had come here loaded and left lighter, and come back with something heavy in it.
She did not tell Grant. She told no one. The dust was the canyon's memory, and this was a memory she meant to keep until she knew the name that belonged to it. She rode home in the long red evening with the place in her head, and she thought: the road has always kept the canyon's secrets, and it has begun to keep the town's.

Chapter 12: The Foreman
Ezra Tanner ran the mine the way some men run a clock — everything in its place, everything on time, nothing wasted.
Rosa watched him for a week from the edges of the operation. She watched him at the hoist in the mornings, at the picking shed at noon, at the weigh station in the evenings when the last wagon was loaded for the run to Arroyo Seco. He knew every man's name, every mule's temper, every seam of the rock in the face. When a winch chain snapped and a man was nearly crushed, Tanner was there before the dust settled, and the whole shift heard him speak the accident's cause and the fix in the same breath. The men respected him. The company respected him. The town respected him, and so, she found, did she.
That was the trouble. A man who ran the mine too well to miss a theft in his own wagons was the same man who could arrange one so well no one would see it. The thought came to her in the evening light and she held it loosely, the way her father held a newborn lamb — aware of its fragility, unwilling to crush it with a fist. She did not want it to be him.
The other thing the town remembered, and Rosa could not stop remembering, was the marriage. Ezra Tanner, a mine foreman with dust in his blood, had married Lupe Ortega, a herder's daughter — married into the sheep people, the canyon's own, in a wedding that still surprised the town two years later. The men at the Nugget said it was strange; the women at El Refugio said it was love; the canyon kept its counsel. But the marriage meant something Rosa could not set down. It meant the foreman had a wife whose family drove hides and wool down that same road to Arroyo Seco, in wagons that left the canyon regularly and came back with money.
She watched him at the weigh station the evening of the seventh day, the red sun on his back, his hands on the scale beam, patient and fair as ever. The copper was loaded, the slip signed, the wagon rolled out toward the narrows. A perfect man to run the mine. A perfect man to run the theft. Rosa walked her mare home through the dust and told herself she was wrong to look at him — and told herself, too, that she had stopped believing it.

Chapter 13: The Assay
Abel Vickery's office was the quietest room in Cobre, and the most truthful.
The assayer was a mild, meticulous man who measured everything and trusted nothing that had not passed across his scales. His office smelled of acid and charcoal and clean paper, and his books were kept in an order the town itself had never achieved. Rosa came to him the week after the weigh went short, sent by no one, and stood in his doorway until he waved her to the stool by the window.
He spread the books for her as if they were a map of the canyon and she a rider who could read it. The grades — the malachite and chalcocite of every wagon that had left the mine, sampled and recorded. The weights — the tallies from the mine platform and the tallies from the Arroyo Seco scales. The assays — the green and black ore's worth, rendered into numbers a man could hold in his hand.
Rosa had learned letters late and learned them hard, but numbers were like dust to her — a record of what had passed, patient and true. She ran her finger down the columns and found the shape of the gap. It was small. It was regular. It was patient. Every few loads, a little less high-grade arrived at the railhead than had left the mine — not enough to break any single load, enough to make a fortune by autumn. A thief who took a little, often, and never greedily.
The books proved it was inside the operation. A stranger on the road could not know which loads to take or where the gap would not be noticed. Whoever stole this copper knew the loading, the weighing, the schedule, the road. He was one of the men the town trusted — the clerk, the weighmaster, the teamsters, the foreman.
"Your arithmetic is unassailable," Rosa said, and Vickery looked up, surprised by the word from a woman's mouth.
"It is the only thing I have," he said.
She thanked him and walked out into the dust and the sun, the shape of the gap in her head like a road she had not yet found the end of. The books had told her the thief was inside. Now she had to learn which trust was the lie.

Chapter 14: The Suspects
The town wanted a name, and the town had its names ready.
Jake Bodine was the first. Big, loud, in debt to half the saloons in Cobre, the kind of man the Nugget's speculation settled on like dust. He had been seen at the weigh station the day the load went short. He had been seen drinking the night before, boasting he would be rich by fall. The town said arrest him. Brand said hang him. Rosa went instead to the stable where Bodine slept and read his habits the way she read everything.
His saddle was greased and worn on the left from a leg that had been broken in a mine and never set true. His boots had never walked the road to Arroyo Seco — the road dust was fine and red, and the mud on his boots was the black of the bunkhouse yard. He was loud because he was afraid, and he was afraid of the debt, and a man that loud about being rich was a man who had never stolen anything in his life. He had the wrong kind of loud. Rosa cleared him.
The teamster with the grudge came next — a man fired from La Fortuna for a fight, who had sworn he would see the company ruined. But his wagon had been in Cobre the day of the theft, and a half-dozen men could swear to it, and his anger, she found, was the honest kind that talks and does not plan. She cleared him. The saloon man with the quick hands at the Last Water was the third, and his hands were quick, but the theft had required a man who could drive a wagon and lift a hide, and this man could not tell a mule from a horse.
She cleared them all, one by one, and the town grew impatient. Grant felt the pressure and drank against it. Brand came to the office and demanded an arrest, any arrest, and Rosa told him she would not arrest a man because the town wanted an arrest — the canyon did not forgive a lie, even when the town demanded it.
The truth was plainer than the town would see. None of the usual suspects knew the weighing, the loading, the road, the hides — the patient knowledge the gap required. The thief was not where everyone was looking. He was where the town never looked, because the town trusted him too completely to think. Rosa stood in the door of the office in the red evening, watching the dust stand gold in the light, and she let the truth take its own shape in her. The town wanted a name. She was beginning to believe the name would break the town's heart.

Chapter 15: The Miners
The bunkhouse talk was the town's weather, and Rosa listened to it the way she listened to the wind.
The miners came off shift at dark and gathered at the hoist and in the bunkhouses, and their talk was wages and ore and the company, and by the second week after the theft it was the herders. The wagons that left the canyon with hides and wool left from the far side of town, past the corrals, and they were Spanish wagons, and the men who drove them were the same men who had grazed these slopes for generations — and suspicion found them easier than it found the company's own men.
The Cousin Jacks from Cornwall were the loudest at first — Jack Trelawney among them, called Cousin Jack by every man in the bunkhouse, a fair-minded man with a face like weathered ore. "It's not the herders," he said one night in the Nugget, and the table went quiet, because a man who said that was speaking against the town's grain. "A man who steals copper is a man who knows copper. Them sheep men don't know the high-grade from a river stone."
Rosa heard it from the door. She did not step in. She was learning that the miners would say more freely what they thought if the law was not in the room, and she stood in the dust and the dark outside the Nugget's light and let the talk come to her.
The union murmur ran under the loud talk like a thread of water under a dry creek bed. The company weighed the ore and set the wages, and no one had ever read the books but the company, and the men suspected the company of cheating their weights the way they suspected the herders of the theft — without proof, with feeling. Rosa heard both suspicions, and she heard the shape of them: the town was looking everywhere but at the operation itself. Even the miners' best guess, that the thief knew copper, pointed at the mine's own men — the clerk, the weighmaster, the foreman — and the town's suspicion ran the other way, downhill, toward the herders' wagons and the Spanish names.
She walked her round after the Nugget shut its doors, through the dust and the cold of the canyon night, and she thought about what a fair man had said in the loud room. A man who steals copper is a man who knows copper. The town had heard the words. The town had not understood them.

Chapter 16: The Herders
The company's eye turned to the sheep people, and the wound of it was that the arithmetic fit.
The herders' wagons were the only regular traffic on the road that did not belong to the mine. They carried hides and wool down to Arroyo Seco, where the railhead bought them, and they came back with money and supplies and the quiet talk of their own kind. They were the canyon's first nation of work, and the town's new suspicion fell on them like a shadow on the water.
Rosa defended them — she was one of them, and everyone in Cobre knew it. She stood in Brand's office and told him the herders had no more reason to steal the copper than the canyon did; they had grazed this land for four generations and never taken more than the wool off a sheep's back. Brand's face was hard and patient, and when she finished he said only: "Their wagons leave the canyon with hides and wool. Hides weigh what they weigh. And they leave from the side of town no one watches."
The wound was that he was not wrong about the wagons. Rosa could not make the herders' wagons lighter than they were. She walked out of the mine office into the sun and stood in the dust, and the thing she could not say to Brand was the thing she could not say to anyone: the arithmetic fitted the herders' wagons, and if the copper was leaving in the hides, then it was leaving in her own people's honest trade, and she would have to suspect one of her own.
Her father saw it in her face that night by the fire in the adobe. He asked nothing, but he looked at her a long time, and then he said, in the old way, that the sheep did not lie, and the hides did not lie, and the canyon did not forgive a lie, and a lie told in the herders' name would rot the herders' name for a generation.
Mateo was harder. Her brother had been a boy when their mother died, and the town had always seemed to him a threat wearing a hundred faces, and now the town was pointing at their own kind. "They blame us," he said, and there was a fury in his young voice that Rosa had not heard before. "They will always blame us."
She rode the ridge that night under the stars and looked down at the lights of Cobre, and she thought about the arithmetic and the hides and the one name the canyon was beginning to write in the dust. She did not want the name to be a herder's name. But she had learned dust before she had learned letters, and the dust did not lie, and she would follow it wherever it went, even home.

Chapter 17: The Hides
The railhead at Arroyo Seco was where the canyon's honesty came to be sold, and Rosa stood in its dust and watched her own people unload.
It was a hides wagon, one of the Ortega brothers' wagons — Cisco at the traces, Manuel walking the off side — come down from the canyon with a load bound for the tanneries east. The hides were rolled tight, stacked like cordwood, the smell of salt and wool and old blood rising off them in the heat. The railhead men swung them down one by one, and the scales took them, and the weighmaster wrote the numbers in his book.
Rosa had lifted hides all her life. She had helped her mother salt them, roll them, bind them; she had carried them on her shoulder from the skinning shed to the storehouse since she was old enough to walk under the weight. She knew a hide's weight the way she knew a trail's turn — in the bone, in the balance. And as she watched the rolled hides come down from the Ortega wagon, she knew with a cold that went through her that one of them was wrong.
It was not that it was heavier — not so she could prove it by the scales, not so the weighmaster would notice, not so anyone who had not carried hides since childhood would feel it. The roll was a little too firm, a little too dense, the shape of it a little too regular for a hide that had been salted and rolled by hand. A hide weighed what it weighed. This one weighed what a hide and a piece of high-grade weighed.
She did not open it. The railhead was full of eyes, and the men who would notice an opened hide were the men she was not yet ready to face. She counted the rolls as they came down — the wrong one, and another behind it — and she filed the count in her head the way she filed the ruts in the dust, and she walked away across the yard so that no one would see her know.
The truth opened in her as she rode home, slow, in the falling light: the hides had always been honest. The herders' trade had been honest for four generations, and the canyon's word had been good on the road longer than the town had a name. The lie had not come from the hides. It had come from whoever had learned to hide copper inside the honest trade, and rolled it so well that only a woman who had carried hides all her life could feel the weight of it.
The name was there in the dust of her mind, patient, waiting. She rode through the narrows in the dark and heard the wind make its voice, and she thought: whoever taught himself to weigh the copper the way I weigh a hide has been watching the canyon longer than I knew.

Chapter 18: The Weight
Rosa worked the arithmetic the way her father worked a season — slowly, in the evenings, letting it come to her.
The numbers were the hide weights and the wagon weights and the weighmaster's slips at Arroyo Seco, and they were the ruts on the canyon road, the depth of the wheel cuts where a wagon passed loaded and where it passed light. She laid them out in her head the way she laid out a camp, one thing after another, in the order of the canyon. Hide weights. Wagon weights. The load that had gone short at the mine. The load that had not arrived at the railhead. The patient, regular gap in Vickery's books.
The copper rode in the hides. She was certain of that now — certain enough to have stopped doubting, and the certainty sat in her like a stone she had swallowed. A hide weighs what it weighs, and a hide with a piece of the high-grade rolled in its heart weighs more, and the weighmaster's slip would show it if anyone thought to check the hides against their true number. No one checked. No one had ever checked. The hides were honest, and the town's suspicion had stopped at the honest thing's door and gone no further.
Whose hands loaded them? That was the question the canyon had been writing in the dust, and Rosa had been walking around it for weeks, the way you walk around a hole in the road you do not want to look into. The hands that knew the copper were the mine's hands. The hands that knew the hides were the herders' hands. And there was one pair of hands in all the canyon that knew both — the hands of a man who had married into the sheep people and risen through the mine, who ran the loading and the weighing and the road, whose wife's family's honest wagons left the canyon as regular as the seasons.
She did not say the name. She could not say the name. She said it to no one — not to Grant, not to her father, not to the fire in the adobe where she sat with the canyon's dust still on her boots. But the canyon knew, and the canyon kept its counsel, and the name sat in her like a seed that had already put down root.
The moon came up over the red wall, and the wind moved in the narrows, and Rosa thought about Ezra Tanner, and about his wife Lupe, and about the honest Ortega wagons rolling down to Arroyo Seco with copper in their hides. She had wanted it to be a stranger. The canyon had told her otherwise, in the patience of its dust. The canyon had been right about everything it had ever told her. She went to sleep with the name held loose and careful in her hands, the way her father held a newborn thing he did not want to harm, and the fear in her was not for herself.

Chapter 19: The Second Theft
The thief grew bold, and the boldness cost a man his life.
The full wagon of high-grade vanished somewhere between the mine and Arroyo Seco — not a little, this time, not a patient, regular gap, but a whole load, gone, as if the road had opened and swallowed it. Rosa was on the road by the time the word came back, but the word that came back was worse than the missing load. At the stockyard in Arroyo Seco, the night watchman Hob Barker had been found dead in the dust beside the scales, struck down, his lantern cold beside him.
The town howled. Brand came down from the mine office with a face like rock and demanded a hanging — the herders' wagons, the herders' names, he had been right all along, and the law had done nothing. Grant heard it in the office and went very still and then very drunk, and Rosa found him at midnight with the empty bottle and the wanted posters on the wall, and she saw the old lawman failing the way she had seen the canyon fail a dry year — not all at once, but by degrees, by the things he no longer had the nerve to do.
She took the road alone at dawn. She told no one where she was going; there was no one to tell. The mare was fresh, the rifle was in the scabbard — her father's rifle — and the badge was on her chest, and the canyon opened ahead of her in the first light, the walls going from black to red, the dust standing gold in the low sun.
She read the road as she rode. The ruts of the stolen wagon were there in the fresh dust — deep for a wagon carrying high-grade — and they told her more than Brand's fury or the town's fear could: they left the road at the place she had found in the first weeks, the arroyo screened by the shoulder of red rock, and they came back onto the road lighter, and they went on to the stockyard where Hob Barker lay dead.
The wind moved in the narrows as she passed the mouth of the canyon, and it sounded, to a woman who had heard it all her life, like a voice. She did not stop. A man was dead, and the canyon had begun to write the name of the man who killed him, and Rosa was riding the canyon's writing now, the way she had ridden it all her life — dust before letters, memory before law.
The road ran on ahead of her, red and patient, and she followed it, and the canyon kept her secret and her company.

Chapter 20: The Chase
The ruts of the hides wagon led her off the road and into the canyon's quiet, and Rosa followed them the way she had followed every trail of her life — patiently, in the heat, reading what the dust had written.
The wagon had left the road at the arroyo she had found in the first weeks, the place screened from the road by the shoulder of red rock. The ruts were deep there — deeper than hides should have cut them, deep as a wagon carrying a load of ore. It had come in empty, left full, and the tracks told her the rest: the place where the men had worked, the scuffed ground where the transfer had happened, the fresh-cut brush dragged over the evidence in a hurry.
The heat of the day came down on the canyon floor. The mare's sides darkened with sweat, and Rosa let her have her pace, saving her for the work ahead. The walls reddened toward evening, the long light falling across the red rock and the green seam of ore in the upper wall, and the dust of the road stood gold in the air behind her, and she read the trail the way she had read it as a girl — the drag of the tarpaulin where a loose end had trailed and scored a line in the gravel, the place where a wheel had caught and a man had jumped down to curse it, the boot prints, the shape of the work.
The trail went where she had always known it would go, and the knowing sat in her like a stone. It climbed the old sheep trail above the narrows, the path worn by hooves and generations, and it ended at the mouth of la cueva — the shallow cave above the narrows where the herders had sheltered from weather and wolves, and where the old ones said the canyon kept its secrets. The cache. She knew it without seeing inside, the way she had known the wrong hide on the Ortega wagon — in the bone, in the balance.
The wind in the narrows rose as evening came on, and it sounded like a voice, and Rosa had heard it all her life and never once been afraid of it, and she was not afraid now. She tied the mare below, took her father's rifle from the scabbard, and went up the old trail on foot, her breath careful, her boots finding the worn places in the rock as if they remembered the way themselves — because they did. The cave had sheltered her people for generations. Someone had made it the hiding place of the theft.
The canyon told the truth. It had told her all along. She had come at last to the place where it would tell her the name out loud.

Chapter 21: The Narrows

The ruts of the hides wagon ran deep into the sand, deeper than hides should cut, and Rosa had read them all the way from the wagon road. Now they turned off where the walls opened, and she left the bay mare ground-tied at the mouth of the narrows and went on foot, her father's rifle across her arms, her breath slow and careful.

The narrows closed above her. The red walls leaned in until the sky was a bent ribbon, and the wind that lived in that place found her and sang. It had always sounded like a voice to the herders. Rosa had grown up hearing her mother say the canyon was speaking, and that a body could learn to listen. Today the wind sang a warning, and Rosa listened.

She climbed the old herders' stair, a notch in the wall her grandfather had cut, up to la cueva, the shallow cave above the narrows. The sheep trails had run past it for generations; herders had sheltered there in the lightning. The mouth was dark and low, and the air came out of it smelling of hide and of something sharp underneath, the bite of malachite, the green dust of the green eye.

She went in. Her eyes took a moment to make the dark. Then she saw them, bundles wrapped in sheep hide, tied with the same rope the herders used, stacked like cordwood against the back wall. She cut one thong with her knife and let the hide fall. Inside, the ore gleamed, high-grade, the selected green and black that Vickery's books said never reached the railhead. She laid her hand flat on it. The copper was cold and patient. It had been coming here for months, hidden inside her own people's honest trade, and the hides had carried the lie as easily as they had ever carried a lamb.

Rosa stepped out of the cave and stood in the wind, thinking. The evidence was hers now. She could ride for Grant, bring the law, take it all down clean. And then she heard it, under the wind, the creak of a wheel and the jingle of harness, coming up the narrows from the town side. A wagon, moving slow, careful, at an hour when honest wagons were unhitched.

She stepped back into the cave mouth, into the shadow, and worked a cartridge into the rifle's chamber. The click was small, but in the narrows everything small was loud. The wind rose and sang.

She had her evidence. Now the thief was coming to move it. Rosa settled against the cool stone and waited for whatever came up the trail, the canyon's voice at her back.

Chapter 22: The Confrontation

He came up the stair the way she had come, and he knew the way. Ezra Tanner moved like a man who had climbed it a hundred times, hand to the notch, boot finding the old holds. He carried a coil of rope over one shoulder and a lantern in his hand, unlit, and he was already looking at the cave mouth when Rosa spoke.

"Ezra."

He went still. The lantern came down slowly, his hand went to his hip, and Rosa watched him weigh the dark between them, her voice, the rifle, the depth of his trouble. The wind sang on, and below them in the narrows the wagon waited with its team.

"Deputy," he said.

"You've been patient," she said. "Months of it. That's what held the books together, the patience. The weighmaster never saw it because it came regular as water."

"Rosa." His voice was tired, almost gentle. "You don't know what you're stepping into."

"I know what's behind me. Hide-wrapped, green as the eye. I know what it's worth, and I know your hand is on your gun."

For a moment he did not answer. Then: "The company's been cheating those weights for years. You know that. Every man in the mine knows that. I only took back what was owed."

"The copper's not yours to take back. You're a thief, Ezra. A patient thief, but a thief."

He drew then, fast, the way a man draws when he has decided, and the shot went into the rock beside her head, sharp and loud, and the narrows took it and threw it back and forth until the whole canyon rang. Rosa did not fire. She held her place in the cave mouth, her father's rifle across her, and watched his eyes in the ringing dark.

"Your lantern's unlit," she said. "You meant to carry the copper out quiet. You never meant to use that gun, Ezra. It came out because you're cornered, and a cornered man's hand lies."

He fired again, into the cave wall above her, and the stone cried out and the echoes took it. Rosa raised the rifle and put one shot over his head into the rock, and the canyon answered her with a sound like the whole wall falling.

Silence came down like dust.

"Drop it," Rosa said. "Drop it and walk down ahead of me, and I'll take you in the way you came, on the wagon road, in the light, with your hands where the town can see them. Or stay here and let the canyon decide, the way it decides everything."

His arm came down slowly. The revolver turned in his hand, muzzle down, and he let it fall into the sand. It made a small, final sound.

"Rosa," he said, and his voice cracked. "You don't know."

"I know the law," she said. "And I know this canyon. That's enough."

She stepped out of the cave into the open, the rifle steady, and Ezra Tanner walked before her down the stair into the singing wind, one hand against the old notches as if he could not bear to look up.

Chapter 23: The Confession

They came down out of the narrows into the wide open of the canyon floor, and the light hit Tanner like a blow. He blinked in it, and for a moment Rosa saw the foreman he had been, the man who ran the hoist and the crews and the weights with a steady hand, and then he stood in the sun, empty-handed, and she saw the shape of the trouble he had carried.

"Walk to the wagon," she said. "Slow."

He walked. At the wagon he stopped with his back to her, the team shifting in the traces, and he began to talk without turning around, as if he could not say it facing the canyon and the law at once.

"It was her family's trade," he said. "The hides. The wagons. The Ortega name on the waybill. I married into the cleanest thing in this canyon and I used it to carry the dirt."

"The company cheated the weights," Rosa said. "I've read the books. That part's true."

"True." He said it like the word hurt him. "They've cheated the men's weights for years, shaved the assay, paid for the grade they didn't weigh, pocketed the difference. I kept the books straight for them and I watched it, year in, year out. And I told myself, when I started, that I was only taking back what was owed."

The wind moved over them. The team stamped.

"That's the story I told myself," he said. "That's the lie I wrapped around the theft the way I wrapped the copper in the hides. And the lie went further than the copper. It went into Lupe's house, into her brothers' wagons. I told them the loads were heavy, told them the mine was shipping salted hides for the tannery trade, and they believed me. I took the cleanest thing and I dirtied it, and it wasn't taking back a debt anymore. It was stealing from the canyon. It was stealing from her."

His shoulders went down, a long slow settling, like a man putting down a weight heavier than he could name.

"I'm not asking," he said. "I know what the law does with this. I'll take the sentence and I'll take it clean."

Rosa was quiet a long time. The canyon held its light around them, the red walls, the dust standing in the afternoon, the green eye high on the far wall watching like it had watched everything since before the herders came.

"Walk," she said. "You'll tell it that way, in front of the judge, in front of the town. You'll say her name clear, and the Ortega name clear, and you'll say they never knew. You'll put it right for the people you dirtied before you take your years."

He turned then and looked at her, and there was something in his face she had not expected, a kind of relief, as if the confession had let him set something down.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, I'll say it."

"Then walk," Rosa said. "The town's waiting, and the law's done waiting."

He walked ahead of her into the long afternoon, and behind them the narrows sang on, holding its secret the way it had held hers and her mother's before her.

Chapter 24: The Recovery

They brought the copper out of the cave and out of the hides in the middle of the town, and Rosa made sure the whole canyon saw it done.

She sent Mateo up the old stair with a lantern and the word, and the town came out to watch, the miners from the hoist, the saloons emptying, the boarding house women at their doors. Brand stood at the front of it with his arms folded, his face hard as the rock behind him. Grant stood beside Rosa, sober, steadying himself against the office door as the bundles came down.

Tanner's wagon rolled in under guard, and Rosa herself cut the thongs on the first hide, in front of everyone, and let the green ore fall out into the sunlight where every man could see the truth of it. The town drew one breath. Then the counting began.

Bundle after bundle came out of the hide-bound casings, high-grade malachite and chalcocite, the selected green and black that Vickery's books said had vanished into the air. They weighed each one on the assay office scale, Abel Vickery writing the numbers in his even hand, the town reading them over his shoulder. The copper made a growing green and black heap in the street, and the heap told a story the way the ruts had told Rosa a story, patient, small, regular, the theft of a year brought home.

And then Rosa saw Lupe.

She had come down from the adobe with her brothers, Cisco and Manuel, the Ortegas standing together at the back of the crowd where the herders stood. Lupe's face was still, and Rosa watched the understanding cross it, the hides she had cut herself, the wagons she had watched her brothers load, the honest trade of her family's blood carrying the lie all the way to the stockyard. Her face did not crumple. It hardened slowly, the way earth hardens, and Rosa thought she had never seen a woman's face carry so much and hold.

Rosa went to her, through the crowd, and stood in front of her.

"You didn't know," Rosa said. "I know you didn't know."

Lupe looked at the heap of green ore, then back at Rosa. "Las pieles," she said in the old tongue. The hides. My family's hides. My husband's hands. She did not say the rest; Rosa heard it anyway.

"He confessed," Rosa said. "He'll say it in court, that you never knew. That your brothers never knew."

Lupe was quiet. Then she said, "He is my husband, Rosa. Whatever he is, he was my husband. And he is my family's blood and my family's shamed name together. I will carry that."

Rosa had no comfort that could answer it, so she stood with her in it, the two of them watching the copper come clean out of the hides until the last bundle was weighed and the heap stood whole in the street, the year's theft returned, the books made right, and a woman's grief standing at the edge of it with the canyon red behind her.

Chapter 25: The Trial

The circuit judge came down the road from Arroyo Seco in a buckboard, and the town had never seen its law wear such a face. Judge Ashford was old, white-bearded, and wore a coat of black wool against the heat, and he looked at the town of Cobre the way a man looks at a flooded creek, as something to be crossed carefully and left behind.

They held the trial in the Nugget's back room, the only room in town with a door that closed and a table wide enough for papers. The miners crowded the door and the windows; the herders stood at the back in their boots, hats in hand; Grant sat on a bench against the wall, and Rosa stood beside the judge's table with the badge on her chest and the dust of the narrows still on her.

Vickery came first, with his books. He read the numbers in his careful voice, the high-grade assayed at the mine, the amounts recorded at the weigh, the gap between them, month by month, small and regular as a clock. "The arithmetic is unassailable," he said, and Rosa watched the miners nod at the word, as if the books were a thing they trusted more than men.

Then the weighmaster from Arroyo Seco, who read his slips, and his testimony put the theft's road down in plain fact: wagons had arrived carrying hides and left the books lighter than the hides should have made them, and the difference had gone somewhere between the canyon and the railhead.

Then Rosa, who told it simplest of all. She spoke of ruts too deep for hides, of a cave above the narrows, of a stair her grandfather had cut. She did not raise her voice. The room was silent, and the wind moved in the street outside, and every man there understood that the law had walked a trail the town could not read.

Then they called Ezra Tanner.

He came in with his hands tied and his head up, and he said it clean, the way he had promised Rosa he would, the company's cheated weights, the years of shaving the assay, the hides of his wife's family carrying the lie, Lupe's name and the Ortegas' name spoken clear in front of the whole town, in front of the herders at the back, in front of the men he had worked beside. He took the confession and he did not reach for his excuse.

The room was very still when he finished. Judge Ashford looked at him a long time, the wind moving in the window, and when he spoke his voice was old and dry as canyon rock.

"Mr. Tanner," he said, "the law does not care whether the company is honest. The law cares whether you are. And it appears the company's dishonesty has made you a thief, which is a lesson in how dishonesty spreads. I will take a day to consider what the canyon's law requires of this town."

Rosa stood at the door as they led him out, and she understood for the first time that a trial was not the end of a thing. It was where a thing began to cost.

Chapter 26: The Justice

Judge Ashford took his day, and the town spent it arguing. The Nugget ran hot on the subject of the rope. "A man kills a watchman and they'll hang him on sight," Jake Bodine said, loud as ever. "A man steals a fortune and they measure it in years?" But Rosa heard the herders talk differently in the back of El Refugio, and she heard Grant talk the way a lawman talks when the law is at its turning.

"Years in the territory prison," Grant said to her in the office that night. "That's what it'll be. And it'll be right. A hanging would make this town a mob with a memory. Years make it a place that tried a man and kept its own law."

In the morning Judge Ashford read the sentence from the table in the Nugget's back room, the miners pressed at the windows, the herders at the back.

"Ezra Tanner, the court finds you guilty of the theft of high-grade copper from the Fortuna Mining Company, and guilty of the manner of its carrying, which dragged honest men and an honest family into a lie. The court sentences you to eight years in the territorial prison at Santa Fe. The court finds no evidence that any member of the Ortega family knew the nature of the loads they carried, and their names are cleared."

The herders shifted at the back. Rosa watched Lupe's face, and saw the grief in it ease by a small, measurable amount, the way water eases around a stone.

"Furthermore," the judge said, and the room went still again, "the court has reviewed the company's own assay books and finds that the Fortuna Mining Company has systematically underweighed and underpaid its men for years. The court orders that the company pay its back weights in full, in cash, to every man owed, before it ships another pound of copper from this canyon. The town of Cobre will learn its law's justice, and so will its company."

For a moment no one moved. Then, slowly, the miners began to understand what had been said, and the murmur rose and turned, and Rosa saw Hiram Brand's face go white at the back of the room, and saw him close his eyes and take it, the way a man takes a blow he has known was coming.

"Sheriff Grant," the judge said, "the law thanks your deputy. In a town without her, this would have been a lynching with better weather."

Rosa stood at the door, and she felt the weight of the sentence settle over the town like the dust settling over the wagon road, the herders' names restored, the men paid what was owed, the thief's years measured, and the canyon's law, for the first time, meaning exactly what it said.

Afterward, in the street, she made sure the town heard the herders' clearing said again, her voice carrying over the crowd the way it carried over the wind in the narrows, because a name restored, she knew, was a thing that had to be spoken to hold.

Chapter 27: The Canyon

The trial ended, and the town went back to its work, and Rosa found herself, on the third morning after, riding up the old sheep trail to the ridge with the badge on her chest and no particular errand at all.

The bay mare knew the way better than she did. They climbed past the green eye on the far wall, past the old seep where the herders said the sheep would not drink, and up through the switchbacks her grandfather had cut for the flocks, until the canyon opened out below her like a room she had known all her life.

Rosa sat her saddle a long time, looking down.

She could see it all at once, the mine works with their headframe, the town of Cobre at the canyon mouth, the three saloons, the adobe that was her father's and hers, the sheep trails cutting across the new wagon road, the narrows where the walls closed, and far off the spring, el ojo del agua, winking up at the sky. Mine and town and sheep and trail, all of it held in the red bowl of the canyon like water in a cupped hand.

She had been afraid in the cave, and she had not been afraid. She had walked a man out of the narrows into the light, and the canyon had not swallowed either of them, and now it lay below her, whole, and she understood something she could not have put into words but knew the way she knew the feel of her own mare's reins.

The canyon could hold all of it. The copper and the fever, the town and the sheep, the lie and the truth, the walls were wide enough, and the water ran, and the wind sang, and there was room. There was room for all of it, if the law held the line.

Her mother had died in that canyon, taken by the flood in the narrows, the body never found, and for years Rosa had thought the canyon had swallowed her, too. But sitting the bay mare on the ridge, with the dust standing in the afternoon light and the green eye burning softly in the far wall, Rosa understood it differently. The canyon had not swallowed her mother. The canyon had kept her. Every spring, every flood, every wind in the narrows, that was the keeping.

Grief and pride sat in her together, quiet as two hands folded.

Below her the mine whistle blew for the shift change, small and thin across the distance, and the town went on with its fever and its building and its forgetting. But the ridge remembered, and so did she.

She turned the mare for home. The badge caught the sun. The canyon lay open below her, patient and red and full of the spring's turning, and Rosa rode down into it, small on the trail, at home in the only house she had ever known.

Chapter 28: The Spring

The herders' spring lay at the canyon head, where the red walls opened like a cup and the water came up out of the stone in a clear eye that looked straight up at the sky. El ojo del agua, the water's eye, the oldest landmark in the canyon, older than the town, older than the mine, older than Rosa could remember anyone being old.

She found Lupe there at evening, sitting on the flat stone where the herder women had sat for generations, and Rosa sat down beside her without a word, and the water's eye lay between them, looking up.

They were quiet a long time. The canyon was quiet with them, the wind high and small, the sheep moving somewhere out of sight with their bells, the town's noise thin and distant at the mouth. Here, at the head of the canyon, the world was still the old world, and the spring held its light.

"Él era bueno conmigo," Lupe said at last, in the old tongue, the way she spoke when the English town was far. He was good to me. "That is the hardest part, Rosa. He was good to me, and I never saw what he carried. I slept beside a lie for years and never woke."

Rosa did not answer right away. She watched the water's eye hold the sky. "You didn't carry it," she said. "You didn't make it. He made it, and he covered it in your name, and the name is what he dirtied, not the blood."

Lupe was silent, and then she said, "I have decided. I am taking my father's name again. I am Lupe Ortega, and I will be Lupe Ortega until the name is clean in the town's mouth."

Rosa nodded. It was the right thing, and she said so.

"And the wagons," Lupe said. "My brothers' wagons will carry wool. Only wool. The hides." The pause was long. "The hides will be my family's trade no more. I will not have a single hide of my father's blood carry anything that is not what it seems. The lie ended in the cave. It will not begin again on a wagon bed."

The spring moved, the water's eye blinking with the slow lift of the water.

"Then the name will come clean," Rosa said. "A name is a thing you can wash, if you keep it out in the light."

Lupe looked at her, and her eyes were wet, and Rosa thought she had never seen a harder or a braver decision made so quietly.

"You stood with me," Lupe said. "In the street, in front of all of them. You stood with me and you did not move."

"The herders' blood carried the lie without knowing it," Rosa said. "The least the law could do is stand with it while it washed. That's all a law's for, I'm beginning to think, standing with the true thing while it comes clean."

They sat until the light went amber and the water's eye held the first stars, and when Rosa rode home the spring was behind her, witness to what had been said, the oldest thing in the canyon keeping the new beginning the way it had kept every beginning since before the herders came.

Chapter 29: The Mine, Kept

The Fortuna's whistle blew the shift in on the first Monday after the trial, and the town came out to watch the mine open again, not under Ezra Tanner, but under a new foreman chosen from the men, a Cousin Jack named from the hoist crew, chosen because the men trusted him and the books trusted him and, Rosa saw, because the company had learned that it could not run a canyon it could not keep honest.

She stood at the weigh this time, and she made sure the town understood the new order. The weighing was done in the open now, on a scale set up at the mine's mouth where any man could watch it, the ore weighed in daylight, the grade read aloud, the weights recorded on a board hung where the men could see their own work counted. The old weighmaster's shed, where the numbers had gone to die in the dark, stood empty with its door off its hinges.

The men tested it at first, the way they tested everything, a few came early and watched, suspicious, waiting for the old shave. But the board did not lie and the scale did not lie, and by the second week the weigh was a thing the men stood around at shift's end the way they stood around a fire, and Rosa heard Jack Trelawney say to the crew, "That's the first law this mine ever had that weighed as heavy as the copper."

She was at the office when Brand came.

He stood in the doorway a moment, hat in hand, Hiram Brand, who had wanted a hanging, who had demanded results, who had never had a civil sentence for the canyon's law in his mouth. He looked at Rosa behind the desk, the badge on her chest, the rifle rack on the wall, and he said, "Deputy. Sheriff Grant about?"

"Grant's up at the boarding house," Rosa said. "You can say it to me."

Brand was quiet. Then he said, "You saved my mine. That's the first time in my life I've said that to a lawman and meant it. The company's shipping copper at a fair price and the men are working with their eyes on the board instead of on each other. If the law hadn't caught it, the strike would have bled out slow, cheated and rotted from inside. The canyon's law saved my mine. I wanted a hanging. I got an accounting. The accounting was better."

Rosa looked at him, and she understood that this was as far as Hiram Brand would ever bend, and that it was enough.

"The books stay honest," she said. "That's the price of the canyon's peace. You cheated the men once, and the town knows it, and the town's memory is long as the canyon."

"Then it'll stay honest," Brand said, and he put on his hat. "A canyon with a memory is cheaper to run than one without."

He left, and Rosa sat in the office with the evening coming on, and she heard the whistle blow the shift out, and she knew the mine was kept, not by the company, not by the foreman, but by the law, and the law was her, and the law would hold.

Chapter 30: The Town

The town of Cobre grew up that summer the way a boy grows when he has had a scare, suddenly, and toward steadiness.

Rosa saw it happen on her rounds, one door and one street at a time. The tents came down and the frame buildings went up in their places, lumber hauled thirty miles from the railhead, nailed square by the miners' own hands in the evenings. The boarding houses filled with wives now, and with the wives came wash lines and gardens and the smell of bread in the afternoons.

The schoolhouse came first. The miners raised it themselves, off-hours, with the company's blessing and the town's money, a single room with a stove and a blackboard, and a young woman from back east who had come to teach. Rosa watched the men of Cobre stand outside the open door that first morning, holding their hats, listening to the letters go up and down inside. Some were older than she was, and Rosa understood they were not listening to the children. They were listening to something they had been told they would never have, and it was there, in their own town, saying its alphabet.

The graveyard came second. They walled it with stones from the canyon, a low wall on the bench above the town, and they buried Hob Barker in it with his name cut in wood, and Rosa stood with the miners at the burying and thought about the one death the theft had cost. The town that began as a fever had a past now, and a dead man in it, and the past made the future heavier and truer both.

The tents were gone by autumn. The three saloons stood where the shanties had been, the Nugget loud, El Refugio warm, the Last Water quiet and meaner, and between them the town had become a place that expected to be here next year, and the year after. Rosa walked her rounds at night and heard the sound of it, not the hammers of the fever years, but the slower sound of a town settling, doors latched, windows lit, a place with a future and a past and a law that held the line between them.

She was uncomfortable with the size of it. She told Grant so, one evening, standing in the door of the office looking out at the lamps coming on along the main street.

"I didn't set out to make a town," she said. "I set out to keep a canyon from tearing itself apart. And now look."

"Look," Grant said. "And you did it with a badge you didn't ask for and a rifle that was your father's."

"It's a lot of people to be responsible for," she said.

"That's the size of the thing," Grant said. "That's what a law is. It grows with the town, and you've grown into yours."

Rosa stood a long time in the door, the canyon red in the last light beyond the lamps, and she did not know if she was big enough for the town she had helped build. But she knew she would hold the line, because that was what she had always done, and the town counted on it now, and the canyon remembered the whole of it.

Chapter 31: The Saloons

The Nugget was loudest, and the loudness had not changed, but the weight of it had. A year of honest weighing had done that. The men's money was their own now, spent over card tables and bars without the old urgency, the old reach for a bottle that said nothing and took everything. Rosa stood a moment in the doorway, the dust of the street settling behind her, and let the noise wash by her like weather.

Cousin Jack Trelawney called her over and showed her a knife that had been pulled and put away without blood. "First time I've seen a blade go back in its sheath because a woman walked in," he said. She said the peace was the sheath. He laughed, which was the closest Cornish to an apology, and the table settled.

El Refugio was the second stop, and here she was not watched. Doña Elvira poured her the coffee without being asked, and the Spanish voices closed around her like the walls of the canyon after rain. Mateo was there, her brother, tall against the bar, and he raised his cup to her — no pride in it, just the old loyalty of the herds, pleased and wary at once. She stayed for the one cup. It was her home ground, and she let it warm her before she went out again into the cold of the last stop.

The Last Water had not gone respectable. The new town had built its schoolhouse and its graveyard, but the last water at the edge of town still kept the desperate. The floor was sanded by boots and the light was low. Rosa stood at the bar and ordered nothing, only looked around her until the room remembered it was watched. Two men who had been counting a debt in their heads found reasons to be elsewhere. She did not draw; she rarely drew. The peace was in the watching.

Out in the street the moon stood over the red walls, and the wind came down out of the narrows with its voice. She walked the length of the town with the dust under her boots, and behind her the three saloons hummed, the town's three hearts beating steadier now than they ever had. Steadier because of her, she knew. That was a weight she had not asked for. It was the one she carried anyway.

Chapter 32: The Law, Owned

She did not go straight to the office. The night was warm and the dust of the day hung in the street, golden in the lamplight from the Nugget's windows, and Rosa walked the town's whole length before she turned back, the way a herder walks a fence at the end of a season to see it whole.

Past the schoolhouse, the raw lumber still pale among the older frames. Past the graveyard wall they had raised for Hob Barker, the stone set square, the dust already wearing it soft. The town had a past now, and a future, and both of them were laid out along this street like a hand of cards she had somehow been dealt.

She had been thinking about the law for three months — since the trial, since the copper came out of the hides, since Grant had begun, quietly, soberly, to prepare a thing he would not yet say. The county had sent him to keep a lid on a mine town. That was not the law. The company had wanted the law convenient, and for a year it had been. But she had walked a man out of the narrows in his own confession and set the books right, and she understood, standing in the dust of her own street, that the law was no longer the company's convenience or the county's patience. It was hers. Her choices, her canyon, her name on it. The badge meant what she made it mean, and she had been making it mean something all year without once asking if she wanted the making.

The wind came down out of the narrows, dry and warm, and it smelled of creosote and dust, the rain smell still waiting in the dark even though no rain had come. She remembered the arithmetic that had broken the theft open — hide weights, wagon weights, the depth of ruts — and how the canyon had given her the evidence, the way it gave a herder his trail. Dust was the land's memory. She read it. And the land had never once lied to her.

She turned back toward the office and saw the lamp was lit in Grant's window, burning steady at this hour for the first time in months, and she knew before she reached the door that something had come to a point. She stood a moment on the step, the star cold against her shirt under the vest, and then she went in.

Chapter 33: Sheriff Grant

The office smelled of dust and paper and, faintly, of the man. Grant sat behind his desk with his hands flat on the wood, and the bottle that had lived in the bottom drawer for a year was gone, and the drawer hung open and empty. Rosa took the chair across from him without being asked. For a long while neither spoke. The wind worked at the doorframe; the lamp flame stood straight in the still air.

"I've been sober a week," Grant said. "First week in three years. It's a terrible long week." He said it without humor, and that was the truest thing he had said in a year.

He told her what he was. A good lawman once, in the older counties, before the booms wore him down — a man who had known the names of his roads and the men who used them, before the world started arriving in trains and outrunning him. He had come with the strike because the county needed a body to pin a star on, and he had meant to keep the lid on, and that had been the whole of his law. Not to make the town a place. Just to keep it from boiling over.

"The kindest lawman you'll ever meet," he said, "and the laziest. I never cheated a man, Rosa, and I never caught one either. I let the canyon keep its own peace and called it mine."

She did not argue. It was true, and he needed her to let it be true. He had chosen her for deputy because he understood the one true thing about canyon law — that the law must know the canyon. She had known it all her life and never needed to be told. That had been the one good lawman in him left alive, and he had spent it on her.

"The kindest," he said again, "and the best thing I ever did was pick you. Not because I'm good. Because I could see the canyon had already picked you, and I had the sense to step aside."

Outside, the moon was down and the walls of the canyon were black against the stars. The wind in the narrows sang its long note, the sound the old herders said was the canyon singing to itself. Rosa listened to it through the door and felt the whole weight of the coming thing settle on her like a coat she had been handed.

Grant opened the top drawer and took out the star — his own, the county's star, the one she had worn as deputy — and laid it on the desk between them. "Tomorrow," he said. "I want it done in daylight, in front of the town, the way a law should pass. You take it, and I ride out the way I came."

The lamp flame bent and steadied. The canyon sang. Rosa looked at the star on the wood and knew she had already answered, a year ago, on a sheep trail, before she ever knew the question was being asked.

Chapter 34: The Retirement

The town came. That was the first surprise, and Rosa carried it with her through the whole morning — the town came, and stood in the street before the office, miners and herders and shopkeepers, the men from the Nugget and the women from the boarding houses, the whole of Cobre gathered in the dust as if for a hanging, except that no one looked hungry for blood.

Grant stood at the office door with his hands at his sides, and he was sober, and his hand did not shake. He said few words. That was his way of being honest: he said that the law in a canyon must know the canyon, and that he had ridden in not knowing it, and that he was riding out having at least had the sense to name a lawman the canyon had already raised. Then he took the star from his vest and pinned it to Rosa's shirt, over her heart, and the metal was warm from his hand.

She had thought she would feel something vast — the canyon handed over, the weight of it — but what she felt was smaller and stranger: the star was hers, and it fit her the way her father's rifle fit her hands, the way her mare's back fit her seat. She had worn it as deputy for a year, and the only change was the word under it. Grant did not say sheriff. He said her name, once, and stepped back.

Doña Elvira called out from the crowd — "¡Que viva!" — and the canyon caught the cry and threw it back, and then the whole street was cheering, and Rosa stood in the middle of it with the dust settling gold in the morning light, and for a moment she did not know what to do with her hands.

Grant shook her hand, once, hard, and then he went to his horse, a patient roan that had stood at the hitching rail all morning with the patience of a man who knew his riding days were ending. He swung up and turned the horse down the street toward the road to Arroyo Seco, toward the railhead and the county beyond, the way he had come. At the edge of town he reined in, and Rosa thought he would look back. He did not look back. He raised one hand — a gesture that might have been a wave, or a blessing, or a man checking that the sun was where it should be — and he rode into the mouth of the canyon, small against the red walls, and the walls closed behind him.

The crowd was still there. Rosa turned to face them, and then she turned to the office door, and the badge on her chest was warm and the canyon was hers, and she understood, with a steadiness that surprised her, that she had been holding it all along.

Chapter 35: The Badge

She did not sleep. That was not sorrow or fear; it was the old herder's habit of rising before the day, and on this first morning as sheriff she rose before the sun itself, dressed in the dark, and went to the office while the town still lay under its blanket of silence.

The office was Grant's no longer. She moved through it by the lamp, and the things in it were hers by right of use — the desk, the rifle rack, the wanted posters yellowing on the wall, the window that looked out onto the street where the dust lay undisturbed until the first wheel should cut it. She took the star from the desk where she had laid it, and she stood before the empty room and swore the oath to herself, quietly, in the Spanish her mother had given her, because it was the only tongue that seemed large enough for a thing she meant to keep.

...to uphold the law of the territory and the peace of this canyon, so help me God. The words went up into the dark of the office and hung there, and Rosa knew the oath was not to the county. It was to the canyon — to the narrows where her mother had died, and the spring that had never failed, and the green eye in the wall, and every herder's hoof-mark that had worn the sheep trails before there was a town to guard.

She unlocked the door at dawn. The light came over the red walls in a single wash, first pink, then gold, and the town woke around her — a door banged, a mule complained, the mine's whistle called the morning shift. She stood in the doorway with the star on her chest and watched the canyon fill with light, and it seemed to her that the two things, the badge and the canyon, fitted together like a hand in a glove she had worn all her life without knowing the glove was hers.

The bay mare nickered at the rail. Her father's rifle hung in its scabbard on the saddle. Rosa checked the street, the way she had always checked it, and then she swung up and rode the length of the town before breakfast, so that the day would begin with her having seen it all whole.

Chapter 36: Rosa's Office

The office was a room, but it had become a place. That was the difference, and Rosa had not set out to make it; it had happened to her the way a trail becomes a road, by being traveled. The desk sat square in the middle, scarred by Grant's boots and her own. The wanted posters yellowed on the wall — the same faces, year after year, none of them ever passing through Cobre. The rifle rack held her father's rifle and a shotgun the county had sent and no one had ever loaded. The door stood open onto the street, and through it came the dust and the sun and the sounds of the town, so that the office was never truly indoors.

She kept it the way she kept a camp: bare, swept, everything in its place. The first man to call her Sheriff without irony was Jack Trelawney, who came in one morning with a complaint about a water right and used the word as though it had always been hers, no pause before it, no smile after. She settled the water right the way she settled everything — by riding out and looking at the ground. The two ditches. The grade. The way the water ran. She had learned to read a slope before she learned to read a book, and the ground told her who had dug where, and she split the water fairly, and both men left grumbling equally, which was the closest thing to justice a town like Cobre could feel in its bones.

After that the office became the town's hearthstone of order. Men came in to sit, to complain, to be told the law and to be told no. She had no jail to speak of, only the one room behind the office with the barred window, and it was empty nine days out of ten, which was the way she liked it. A jail full of men meant the law had failed somewhere upstream.

One evening Doña Elvira came in with a basket — bread, cheese, a skin of wine — and looked around the bare room and said, in Spanish, that a law's house should have a fire. Rosa laughed, and for a week afterward there was a pot of coffee always on, and the office smelled of it, and men came by the smell the way they came to the saloons, only here the talk was of the town's business, and the peace held like a wall.

Outside the door the dust lay golden in the long light, and the canyon stood red and patient above the rooftops, and Rosa sat at her desk with the town's business in a neat pile before her and thought, without vanity, that she had become what the canyon had always meant to raise.

Chapter 37: The Herders' Thanks

The lambing ended with the canyon full of bells. That was how the sheep people marked it — not with a day on a calendar but with the flocks moving down through the narrows in the long light, ewes and lambs together, the air carrying the soft racket of the small bells the herders hung on the leaders. Rosa heard them from the office and went out, and the dust of the moving herds was gold in the air, and she stood a long moment breathing the old smell of wool and dust and dung, knowing that no matter what the town became, this was the smell it was built on.

That evening the sheep people gathered at El Refugio. Doña Elvira had thrown the doors wide and the cantina was full of Spanish voices and the smell of meat and fire, and Rosa came in her badge to find them all there — the Ortegas, Cisco and Manuel, tall and grave and smiling at her; Lupe, her father's name restored, her dark eyes steady; Mateo and her father Benito at the bar; and all the herder families she had grown up among, the canyon's first nation of work.

There was a feast. There was a blessing, said by the oldest herder, a man Rosa had known since she was a girl, his voice cracked and certain, his hands raised over the table. And then Doña Elvira stood and raised her cup and made the toast that would be repeated in Cobre for years: that the canyon had raised a lawkeeper, and the lawkeeper had kept the canyon, and the two could not be parted now if they tried.

Benito came to her then, through the crowd, and he was not a man of many words, but he had made her a gift with his own hands — a bridle of braided rawhide, worked thin and tight and even, the way he had braided hides on the trails for forty years. He held it out to her, and his weak eye watered, and he said only, "For the mare, and for the law." She took it, and her hands knew the feel of his work before her eyes could tell her what it was, and she could not speak.

Lupe came after, quiet, and took Rosa's hands in her own. "The wagons carry only wool now," she said. "The hides are honest again. You gave us that." Her thanks was the hardest thing Rosa had heard in a year, and she held Lupe's hands and said nothing, because anything she could say would be smaller than the moment.

The fire burned in El Refugio, and the canyon outside stood dark and singing in the wind, and Rosa sat among her people with the bridle in her lap and her heart full past speaking.

Chapter 38: The Copper, Clean

The weigh was done in the open now. That was the difference the trial had made, and Rosa liked to watch it, because it was the whole of the new order in one small ceremony. The green ore came up out of the shaft in the buckets and was sorted on the platform in the daylight, in front of the weighmaster and any man who cared to stand and watch, and the weights were read aloud and written into a book that any man could open. The mine had learned that secrecy was what had let the copper rot, and it kept no secrets now.

She stood at the edge of the platform on a day of high sun and watched the men work, the green and black of the malachite and chalcocite catching the light, the dust hanging gold over everything. Cousin Jack came up beside her and watched with her, and after a while he said, "Clean weights, clean money, clean men. A year ago I'd have called it a dream. You'd have called it... the way things ought to be."

"It's the way the canyon does things," she said. "Out in the open, where the ground can see you."

The copper went into the wagon bed and the tarpaulin went over it and the wagon pulled out toward Arroyo Seco, and Rosa watched it go without a flicker of the old unease. The road was honest now. The ruts it cut were the ruts of a fair load. She thought of the hides that had ridden that road full of copper, of the man in the territorial prison who had put them there, and she did not wish him worse than he had; the canyon did not hold grudges, only records.

She rode up the canyon that evening, past the mine gates and up the old trail to where the green eye lay in the upper wall — the malachite outcrop the herders had known for generations as the place the sheep would not drink. She sat her mare and looked at it a long while, the green fire in the red rock, and she understood that the fever had gone out of it. It was only ore again — the canyon's green eye, no longer a temptation but a fact, like the spring or the narrows or the wind. It had been there before the town, and it would be there after, and now the town could look at it without wanting to eat it whole.

The sun went down and the red walls darkened and the wind began its evening song. Rosa turned the mare toward home, and behind her the mine's whistle called the end of the day, and the copper in the wagon on the road to Arroyo Seco rode honest into the night.

Chapter 39: The Anniversary

She rode the ridge on the anniversary morning, up the old sheep trail before the sun was fully over the walls, and she remembered the day the world had arrived. It was a year to the day — one year since old Hugh Gantry had walked out of the canyon with the green rock in his hand, and the word had gone to the railhead and then to the world, and the first strangers had come up the sheep trail she had known since childhood.

From the ridge she could see all of it at once, the way a bird sees a canyon: the mine and the town at the mouth, the frames and shanties settled now into the shape of a place; the schoolhouse; the graveyard wall; the three saloons already waking; the road to Arroyo Seco cutting across the old sheep trails; and above it all the red walls, and in the upper wall the green eye, patient as ever, that had begun the whole fever with its glint in an old man's hand.

Benito had said, that first spring, that the sheep never drank at the green seep. The herders had known the green was there all along — they had just never wanted what it was. And Rosa, standing on the ridge a year later, understood that was the whole difference between the canyon and the town: the canyon had always known, and never cared, and the town had not known, and had cared too much.

She had thought the anniversary would bring her something — the closing of a circle, the proof of a year's work. What it brought her instead was the plain sight of her own rootedness. She was still here. A year after the world arrived, she was still here, the town's memory and its law, the only local in the law the way she had been the only local in the new town.

The sun climbed and the canyon filled with light and the wind came down out of the narrows with its old voice, the sound that had sung before her mother died and before the strike and before the first churro bell had ever rung in the canyon. The canyon did not care about the anniversary. It did not care about the town or the mine or the copper or the law. It was older than all of it, and it would outlast all of it, and that was not coldness — it was the one true thing, the thing she had built her law on.

Below her, far down, a lamb bleated, thin and new in the morning air. Rosa touched the badge on her chest and turned the mare toward home, and the first flocks were already stirring toward the narrows.

Chapter 40: The Sheep, Again

The herds came down the canyon in the spring, and the bells came with them. Rosa heard them from the office — the small bells first, a rumor in the wind, then the fuller sound, the clatter and the bleating and the soft thunder of hooves on the old trail — and she went out to the street to watch the canyon come home.

They came down through the narrows and past the mine gates, the ewes and the lambs and the leaders with their bells, the dust of them rising gold into the morning light. The sheep walked through the new town as they had walked through the old silence — they did not know the town was new, and the town had the grace, for once, to stand aside and let them pass. Miners stopped at the hoist to watch. Women came to the doors. Children ran alongside the flock and were shouted at, gently, in Spanish, by the herders.

Mateo drove them, tall in the saddle, and Benito rode the flank with his weak eye squinted against the dust, and they were the same men who had driven the flocks before the strike, before the town, before the world had found the canyon. The sheep did not care about the copper. The sheep did not care about the fever or the theft or the trial or the law. They cared about grass and water and the way home, and they were the canyon's oldest truth, older than the mine, older than the town, older than the green eye that had brought it all.

Rosa swung up on the bay mare and rode out to meet them, and Mateo raised a hand and grinned, and Benito nodded once, gravely, the way he had greeted her on a hundred mornings on the trails. She fell in with them, the badge above a herder's saddle, and rode a while beside the flock, down the main street of the town that had grown up in their pasture.

A miner called out something about the sheriff herding sheep, and there was laughter, not unkind, and Rosa laughed too, because it was true and it did not shame her. The law and the flock, the new town and the old trail — the canyon held them all. The sheep passed the mouth of the narrows and the bells faded into the canyon's length, and Rosa rode with them until the town was small behind her and the red walls closed around them all, and she was, as she had always been, at home.

Chapter 41: The Winter

The first snow came down the canyon in the night, and the red walls stood up out of it like the walls of a drowned church.

By morning the town of Cobre was a town under a white lid. The snow lay deep in the wagon road, in the ruts that had carried the copper to Arroyo Seco, and the mine's whistle sounded through the muffling air like a voice calling from the bottom of a well. The hoist was still. The loading platform was a white shelf. The men of the bunkhouses kept their fires and their talk low, and the Nugget's piano came through the walls as a soft, far thump. Rosa made her round in the gray light with her coat collar up and her father's old rifle wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and the whole canyon was a held breath.

She ended her round at the adobe. The fire was banked and low, and Benito sat in his chair by the hearth with his weak eye toward the flames, and the smell of the stew was the smell of every winter of her childhood.

He told the old stories that night, the way he did every winter when the snow shut the canyon in on itself. The lambing the wolves took. The year the spring ran dry. The summer pasture beyond the second ridge, where the green eye's seep made the sheep refuse to drink — the story of how the canyon had named itself before the town ever raised a saloon. Rosa sat on the floor with her back to the hearth and listened, and the year's accounting passed between them without being spoken: the trial, the sentence, the recovered copper, the mine kept honest, the badge she had laid on the table.

"You stayed," Benito said at last, not looking at her. "I thought, when the fever came, the town might carry you off with it."

"The town is mine now," Rosa said. "That is what the badge means. It means I stayed."

He said nothing. In the firelight his face was all the canyons of his life, and she knew he understood her better than she understood herself.

Outside, the snow fell on the narrows, on la cueva above them, on el ojo del agua where the water still ran under its white lid. The canyon slept. For the first time since the fever came, Rosa slept with it.

Winter, she thought, was the canyon's way of keeping the memory safe until the spring. And when the snow let her, she would go up the old trail to the narrows, to her mother's place, and she would not put it off any longer.

Chapter 42: The Trail

The snow held for three days, and on the fourth the wind came down out of the north and scoured the canyon clean enough to ride.

Rosa saddled the bay mare in the gray dawn and took the old sheep trail out of the adobe yard — the trail that had been there before the wagon road cut across it, before the town, before the mine; the trail her mother had walked with Rosa's hand in hers; the trail that was the family's first house. She rode slow, letting the mare pick her footing on the frozen ruts where generations of herders and generations of sheep had worn the rock smooth. The walls rose red above the snow, and the dust, frozen, lay in the trail like a record gone white.

She had been putting this ride off since the snow. She had been putting it off longer than that — through a year of fever, and through every spring when she had laid her flowers by the spring's edge instead of here, where the water had taken her.

The narrows closed around her at midday. The wind came down them thin and cold, and it sounded like a voice, and she stopped the mare and sat in the saddle a long time, hearing it.

She dismounted where the walls came closest, where the rock narrowed to a knife's width and the water had run on the day, seventeen years ago, when the flash flood came down the canyon with no warning. The body had never been found. Rosa had known that as a child, and it had been the hardest knowledge of her life — that a woman could be held by a place and never given back.

She had brought the flowers from the adobe's garden, dried and tied with a length of old bridle leather. She knelt in the snow at the foot of the red wall and laid them where the water would find them when it ran again, and she spoke her mother's name aloud for the first time in her life.

"Serafina," she said. "I am the sheriff of the canyon now. I think you would have laughed to see it."

The wind in the narrows answered nothing and everything. She stayed until the light went gray, then mounted and rode home by the way she had come, and the trail was the same trail, and it was not the same, because she had walked it as a girl and she rode it now as a woman who had buried her mother at last in the only grave the canyon would allow.

The canyon, she understood, had been holding the name for her all along — waiting, patient as water, for her to be ready to take it back.

Chapter 43: The Memory

The thaw brought the town out of its houses, and the town had questions.

It began with the schoolmaster's boy, who came to the office with a slate and asked Rosa the name of the peak that stood over the canyon's mouth. She told him. Then his mother came, wanting to know what the town had stood on before the frame buildings — what had been there, what the ground remembered. Then the assayer's new clerk, up from the railhead, asked her about the herders' year, the lambing and the summer pasture and the fall drive, as if it were a story out of a book instead of the life she had lived.

Rosa found she could answer them all. She knew the names of the peaks, every one her father had given her on the trails, and the springs that ran in the dry years, and which flats flooded, and which arroyos ran water in August. She knew where the sheep would not drink, and why the canyon had been called del Cobre before a single pound of ore had been sold — because the green eye had been green in the wall since before her grandmother's grandmother. The town had been built in one season on ground that remembered a thousand seasons, and Rosa was the only one in it who could say what the ground remembered.

She told the stories the way Benito had told them to her, plain and slow, letting the land do its own work. She told them in the office and at El Refugio, in English and in Spanish, to miners and herders and the schoolmaster's boy. And as she spoke she understood something that settled in her like a stone in a streambed: the canyon's memory did not live in the canyon. It lived in her. The town would forget the first spring in a generation, forget the fever and the trial and the names of the peaks, unless there was one who kept them.

A town without memory was only a camp. She had seen camps — they came up in a season and blew away in a season, leaving nothing behind but the dust and the marks of their passing.

Rosa locked the office that night with the old names warm in her head, and she thought she would keep them. It was a lawkeeper's work she had not asked for, but the canyon had given it to her the way it gave her everything, and she meant to hold it.

The snow had gone from the lower trail. In the spring, the old life would come down the canyon past the mine gates, and she would be there to name it.

Chapter 44: The New Miners

They came up the wagon road in the first warm week, before the grass turned green — a second year of men, off the railhead at Arroyo Seco, with their picks and their bedrolls and their talk of other strikes in other canyons.

Rosa stood in the door of the office and watched them come. They were the same men as the first year, or close enough — the same boots, the same dust, the same hunger behind eyes that had not yet learned the canyon. They had heard the copper was clean now, the weights paid true, the company honest. They had heard there was a woman sheriff in the canyon who read tracks the way a priest reads scripture, and some of them said it with a smile and some with a challenge, and all of them watched her as they rode past, to see what the law looked like when it had a woman's face.

She greeted them the way she had greeted the first year's men, with the same words and the same eyes. Her word was plain, and her eyes told them the truth: the town's door was open, the saloons were open, the mine was hiring — and the law was fixed. She did not have to say it twice. A newcomer with a reputation and a revolver that hung too easy on his hip tested her inside a week, loud in the Nugget over a card debt, and Rosa walked him to the edge of town with her hand off her rifle and her voice low, and he was not seen in the canyon again.

"The old ones left," Jack Trelawney said to her at the hoist, watching the dust settle on the road out. "The new ones learn the canyon or they leave it. You do not bend the canyon, Sheriff. You learn its name."

"Or it learns yours," Rosa said.

He laughed, and it was a good sound, the sound of a man who had chosen to stay.

That night she made her round past the three saloons — the Nugget loud, El Refugio warm with Spanish voices, the Last Water mean and quiet at the edge of town — and the canyon lay dark and red around the lights, patient as ever. A second year of men, and the town's door still open, and the law still fixed.

Rosa thought the canyon could hold them all, if they would let it teach them. The ones who would not, it would teach them anyway, the way it taught everyone — and send them on down the road, lighter than they came.

Chapter 45: The Peace

The peace held the way a good rope holds — not by being watched, but by being true.

The fights that came to Rosa that spring were the small kind: a claim line argued over in the dust, a mule stolen and returned, a wedding at El Refugio that ended in a fistfight between two cousins and a general reconciliation over Doña Elvira's mescal. She settled them with a word and a look, and her rifle stayed in its scabbard from the first thaw to the last frost, and no man in the canyon could say when he had last seen her draw it.

The weigh was honest now. She watched it sometimes, standing back in the shade of the loading platform while the clerk weighed the green and black ore in the open daylight, the slip written where every man who wanted could see it. The copper left the canyon at a fair price, the men were paid true weights, and the fever that had been in the town's eyes was gone. It had gone out of the copper when the copper was returned, and the canyon's green eye had become only what it had always been: a green seam in the red rock.

The town had learned to trust the law the way it trusted the weather. It did not ask the law to prove itself every morning. A man drunk in the street would see Rosa coming and straighten his coat and walk himself home, and that was the whole of it. She was not feared the way the old county lawmen had been feared — she was expected, the way the sun was expected, and a town that expected its law did not need to see it drawn to know it was there.

The herders came down the canyon with the sheep, the bells ringing, and the sheep walked through the new town as they had walked through the old silence, and no man threw a stone, and no dog was set on them. Mateo rode at the tail of the flock and raised a hand to Rosa, and she raised hers back, and the badge on her chest was not between them.

She made her round in the long red evening, the dust gold in the low light, and she thought this was what she had been trying to keep all along, though she had not had the words for it then.

The peace was not a thing to be guarded. It was a thing to be lived, and the town was learning to live it — and the canyon, patient, held the whole of it in its hands.

Chapter 46: The Canyon's Song

In the evening of a still day, when the town was quiet and the mine whistle had done for the day, Rosa took the old trail up into the narrows to hear the canyon sing.

The old herders had said the wind in the narrows was the canyon singing to itself — a low, hollow sound that came and went with the light, no louder than a man's breathing, but there, always there, if you stopped to listen. Her mother had believed it. Serafina had taken Rosa up to the narrows at dusk and made her be still until the song came, and Rosa, a child, had thought the song was a thing that had to be called out, that only certain people could hear.

She sat on a shelf of the red rock now, her back against the warm stone, and let the evening come down. The wind rose with the dark, thin and cold, and the narrows began to sing — the same low hollow sound, coming and going, like a voice speaking to itself in a language with no words. The song had not changed. It had been singing when her mother listened to it, and when her grandmother listened to it, and when the canyon was only sheep trails and sheep bells and the spring at the head of it. It had been singing through the fever and the trial, through the theft and the lie of the hides, and through the long winter while the snow lay white on the red walls.

She had changed. The child who had thought the song had to be called out had grown into a woman who knew better — the song had never once stopped, had never waited to be heard, had never cared whether there was anyone in the narrows to hear it. It was the canyon's own voice, and it had been there all along.

Rosa sat in the narrows until the stars came, and the song rose and fell around her like a breath, and she thought that was the whole truth of the canyon, if a person wanted it: it did not sing to be heard. It sang because it was the canyon, and it would sing whether the town stood at its mouth or the sheep alone walked its trails.

She walked home down the dark trail with the song still in her ears, and she did not feel small. She felt held — the way the canyon had always held its own, singing to itself, and to her, and never needing to know she was there to keep on.

Chapter 47: The Sun

There came a day of sun that spring when the canyon seemed to be made of light.

Rosa had seen the red walls a thousand times, in a thousand lights — the gray of winter, the white of snow, the purple of dusk — but this day was different. The sun came up clean and the air was still, and the whole canyon lit like a room with its shutters thrown open. The copper seam in the upper wall, the green eye the herders had known for generations, caught the light and burned green fire in the red rock, and the dust that hung above the wagon road stood gold in the sunbeams, so that the road seemed paved with light.

She rode the ridge that day for the plain pleasure of it, and the bay mare stepped high, and Rosa tried to see the canyon the way a stranger would see it, and could not, because she knew it too well — she knew the name of every peak and the lie of every trail, and the green fire in the wall was her people's old knowledge, not a wonder but a fact of her blood.

And yet. She reined in on the ridge and looked down at the mine and the town and the sheep trails and the spring, all of it in the one light, and she understood that the true wealth of the canyon had never been the ore.

The ore could be mined and weighed and sold and stolen. Men had killed for it and lied for it, and the canyon had held the lie until the truth came out of the hides. But the light on the copper rock could not be weighed. The green fire in the red walls could not be assayed. The dust turned to gold in the evening air could not be shipped to any railhead, could not be hidden in any hide, could not be taken by any thief.

This was the wealth that could not be stolen. This was the wealth that did not rust or deplete or run out — the one thing the fever had never once touched, because the fever had never once seen it.

Rosa sat the mare on the ridge until the sun went down and the green fire in the wall went out and the dust settled back into the road, and she thought the canyon had given her everything it had: the copper, the law, the memory, the peace.

And the sun, on top of it all — the sun that had been there before the strike, and would be there long after the last wagon rolled out of the canyon — the sun that could not be stolen, could not be weighed, could not be held in any man's hand, and was hers, and was every living thing's, and was enough.

Chapter 48: The Dust

The dust of the wagon road was the canyon's memory, and Rosa had read it all her life.

It was the same dust her mother's sheep had walked through, generations of hooves wearing the trail into the rock; the same dust the first strangers had raised when they came up the sheep trail with the word of the strike; the same dust the mule wagons had raised on the road to Arroyo Seco, heavy with ore and, for a season, heavy with the lie of the hides. Every passing had left its mark in it — hoof, wheel, foot, hide — and the dust had kept the record and given it back to anyone who knew how to read. Rosa had learned dust before she had learned letters, and she had never once found the dust lying to her.

She stopped on the road one evening on her way home, dismounted, and knelt in the deep dust of the wheel ruts. She let a handful run through her fingers and watched it fall, fine and red, and she thought about everything the dust had told her across a year and a season: the depth of a rut that meant a wagon was heavier than it should be, the print of a boot that did not belong, the place where a wagon had left the road and come back lighter. It had named the thief for her before any man's confession did. It had witnessed the trial. It had recorded the clearing of the herders and the return of the copper, and it would record whatever came next, whether she was there to see it or not.

Dust, she thought, was what they all came to — the miners and the herders and the law and the thief, the town and the sheep and the mules. The canyon had stood long before any of them and would stand long after, and the dust would hold the memory of all their passing, patient and true, the way it had held the memory of her mother, of the flood, of the spring that had never stopped running.

She let the last of the handful fall and brushed her hands on her trousers, and she did not feel small in the canyon's long accounting. She felt what a herder felt at the end of a good year — that she had been part of it, that her mark was in the dust beside her mother's and her father's and the sheep's, that she had done the work she had been given.

Rosa mounted and rode the last mile home in the red evening, and the dust of the old road and the new road was one dust, and it remembered her.

Chapter 49: The Home

Rosa came home at dusk, and home was the adobe at the mouth of the canyon, where the fire was lit and her father's voice was answering some question she could not hear, and the smell of the stew came out to meet her before she reached the gate.

She tied the bay mare to the rail and stood a moment, not going in. It was the hour she loved best in the whole canyon's day — the light gone red and then gone gray, the town's lamps coming on one by one down below, the mine's whistle silent, and the walls of the canyon darkening around the small warm house the way a body darkens around a held light. She could see Benito through the window, one eye on the fire, his hands still from the day's work, and the sight of him was the sight of every homecoming of her life.

Inside, the stew was lamb and chile, the way her mother had made it, the way her father still made it on the nights she was home. He asked her about the day, and she told him the day — a claim dispute settled on the flat, a horse shod, the new miners learning the canyon's ways — and he listened the way he always listened, patient, asking the one question that mattered and letting the rest go.

The badge lay on the table between them. She had taken it off the way other women took off a hat, and it lay there in the lamplight, small and heavy, the star she had not asked for and had come to own. She looked at it, and then at the fire, and then out the window at the darkening canyon, and she understood something she had known all her life without having words for it: this was what the law was for.

Not the office. Not the star. Not the keeping of order in a town of a hundred men and three saloons. The law was for this — the adobe, the fire, the father at home, the trail outside that a woman could walk in the dark without fear, the canyon darkening around a town that had learned to sleep.

"The canyon is quiet," Benito said.

"It is," Rosa said. "And I mean to keep it so."

They ate in the warm light, and the stew was good, and the walls of the canyon stood dark and patient around the little house, and Rosa thought that a lawkeeper who had a home to come home to had something to keep — and that was the whole reason for the badge, and the whole reason for her.

She laid the star on the shelf by the door where it would be ready in the morning, and the trail outside ran down into the canyon's dark, and she was not afraid of it.

Chapter 50: The Copper Canyon

Rosa woke before the sun and rode up to the ridge to watch the canyon come into the light.

The bay mare knew the way in the dark. The old sheep trail rose through the switchbacks her father had shown her as a girl, past the place where the wagon road had cut across it and gone on anyway, older than the road and stronger for it, and came out at last on the long spine of the ridge above the canyon's mouth. Rosa reined in and sat the mare and waited, and the canyon lay below her in the gray dark, a bowl of shadow holding the town's few lights.

Then the sun came up, and the canyon came into its own.

The red walls caught the light first, top to bottom, and the green eye in the upper wall began to burn — the malachite catching the sun, green fire in the red rock, the canyon's copper, older than the mine, older than the town, older than the sheep, known to the herders for generations as the place the sheep would not drink. The light ran down into the bowl of the canyon and found the town of Cobre: the mine at the head of it, the hoist and the loading platform and the honest scales; the three saloons along the one street, the Nugget loudest, El Refugio warm, the Last Water quiet at the edge; the schoolhouse and the graveyard and the frame buildings where the tents had been. It found the sheep trails, the old ones cut across by the wagon road, and the spring at the canyon's head, el ojo del agua, the water's eye looking up at the sky, and the narrows where the walls closed and the wind sang, where her mother was held and her mother's name was spoken at last.

All of it, hers to keep.

She had seen the canyon in its fever, when the copper was a temptation and men's eyes went green with it. She had seen the canyon in its lie, when the hides covered the theft and the truth waited in the dust. She had seen the canyon in its peace, when the copper flowed clean and became only ore again, and the sheep came down the old trails past the mine gates with the bells ringing. Copper and sheep, fever and peace, lie and truth — the canyon held them all, the way it had always held them, patient, remembering, whole.

Rosa sat the mare on the ridge in the growing light, the badge on her chest, her father's rifle in its scabbard, the bay mare warm under her hands. The sun stood full on the green eye now, and the whole canyon burned, red and green and gold, and Rosa Aguilar, the canyon's memory and its law, sat small on the ridge above it all.

The copper had made her town and her grief and her name.

The canyon had made her.

And as the sun came full over the red walls, and the green eye of the canyon opened in the light, she turned the mare toward home, and the trail led down into the heart of it.

It was the copper canyon. It was hers. It was home.
