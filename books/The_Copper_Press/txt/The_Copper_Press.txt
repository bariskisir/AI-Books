The Copper Press
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Dying Master

Snow fell on the Leie all night, and by dawn the canal ran gray between white banks, the belfry a ghost in the falling weather. Margriet van der Meer sat on the stool beside Meester Jan's bed and watched him drown by degrees. The fever had taken him three days before, and now his face was the color of tallow, and the candle at his head guttered in the wind of his breathing.

He spoke in fragments, the way a worn forme gives up its letters one at a time. The press, he said. The floor. Then, with terrible clarity, the copper. His hand found her wrist — the one with the mole — and held it with a grip that surprised her.

"You know what is in the base," he said. It was not a question.

"I know there is a panel. You showed me once."

"More than the panel." His eyes went to the ceiling. "I never printed it. Twenty-five years, and I never dared." A cough bent him double; she wiped his mouth with a linen cloth that came away smelling of sickness. "The color is the truth, Margriet. Do you hear me? Black ink can lie. The color can only remember."

"Remember what?"

But he was gone into the fever again, murmuring names that were not Flemish, and roads that sounded like other countries. She laid his hand back on the oiled wool and listened to the press below creak in the dark like a living ribcage, and told herself that while the press breathed, he breathed.

Pieter came up at Matins with a bowl of barley broth she could not make him swallow. The journeyman stood in the doorway, cap in hand, saying nothing. She shook her head, and he went back down.

The bells of St. Nicholas rang the curfew, and the snow came again. Near midnight Jan's eyes opened, and he looked at her as if he saw her whole — the ink-stained hands, the lampblack under her nails, the girl he had taken in off the street five years before.

"The press is yours," he said. "The floor. The copper. Print what I was afraid to print."

His grip tightened once on her wrist, and then loosened, and the candle flared and sank, and the press below made one long creak, like a door closing, and was silent.

She sat a long time in the dark with his hand growing cold in hers. She did not weep; there was not room in her for weeping yet. And the first thing she felt, before the grief, was the shape of the key he had pressed into her palm.

Chapter 2: The Copper Press

The funeral was a wet affair in the February fog. The Guild of St. Luke turned out in its livery — painters, booksellers, illuminators, and behind them the new men of the press — and Meester Jan van den Bossche went down into the ground beside the church wall while the bells tolled and the rain fell without remark. Margriet stood at the head of the grave with a handful of wet earth, let it fall, and watched the sexton's spade begin its work.

The guildsmen filed past, grave and correct. Meester Hendrik van Coudenberghe, the dean, pressed her hand and said the right words about a good craftsman and a grief shared by the whole trade. She thanked him and did not believe a word of it.

Back in the shop, the rain had found the seams of the roof, and the buckets caught the drips in a rhythm like a slow heart. She bolted the door, lit no candle, and went to stand before the press in the gray light, to look at it whole.

It was a massive thing, a wine-press turned to a stranger purpose. The oak frame was dark with a generation of oil, bound at every joint with iron — and there, where no iron should be, plates of copper, bright and hammered thin, holding the frame together at crossbar and screw. Two brass pins stood in the tympan like sentinels. The platen hung above the coffin, patient as a blade. The machine smelled of linseed oil and lampblack and old paper — the scent she had woken to for five years — and faintly, of Jan.

She put her hand on the crossbar. The press gave a long, low creak, a breath drawn through oak, and the sound went through her like a note.

"Jan is dead," she told it. "He is in the ground, and it is raining on him."

The press said nothing. The rain dripped into the buckets.

She ran her fingers along the copper plates at the joints, and the copper was cold and smooth, strangely warm, as if it had kept a little of the day's heat. She thought about the word Jan had died speaking — copper — and about the panel in the base, and the key that lay heavy in her pocket as a sin.

Then her whole inheritance came down on her at once: no money, no family, no one in the world, and a press no woman in this city was permitted by law to hold. The guild would come for it by morning. She knew that the way she knew the seasons.

She stood with her hand on the cold copper and did not weep; weeping was a thing she could put off, like so much else.

Chapter 3: The Inheritance

The notary read the testament in the back room of the guildhall, and the words fell on the silence like stones. Shop, stock, tools, the press itself — all of it to Margriet van der Meer, his apprentice, for five years of faithful service, and because, the parchment said, she has the hands of a printer and the heart of one.

Meester Hendrik rose before the last word had settled. "This cannot stand." His voice was smooth, the voice of a man who had already decided. "A woman cannot hold a press in this city. The law is the law, whatever Meester Jan's fondness."

"He had no wife," said the notary. "No child. He made his choice."

"He was dying," said Hendrik. "The fever took his wits. She must sell, and sell to a printer of good standing. Or she must be made ward of a guildsman, who will hold the shop in trust until she marries. Willem here is a printer. A good one. He has made an offer."

Willem stood against the wall with his arms folded, a man of thirty with a craftsman's shoulders and a gaze that had followed her for two years. He had asked her already, as a kindness, as a bargain. She had refused him without needing to think about it.

"And if I marry no one," she said, "and sell to no one?"

"Then the law sues you out," said Hendrik pleasantly. "The press is seized, and the shop with it. It is nothing personal. It is the order of things."

The order of things. Her mother had used that phrase once, kneeling in the Beguinage garden, before the plague took her. The order of things, she had said, was not the same as the order of God.

"I will consider my choices," Margriet said, and heard how steady her voice was, and was glad of it.

She walked home through the cold rain with the notary's paper folded in her bodice. Pieter met her at the door with a cup of small beer and his wordless look, and did not ask what the guild had said. When he had gone down to the cellar, she took the key from the bottom of her pocket.

It was small, warm from her body, its shaft worn bright. It had lain in Jan's fist the night he died; he had pressed it into her palm with the last of his strength.

She went to the press and knelt. The base was a great oak box that carried the whole weight of the frame, and she had dusted its corners a hundred times without curiosity. Her fingers found the seam — a hairline, almost invisible — and she fitted the key into a lock she had never seen.

The panel swung open on oiled hinges, silent, like a mouth opening to speak.

Chapter 4: The Secret Plates

The cavity inside the base was lined with felt, and in it lay three flat bundles wrapped in oiled cloth, each the size of a man's two hands, bound with cord that had been knotted once and never untied.

Margriet sat back on her heels and listened. The shop was still; the rain had stopped; the only sound was the drip of the last water finding the buckets. She drew out the first bundle.

It was heavy beyond its size, and cold, and the weight of it was the weight of something that had waited a very long time. She undid the cord, and the candlelight caught a surface of copper, polished to a deep rose, and her breath stopped.

She had seen engraving before — the little devotional prints the pardoners sold. Nothing like this. The whole plate was alive with cut lines, fine as hair, so dense they seemed at first a texture. She held it to the candle and turned it, and the lines became a picture.

A river. A road that followed it. Hills she did not recognize, and beyond them more rivers, more roads, a whole country of dark lines branching like the veins of a leaf. And scattered along the roads, so small she almost missed them, tiny crosses no larger than the head of a pin.

She set it down and took out the second bundle, and the third. All three alike — copper, heavy, cold, covered with the same engraving, the same rivers, the same roads, the same small crosses — though the cut lines did not quite match, as if each plate held a different part of a single whole.

She polished the first plate with the heel of her palm, the way she polished the tympan before a run, and the rose deepened to a bright glow, and a green bloom of verdigris came off on her skin, and the smell of it, sharp and metallic, filled the space between her and the candle.

Something scratched at the door.

She froze, the plate in her hands, her breath held, and listened. A cat, most likely — Baes Katelijne's gray tom, which liked the warm press shed. The scratch came again, and then silence.

She did not put the plates back. She could not. She wrapped the first in its cloth and laid it in the cavity, and the second, and the third, and pushed the panel shut until the seam was invisible, and stood, her heart beating in her throat.

She did not know what she held. That was the truth of it. She knew only that her master had hidden it for twenty-five years, that he had spoken of it as he died, and that the Church, if it ever knew, would not leave it in the hands of a woman in a rented shop by the road to the Friday Market.

She blew out the candle and stood in the dark with her hands smelling of copper and verdigris, and did not sleep until the bells rang for Lauds.

Chapter 5: The Color

The pots were hidden where the ink was kept, behind a loose board — three earthenware pots sealed with wax, and a fourth, larger pot of oil. She opened the oil first, and the smell that came out was linseed, but cooked and thickened and bitter with resin. Then she broke the wax on the smallest pot and touched the paste inside with her finger, and the color that came up was the red of old blood.

Vermilion. She knew it from the illuminators' stalls on the Graslei — little jars of brilliant red worth more than a day's bread. The second pot gave up azurite, a blue like the deep water of the Leie at midsummer, and the third a gold — orpiment, glittering on her fingertip like a scaled thing.

Under the pots lay a folded sheet, the paper soft with handling. She read the hand of Meester Jan by candlelight:

Grind the pigment small into the oil varnish — linseed, cooked with mastic and a little white resin, until it strings from the spoon. Ink the plate. Wipe the face, so that only the cut lines hold the color. Lay the sheet against the two brass pins. Screw down, and draw the pass. Then the blue, then the gold, each against the pins, and the sheet comes away — he had underlined it twice — in register. The color is the truth. I have never printed it. I dare not. But it is true.

She chose the plate whose cut lines under the candle had looked like roads, and she did what the recipe said. She ground the pigment into the varnish until the oil took up the red, inked the plate with a leather ball, wiped the face with the heel of her palm, laid the paper against the pins, and screwed the platen down.

The press creaked — its breath — and came up.

The road was there, on the white paper, in bright vermilion, the cut lines holding the color like ink in a ditch, and where the little crosses were, the red deepened into points of blood. She cleaned the plate and pulled it, and against the same pins she laid the blue — and the rivers came, and the sea. And the gold — and the towns appeared, and the crosses burned like small suns.

Three passes. One sheet. In register. The first color print this press had ever made, and the one thing her master had been afraid to make.

Her hands were shaking. The sheet was beautiful and it was terrible, and she did not understand it — only that color had come off copper onto paper, true as a witness. She hid it under the floorboards before the fear could catch the wonder, and stood on it, and heard the press above still creaking softly, like a man breathing in his sleep.

Chapter 6: The Hidden Map

She could not leave it alone. All that week she printed the plates by candlelight after the shop closed, and each night she learned them a little better — which lines were roads, which were water, how the rivers bent and the roads followed. And each night she was left with the same unease: the sheet meant something, and she could not say what.

It was on the third night that she did the whole thing deliberately, the way she would set a forme — red first, then blue, then gold, each pass against the brass pins, the press creaking its one long breath each time — and when the gold came up and the platen rose, she lifted the sheet and saw it whole, and her hand went to her mouth.

It was a map.

Red: the routes, the great roads running down out of the north like the arteries of a body — the old roads of the cloth trade, of the pilgrims, and of something older. Blue: the rivers — the Rhône, the Meuse, the Scheldt, the canals — and at the bottom of the sheet a great blue sea with ships no larger than flecks of ink. Gold: the towns, the little houses — and there, along the red routes, at every third or fourth bend, a small gold cross, precise as a needle-prick, standing over a name.

A route of refuge. Someone had drawn the safe roads and the water, and marked the sheltering houses with crosses in gold.

She set the sheet aside and pulled the same plates with ordinary black ink, one pass, the way any printer would have done it. The result was a tangle — scratches, meaningless, a scribble of dark lines that could have been anything or nothing. The copper, in black, remembered nothing.

She held the two sheets side by side: the black nonsense and the colored map, the dead scratch and the living country. And the sentence her master had died speaking came back to her in his voice, as clearly as if he stood at her elbow.

Black ink can lie. The color can only remember.

She understood, then, what the press truly was. Not a machine for books. A thing for keeping what the world wanted lost. The lines were in the copper always — had been for twenty-five years, in the felt-lined dark of the press's base — and the copper remembered, whether or not anyone ever inked it and drew the color up out of the cuts.

She wrapped the map in oiled cloth and laid it in the cavity with the plates, and stood, and the candle guttered, and distantly the bells of St. Nicholas rang the curfew. She thought: this is what he was afraid to print. And she was afraid too, and hid it, and did not sleep, and the fear and the wonder sat in her side by side like two wolves at a fire.

Chapter 7: The Cathar Routes

The chest was in the corner of the shop where Jan had kept his ledgers, iron-bound and locked, and she had never once seen him open it. The key from his fist turned in it on the fifth try, and the lid rose, and the smell that came out was wool and old cedar and something else — paper, but paper that had lived a long life.

The chronicle was not a book as she knew books. It was a cloth — a long woven strip, folded into panels, worked through with wool and linen thread in a script both familiar and foreign, slanting and elegant, older than the Flemish of the shop. On a separate folded leaf, in a careful hand, someone had written out its meaning in Flemish.

She read the leaf first, by the window where the light was best.

It told of the Good Men — the Bonshommes — who had come up out of Languedoc in the time of her great-grandmothers, when the roads ran with rumor and the bales of cloth hid more than wool. They were weavers, some of them. They were readers. They were hunted. They had moved north along the cloth routes, by night and by the houses of those who would shelter them, and the houses were marked in the woven script with the names of looms and wool lofts and brewhouses.

And the names, in the middle panels, were struck through.

A line had been drawn across them — a single hard stroke, the mark of a pen that had been driven, not drawn. Margriet ran her finger along one of the struck-out names, and the line was pressed so deep into the cloth that it had cut some of the threads.

She had heard Jan's stories over the years — of weavers and burning, of books that went into the flames and men who went with them. She had thought them old men's terrors, half-dreamed. She understood now that they were not stories at all. They were the same story, and the map was its shape.

She fitted the names from the chronicle against the gold crosses she had printed — the surviving names, the ones the pen had not reached. The map was a route of refuge, the chronicle its key, and the struck-out names the ones the Church had erased and meant to keep erased — and the gold crosses remembered them anyway.

The copper remembered. That was the whole of the terror. The copper remembered what the ink of the clerks had tried to forget.

She locked the chest, put the key back in her pocket, and stood at the window a long time, watching the water run in the street. She did not yet know what she would do — only that the press in the middle of the shop had stopped being a machine, and had become a question.

Chapter 8: The Erased Century

There were letters in the chest too — a sheaf of them, tied with leather, the paper yellowed and the ink gone brown with age. Some were in Flemish, some in the foreign slanting script, some in Latin, and these she could read at the top of her breath, moving her lips over the words the way her mother had taught her to read the Office. Her mother, the Beguine, who had died of the plague when Margriet was twelve and had left her nothing but her Latin and her hunger for the written word.

The letters told the rest of it.

She learned the word Montségur, and the fortress that had fallen in her grandfathers' time — the last stronghold of the Good Men, the fire on the hill where they went up rather than renounce. She learned the word crusade, a crusade against Christians, which had seemed to her impossible, and which the letters said had happened anyway, with a count's sword and the Church's blessing. She learned the burnings, the decades of burnings, the last one in the lifetime of living men.

And then she learned the erasure.

The letters wrote of the striking-out, and described it without passion, as clerks describe their work. The names taken from the charters. The registers cleaned. The books of the Good Men gathered from lofts and wool bales and burned in the squares of a hundred towns, until the very memory of them was a rumor, and then not even that. The Church had spent a century making the Good Men dead, and then — the word the letters kept returning to — unremembered.

Dead, and unremembered. The strike of the pen where the fire had already been.

She sat back on her heels in the cold shop, the letters spread around her like a snowfall, and understood her master's fear at last. It had never been the copper. It had never been the process, the three pots, the brass pins. It was what the copper held — the map, the register of names, the memory the Church had spent a century erasing, kept alive in a cavity in the base of a wine-press that a frightened man had carried out of Cologne and never once dared to print.

And the gold crosses. She kept coming back to them, those needle-prick marks over the sheltering houses. The Church's clerks had struck out the names in the woven book. But the copper did not strike out. The copper could not forget. The gold crosses were the names the pen had tried to kill, still alive in metal, waiting for the oil and the paper and the hand that would draw them up out of the dark.

She understood, then, what Jan had been too afraid to do. It had a name, and the name was printing the truth.

The bells rang for None. She gathered the letters and the chronicle, put them back in the chest, and locked it, and stood in the middle of her dead master's shop, twenty-two years old, alone in the world, holding a secret that a century of the Church's care had failed to bury.

Chapter 9: The Bishop's Man

It was Pieter who told her, coming back from the market with the day's paper, his face puzzled under the rain.

"There's a clerk at the guildhall," he said. "From Tournai. The bishop's man. He's been asking questions all morning — asking after the press."

"After every press in the city, I suppose." She kept her voice even, reaching for the compositor's stick. "The new art. It was only a matter of time."

"No." Pieter shook his head. "Not every press. Yours. He asked for the press of Jan van den Bossche. He called it the copper press."

The word went through her like a cold needle. The copper was the thing you did not name — the plates, the panel, the secret her master had died with. And a clerk of the bishop of Tournai had used the name, in the guildhall, in the hearing of Meester Hendrik.

"He was still there when I left," Pieter said. "With the dean. Talking low. The dean did most of the talking."

Meester Hendrik. She saw the smooth face and folded hands of the dean, and heard his voice saying the order of things. If the Church wanted the copper press, Hendrik would help them want it. He was always useful to whoever was winning. A woman's shop was a small thing to trade for a bishop's goodwill.

She went to the guildhall herself, to hear it from the doorway, thirdhand, as she had to hear everything now. A printer she did not know told her the substance of it: the bishop's man had described the press by its fittings — its copper, its brass pins. He had asked who held it now. He had left a sealed letter for the dean, and the dean had promised his help in the matter, whatever the matter was.

She walked home in the rain with her hands in her sleeves and her mind racing. By the time she reached the shop she had decided: the press was broken. Unsaleable. Worthless. The screw would not turn true. The coffin was warped. A woman could not lawfully hold it anyway; what was a broken press to anyone?

That night, for the first time, she took the three plates from the base and hid them under the floorboards, beneath the loose board where the ink was kept, and lay awake counting the ways a secret could be found.

And she began, in her small room above the shop, to watch the door.

Chapter 10: The Inquisitor

He came on a Tuesday, in the last of the winter light, and she saw him through the window before he knocked — a Dominican in the black and white of the Order of Preachers, walking up the wet street with the unhurried step of a man who owned the time and expected the door to open.

It did. A clerk waited at the corner, and she marked the horse tethered beyond him. The friar's face was pleasant, the face of a man who smiled easily and meant it. Only his eyes were strange — the color of the sea in winter.

"Good day," he said. "I am Brother Koenraad, of the Order of Preachers, in the service of the bishop of Tournai. And you are Margriet van der Meer, who holds the shop of the late Meester Jan."

She held the door, neither opening it wider nor closing it. "I hold nothing. The shop is empty. The press is broken."

"I am sorry to hear it." He stepped forward, gently, as if invited, and the shop received him. He stood before the press and looked at it as a man looks at a horse he means to buy. "A fine press. Oak. A wine-press design, by the frame. My master saw such a press in Mainz once, long ago, that could print in red and blue and gold."

"I would not know," she said. "It does not run."

He put his hand on the crossbar — where she had laid her own hand the night of the funeral — and the press gave nothing. "Jan van den Bossche owed the Church a debt, in the matter of his master in Cologne. I came to see the press he was said to have carried with him. They call it, I believe, the copper press."

The copper press. He said it easily, in the middle of her shop, and smiled.

"It is broken," she said again. "There is nothing here."

He walked the whole shop with his hands clasped behind him, courteous, unhurried, and she stood in the center and watched him look, and the silence grew long, and she felt the floorboards under her feet grow heavy, as if the plates beneath them could feel his weight.

At the door he turned. "You are right. It is broken. I will report so." And he set on the counter a sheet of printed paper — a page of indulgences, the woodcut of a saint at the head, the Latin tight and black. "A gift," he said. "For your trouble. The Church remembers everything, my daughter. Even what is erased. Especially what is erased."

He smiled once more, and was gone, and the door clicked shut, and the shop was suddenly cold.

She stood a long time without moving. Then she picked up the indulgence page and looked at the black ink of the Church's printing, and thought of the copper under the floor, remembering what this black ink could not.

The bells rang for Vespers. She folded the page into her pocket, and did not sleep, and understood that whatever she was going to do with the color, she would have to do it soon, while the copper was still hers.

Chapter 11: The Guild

The guildhall of St. Luke smelled of wax, old varnish, and the damp wool of a dozen masters. Margriet stood at the foot of the long table, the stone floor cold through her shoes, and heard her name pass from mouth to mouth like a coin tested by biting.

Dean Hendrik van Coudenberghe read the claim twice, slowly, as if to make certain of it. The craft of printing, the claim said, fell under the guild's jurisdiction, being a thing of books and pictures. Therefore its holdings must stand in a master's name. A woman could not be a master. A woman could not hold a press. She must sell to a guildsman, or be made ward of one, or marry.

"Meester Willem has offered," Hendrik said, not unkindly. "A fair price for the shop, the stock, the press."

Willem sat at the table's end, arms folded, watching her with the look of a man counting out his own worth. She had seen that look before, in the shop, when he came to buy paper and stayed to measure the room with his eyes.

"I do not sell," she said. "I hold the press by my master's testament."

"A testament cannot give what the law forbids a woman to hold."

She had known it. Jan had known it. That was why he had left her the key, the plates, the color — not the law. The law was a wall, and walls were made to be worked around, like a forme gone crooked.

She looked down the table at the masters: the painters, the booksellers, the illuminators, men who had watched her grow up in her master's shop, who had bought her sheets and praised her register. None of them would meet her eye. The new art frightened them; it ate the work of the scribes, and a woman who set it free among them was a worse fear still.

"Then tell me this," she said, and her voice did not tremble, though her hands were ink-black and cold. "Who printed the first page in this city? And who remembers her name?"

The silence held. The candle flames leaned in the draft. Hendrik looked down at his hands.

"That is not the question before us," he said at last.

"No," she said. "It is not."

She walked out into the March wind, and the door closed behind her, and she heard the murmur begin before she had crossed the yard. She had bought herself time — a week, a month, as long as their fear of the Church's new order outweighed their fear of her. It would not be long. The color waited in the base of the press, and the press waited in the shop, and something in her had begun, quietly, to refuse to be the ward of anyone.

Chapter 12: Pieter

Pieter came back from Mechelen on a wet evening in March, the road dust still on his boots, and found the shop changed. The paper was stacked as it had been stacked, the press stood where the press had stood, and yet the air was different — taut, like a rope drawn for a bell. Margriet stood in the doorway with the lamp in her hand, and her face was not the face he had left.

"You were gone a long time," she said.

"A month. The roads were foul." He set down the crate of bound books. "There was talk on the boat — about the guild, about you."

She did not answer at once. She went to the press and touched the copper plate at its joint, and when she turned back she was holding something wrapped in oiled cloth. She laid it on the frame of the coffin and unfolded it, and he saw colors — red, blue, gold — the like of which he had only ever seen in the illuminators' shop, but this was printed, pressed flat into the paper, the lines sharp as an engraving.

"Jan's secret," she said. "The press prints in color. And the color is a map."

She told him the rest quickly, the words coming fast and low, as if speaking them aloud made them true and therefore dangerous: the plates, the three passes, the chronicle in the chest, the erased names. Pieter listened, and the fear rose in his chest like cold water, and he made himself stand still.

"In Mechelen," he said, "I saw the man who will kill you for this."

She looked up.

"There was a ceremony in the square. A Dominican. He burned a cartload of books — a formal burning, with incense and a clerk reading out the titles as the pages went into the fire. Letters of remission for those who brought books in. He did it calmly, the way a man prunes a tree."

"The bishop's man," she said. "His name is Koenraad."

"Then he has seen your face, and now he knows what your shop holds."

"He knows the press is copper. He does not know why."

Pieter looked at the color proof lying open on the coffin, red and blue and gold in the lamplight, beautiful as a window. He thought of the books burning in Mechelen, the pages curling, the names going up. He thought of the press creaking in the night like a living thing, and of Margriet's hands, and of what he would not say.

"Then we print it," he said. "The map. And an account. And we let the paper decide."

"You cannot unsay that," she said. "It is a death, if we are caught."

"I know," said Pieter. "I have been to Mechelen. I have seen what they do to paper. I know what they do to people." He drew his breath. "But if the paper burns either way, then let it burn for the truth."

She looked at him a long moment, and something in her face loosened, as a knot loosens.

"We learn the colors by candlelight," she said. "Night after night, until we can print it blind."

Chapter 13: The First Color Print

They waited for the curfew bell, then locked the shop and hung a shuttered lamp from the beam. Pieter banked the fire low so the room would not look lived in from the street. Margriet warmed the varnish in a little copper pot, stirring with a goose feather, watching it turn clear as honey. The smell of linseed oil filled the shop, rich and heavy, a smell the neighbors would know.

The proof she had shown Pieter had been pulled in fear, half by accident, in the weeks after Jan died. This one she would print with intent.

"The red first," she said. "Then blue. Gold last, because gold is the meaning."

Pieter ground the vermilion into the warm varnish with a stone, slow and patient, and she watched the color come alive under his hands — a red like new blood, like a bishop's seal. He inked the first plate, and she laid the paper on the coffin, brought it down against the two brass pins, and pulled the bar.

The press creaked — a breath, a heartbeat — and the red left the copper and lay flat and true on the page.

They worked through the passes without speaking. The blue azurite went down next, river-cold, and where it crossed the red it did not fight it but lay beside it, each color keeping its own country. Then the gold, the orpiment ground to a buttery brightness, pressed over the towns and the small crosses, and Margriet lifted the sheet free and carried it to the lamp.

It was whole — the whole map on one page: the red routes threading the blue rivers, the gold towns and the small gold crosses where the houses of refuge stood, the blue sea at the edge, the names. It looked alive, the way a thing looks alive when it is made true.

She stood with the sheet in her hands and did not breathe. Pieter looked over her shoulder, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the page did not already say.

"Sign it," he said at last.

She found her pen and signed, small and careful, in the lower corner: M.v.d.M. Her hand, her truth, inked over the gold of a town she would never see. The press creaked once, softly, as if it had drawn breath.

"That is the first," she said. "It will not be the last."

And then they heard it — horses in the street, the ring of iron on wet cobbles, coming slow, coming toward the shop.

Pieter blew out the lamp. In the dark, by touch, they swept the sheet and the plates into the oiled cloth, under the floorboard, into the base of the press, her hands moving blind and sure, the way a woman moves in her own dark house. The knock came as the last plank settled.

They stood, breathing shallow, in a shop that was only a shop, with a cold press and a dead hearth and two who had been working by the light of nothing.

The knock came again.

"That's all right," she whispered to the press, to herself. "We have done it. Whatever they want now, they cannot undo it."

Chapter 14: The Risk

Koenraad came in the morning, in the pale rain, alone except for his clerk. He stood on the threshold and smiled his winter-sea smile and asked, most courteously, whether Meester Jan's shop might sell him a ream of good paper.

Margriet sold it to him. There was nothing else to do. She wrapped the quires and tied the cord, and all the while she felt his eyes moving over the room, over the press, over the closed shutter of the window where the lamp had burned all night.

"You keep a fine press," he said. "The carpenter's work is beyond the common. These copper fittings — I have heard of such a press. They say it was built in Cologne."

"Meester Jan bought it secondhand," she said. "I know nothing of its making."

"Ah." He walked a slow circle around it, and she saw him note the smell — linseed oil, fresh, not yet faded — and she saw him rest his hand on the platen, which still held the warmth of the night's work. He did not remark on it. He thanked her for the paper, paid in good groats, and left.

When the door closed, Pieter came out of the back room. He was white.

"He knows," Margriet said.

"He knows something. It is not the same."

They stood at the window and watched him cross the square, unhurried, the clerk at his heels. The rain fell on his shoulders. He did not look back.

"Count it with me," she said. "The possession of the plates is death. Printing them is the same death, faster. Hiding them is death if we are searched, and selling them is death with a quicker conscience." She turned from the window. "Who else knows?"

"Jan. And you. And now me."

"And Sister Beatrijs." Pieter's face did not change. "She is the only one in Ghent who can read the chronicle — the Occitan, the struck-out names. Without her we cannot read the map true."

"Then we cannot read it," he said. "We can print it without reading it."

"No." She shook her head. "The gold crosses must match the names. If we print the map wrong, we have printed a lie, and black ink lies enough."

They were silent. The rain rattled the shutter.

"Then you must go to the convent," he said, and his voice was level, "and ask the sister to read it. And you must tell her nothing about the color until she has said yes to the words."

"That is how it will have to be," she said. "She will see the chronicle as a puzzle of script, not a heresy. And the abbey is the one place in this city where the bishop's men do not go looking."

"Except they do," Pieter said. "They go everywhere."

"Then we go carefully." She looked at the press, at the copper plate at its joint. "I will go today, under cover of wanting a rubric for a Psalter. If I am not back by the bell, you know what to do with the plates."

He did not ask what she meant. They both knew.

Chapter 15: Sister Beatrijs

The Bijloke Abbey stood by the water, its walls dark with damp, its bell sounding the mid-morning office as Margriet crossed the bridge. A lay sister let her into the porch, where the air smelled of beeswax and wet stone, and took her request — a rubric for a Psalter, red initials, the illuminatrix's hand — into the cloister.

She waited a long time. She had brought a missal for cover, and she turned its pages without reading them, rehearsing her words, until a woman came through the inner door. She was small and pale, in the white habit of the Cistercians, with ink on the first finger of her right hand and eyes that had looked at too many lines to be fooled by a face.

"You wished a rubric," Sister Beatrijs said. It was not a question.

"A red initial for a Psalter," Margriet said. "My master always came to the convent for his colors."

"Your master." The sister looked at her. "Jan van den Bossche, who died. You are the girl who keeps his press."

"Yes."

"He bought his vermilion from us. He knew his reds. The convent buried him a little poorer and a little richer, as men are buried." She held out her hand. "Show me the manuscript you have not brought."

Margriet's blood went cold, and then still. She had the chronicle wrapped in her sleeve, the woven cloth soft against her arm, and she had planned to speak of it last, and carefully.

"I did not say I had a manuscript."

"You did not have to. You are not here for a rubric. You have the look of a woman carrying something under her arm that she is afraid of, and you have not once looked at the missal you brought. Show me."

There was no lie to be made, so Margriet drew out the wrapped book and laid it on the porch bench. The sister did not touch it at once. She looked at the binding, the cloth-woven cover, the way it was sewn — and then she put her hand on it, and her face changed.

"This is a weaver's book," she said quietly. "A book of remembrance. There are not many of these left." She opened it, and her finger ran along a line of Occitan, and then stopped at a struck-out name — the ink scored through it, twice, hard, the way a scribe strikes when he is angry or afraid. "Whoever did this had a strong hand and a clear conscience."

"Can you read it?"

"I can read it," said Sister Beatrijs, "and I can read what was put over it, and I can tell you the difference." She closed the book and held it to her chest a moment. "I will read it for you. But not here, in the porch, where any lay sister may come. Inside, in the scriptorium, where I can see the light. You will come back tomorrow at the hour of terce."

She gave the book back, and for a moment her hand closed over Margriet's, cold and sure.

"And you will tell me, then, why a printer needs to read the names of the Good Men."

Chapter 16: The Convent

The scriptorium at terce was cold and bright. Winter light came through the east windows in long slanted panes, and it fell on the desks, the trimmed quills, the pots of ink, and on the two women bent over a book no one in that room was meant to see.

Sister Beatrijs read aloud in a low voice, the Occitan coming off her tongue like water off a roof, and Margriet listened to the names as if to a bell tolling. The chronicle told of weavers, of looms, of cloth bales carried north; it told of travelers who came at dusk with a password and left at dawn, fed and hidden; it told of the Good Men, the Bonshommes, who carried their books in the warp of the cloth itself.

"Here," said the sister, and her finger stopped. "A name, not struck out. Maerten van der Gracht, weaver of Ghent. He sheltered travelers for twenty years. His house is marked on your map — you say — with a gold cross."

Margriet bent close. The scriptorium smelled of parchment and cold and the faint green of verdigris. "My map is in the shop, hidden. But yes — a house on the Leie, a gold cross."

"And here," the sister said, "the same name, later, struck out." Her voice was careful now, thinner. "Struck out by an inquisitor's scribe. Whoever came after the weaver took care that the memory should not survive."

Margriet looked at the struck line, the ink scored twice through the letters. "You know this name," she said.

The silence lasted. A bell rang somewhere in the abbey, a long way off.

"He was my great-uncle," said Sister Beatrijs. "My mother's mother's brother. We were told he died young, of a fever, before I was born. We were told his name was never spoken because he had been a man of trouble." She touched the struck-out line with her fingertip. "I have copied this name a hundred times. I did not know it was here."

Margriet said nothing. There was nothing to say that was not already lying between them on the desk.

"It is true, then," the sister went on, "the whole of it. What the map shows. What the book remembers. They were here, in this city, and we fed them, and we hid them, and then we forgot them, because forgetting was the price of living."

"Or of printing," Margriet said.

The door of the scriptorium opened. The abbess stood in the light, a tall woman in white, and her eyes went over the two of them and the book between them with the flat attention of a cat. Sister Beatrijs moved her hand to cover the page.

"Sister," said the abbess, "you are wanted in the infirmary."

"I will come, Mother."

The abbess did not move. She looked at Margriet, and Margriet saw that nothing in that room was private — that a convent kept its secrets the way a net keeps fish, by holding them, and letting them be seen.

When the abbess was gone, Sister Beatrijs closed the book.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we finish. And then you will tell me what you mean to print, and what it will cost."

Chapter 17: The Manuscript

They met for six days in the scriptorium, and slowly, name by name, the chronicle became a country. Sister Beatrijs read, and Margriet drew, and between them they rebuilt the road the erased had traveled: out of Languedoc, up the Rhône by barge and on foot, to the great fairs of Champagne, and then along the cloth routes — the wool road through the Ardennes, the river road down the Meuse — until the bales came to Ghent and Bruges and the weavers' houses that waited for them.

"How did the books travel?" Margriet asked.

"In the cloth," the sister said. "A book is pages; a bale of cloth is pages folded in wool. They rolled their books into the warp, between the weft, and the merchants carried them north without knowing — or knowing and not asking. The Good Men themselves walked beside the carts, dressed as lay brothers, mending looms."

"And the houses?"

"Here." The sister's finger moved down the page. "The sheltering houses. Each kept a loft, a cupboard, a false wall — a night's hiding. The password changed with the season. The weavers kept no lists, except this book, and this book they buried with the loom weights."

Margriet looked at her own drawing, the map she had drawn from memory on the scrap of paper, the gold crosses. She had lain awake over it for nights in the shop, the lamp guttering, the plates wrapped beneath her hand.

"The crosses on my map," she said slowly, "the gold towns and the gold crosses — they match the names that were not struck out."

"Then see what that means," said Sister Beatrijs. "The survivors are the record. The ones the inquisitors never found are the ones this map leads to. And the ones they found —" she touched a struck-out line, four names scored through in a row, "— the map remembers them too, because the map was cut into the copper before the erasure, and copper does not forget."

"The gold plate restores them," Margriet said. "When I print the gold, the struck-out names come back."

There was a long quiet. In the scriptorium a pen scratched somewhere, a novice copying a Psalm, and the sound was like the press's creak — a heartbeat kept in another room.

"Then you hold two keys," said the sister. "The manuscript is the key to the map — it tells you what the colors mean, house by house, name by name. And the map is the key to the erasure — it restores what was struck out, in gold, on paper that will outlast every scribe who crossed them out."

Margriet gathered her drawings and the book into her sleeve. Outside, the March light had turned to rain, and the abbey bell was ringing the hour to a gray sky.

"I am going to print it," she said. "All of it. The map, and the account, and the restored names."

Sister Beatrijs looked at her a long moment. Her face was pale, and the ink on her finger was a small dark mark, like a brand.

"Then God forgive us both," she said, "and remember us, as the copper does."

Chapter 18: The Church's Order

The printed order came to Ghent in the third week of March, and the guildhall was full to the door to hear it read.

Brother Koenraad stood at the head of the table in his white habit, and beside him stood two armed men in the bishop's livery. He read in Latin and then in Flemish, slowly, so that every master should understand: by the authority of the bishop of Tournai, and by letters of the Duke's Great Council at Mechelen, every press within the diocese was to be registered and examined. A press capable of printing in color was forfeit to the Church, with its plates, its punches, and its matrices. The penalty for concealment was the penalty for heresy.

The hall was very still. Margriet stood at the back, against the wall, and she could smell the fear — the old sweat of it, the wax and the damp wool — as it went around the room from master to master. The new art had been their pride and their terror in equal measure; now it had a price written on it.

Meester Hendrik rose. He did not look at her.

"We will comply," he said. "The Guild of St. Luke will keep the bishop's register, and every press in this city shall be brought before it for examination." He paused, and she saw his throat move. "Including the press of the late Jan van den Bossche."

"Which is broken," Margriet said, and her voice was clear in the silence. "The screw is cracked, the carriage binds. There is nothing in that shop for the Church to register but a dead man's tools."

Koenraad turned his winter-sea eyes on her, and he smiled a little, courteous, patient, as if she were a child who had made a clever excuse.

"The press will be examined," he said gently, "as it stands. If it is broken, it will be broken, and that will be the end of it. The Church does not want your press, daughter. The Church wants to see what it has printed." He looked down the hall at Hendrik. "And the guild will help us find it."

Hendrik's eyes met hers across the room, and Margriet saw the decision already made on his face — the face of a man who had chosen, weeks ago, which way the wind would blow, and had only been waiting to step into it.

The hall began to empty around her. Masters went past her without meeting her eye, the way men pass a burning house. She stood against the wall until the room was bare, and then she walked out into the cold, and the bells of St. Nicholas were ringing the curfew, and she counted the hours she had left to save what the bishop meant to take.

Chapter 19: The Raid

It was a guild apprentice who came to warn her — a boy she had taught to set a forme, sent by a master who would not come himself. He came to the shop window at dusk and made the sign they used for danger, a hand passed over a flame, and then he was gone into the rain, and Margriet stood in the doorway with her heart going like a drum.

"Now," she said to Pieter. "Not in an hour. Now."

They worked in the dark, by the last gray of the window and the single candle, without speaking, the way people work when the thing they are doing is larger than them. The press came apart as Jan had taught her to take it apart, a body into its bones: the screw lifted off, the crossbar down, the platen laid on its side, the carriage and the coffin slid free of the frame, the great oak frame itself unbolted joint by joint. The copper fittings she turned out one by one into her apron — a dozen small warm coins of the metal that holds memory. The brass pins went into her sleeve.

The base was the last. It was hollow, as Jan had made it, and in it lay the three copper plates in their oiled cloth, the pots, and the manuscript. She took the plates and wrapped them in her shawl and bound the shawl across her chest. The manuscript went under her kirtle. The pots she gave to Pieter.

They carried the press in pieces out the back, through the yard, through the brewery's side door — Katelijne was there, silent, holding a lantern — and down the cellar stairs. The last of it, the heavy frame, they half-carried, half-dragged, and the stones of the cellar floor took the weight of Jan's press for the first time.

They had just laid the floorboards back over the pit when the front door of the shop broke.

Margriet stood in the brewery's dark, behind the stacked barrels, and heard it: the splinter of the bar, the boots on the boards, the voices. She heard Koenraad's clerk, level and calm: "Search it." She heard the crash of a shelf, the rattle of a chase, the smell of ink spilling. She did not move. Pieter's hand found hers in the dark, and neither of them breathed until the steps crossed the room and went out again.

At the corner of the street they lit a fire. She watched it through the crack of the brewery door — a pile of printed sheets, proofs and waste and the stock of years, going up in flame and smoke. Koenraad stood before it, his hands clasped, and the firelight moved on his face like a living thing, and he looked, for a moment, satisfied.

The shop door was barred when they left, and a soldier stood before it in the rain. The shop was placed under watch.

In the cellar, by Katelijne's candle, Margriet undid her shawl and laid the plates on the press's oak frame, and the copper caught the light, and she put her hand on them and stood a long time without speaking, hearing the rain on the street above and the fire crackling out.

Chapter 20: The Hidden Press

The cellar smelled of clay and hops and the damp of the Leie, which ran close under the city here, and by day a thin light came through the grating at the top of the brewery stairs. Margriet learned the night of it: the way the candle moved, the way the press's creak carried in the close air, the way the whole room breathed when the river breathed with the tide.

They had rebuilt Jan's press in the dark, piece by piece, the screw seated true, the coffin sliding smooth on its rails, the copper fittings set back at the joints, the brass pins in the tympan. It stood now like a sleeping body in the corner of the cellar, and when Pieter pulled the bar in a test pass, it creaked once — a breath, a heartbeat — and the sound was the sound of home.

She printed by night. Katelijne kept the watch above — a shuffling step, a tap on the floor, the code of a woman who asked no questions and had kept every secret of a long life. When the tap came, Margriet blew out the lamp and stood in the dark with the plates wrapped to her chest, listening to the boots pass in the street above the grating, waiting for them to go.

The watchers were always there. She watched them from the brewery window, from the crack of the shutters: the man in the gray cloak who stood at the corner of the square and never bought anything, the clerk who came at the hour of vespers and looked at the barred shop and wrote in a little book. They were patient, the bishop's men. They had been patient for a hundred years. A woman hiding a press under a brewery was not, to them, a difficulty; she was a length of time.

She said it to Pieter on the fifth night, standing over the press with a sheet of paper in her hand and the ink-pot warm.

"They will wait us out. They will wait until we are tired, or careless, or until the guild votes to open the register and there is nowhere left to hide." She laid her hand on the copper plate at the frame's joint. "We print the map now, in full, while the plates are still ours. Or we never will."

Pieter looked at her a long moment in the candlelight. His face was quiet, and the fear was in it, but so was the thing that fear could not move.

"Then we print it," he said.

"Tonight we set the account. Tomorrow we pull the first pass." She turned the plate in her hands, and the light ran along the engraved lines — the rivers, the roads, the small crosses waiting to be gold. "Jan kept this secret for twenty years and died of keeping it. I will not die of keeping it. I will die of printing it, if I die at all — and that is the better death."

Above them, far off, the bell of St. Nicholas rang the hour, and the press creaked once, softly, as if it had heard her, and agreed.

Chapter 21: The Cellar
Under the city, the air smelled of clay and hops and the iron wet of the Leie. The press stood reassembled in Baes Katelijne's cellar like a sleeping body, and in the dark Margriet could feel its ribs. Pieter had the candles lit, three of them, and their light moved over the frame like water.

"Now," she said, and she heard the tremor in her own voice and hated it. "Red first."

The vermilion plate lay on the stone, heavy and cold, its engraved routes a maze of fine copper scratches that caught the light. She inked it with the pigmented oil varnish—the smell of it, linseed and mastic and a whiff of verdigris, filled the narrow space—and laid the sheet against the brass pins. Pieter pulled the bar. The press thumped once, deep in the earth, like a heartbeat heard through a floor.

They did not speak through the blue pass. She heard only the creak of the screw, the rat's rustle in the straw, the flame of the candle leaning and straightening. Azurite went down in a blue so pure it seemed to glow from inside the paper. The gold went last.

She lifted the sheet, and the map came whole out of the dark.

Routes in red crossed the sheet like veins. The rivers ran blue, the sea a ragged blue margin along the edge. And the towns, the safe houses, each marked with a small gold cross—there were dozens, and each one was a name the Church had struck out and a door that had opened in the night. Margriet held the page by its corners and her hands were shaking.

"God forgive me," Pieter said softly, looking at it.

"He taught us to print it," Margriet said. "Jan. He was too afraid. I am afraid too." She set the sheet down on the coffin of the press and touched the gold crosses with one finger, as if she were counting the bones of the dead. "But the color can only remember. It cannot be made to forget."

The candle gutted and righted itself. Somewhere above them, through the brewery, a barrel rolled and a dog barked at nothing. The bells of St. Nicholas began, far away, for Lauds, and the sound came down to them muffled through the clay, through the hops, through the dark—the city keeping its hours while beneath it two people held a century that the city had agreed to forget.

Pieter wrapped the sheet in oiled cloth without being asked. Margriet stood over the press with the plates in her hands, feeling their cold weight, and for a moment she wept silently over the platen, once, the way you weep for a thing you have just understood. The press creaked beneath her hand. It was Jan's breath. It was hers now.

They had done it.

Chapter 22: The Night Print
The rest of the night belonged to the press. Margriet set the second forme—not the map this time, but a sheet of names, the struck-out names, restored letter by letter in black that would carry the account like a litany.

Pieter inked and pulled; she fed the paper and checked the register against the brass pins by candlelight. The rhythm became a heartbeat: creak of the screw, thump of the platen, breath of the two of them in the cold cellar. The candles burned down one after another and the tallow smell thickened. Her hands, stained black at the nails, moved without her thinking. She had been five years at this craft and this was the first night it had ever felt like prayer.

"The routes of the Good Men," she said, setting the type of the title, and the letters came under her fingers like old friends. She had written the account herself that afternoon, in Pieter's steadier hand than hers would set. She knew it by heart, every clause: how the Bonshommes had fled Languedoc after Montségur; how the books had traveled north in bales of cloth; how families had hidden travelers for a night and a meal and a cup of water; how the inquisitors' scribes had come later, with the strikes of their pens, and made the sheltering unremembered.

"Names," Pieter said, and he did not look up. "Is it a sin to print the names of heretics?"

"It is a sin to forget them," Margriet said. "That is the whole of the erasure. They are not dead—they are erased. And the erasure was the heresy, because it lied about what God had made." She heard herself and almost smiled. She had been teaching him to read for two years, and now it was he who made her say the thing plainly.

A rat rustled in the straw. A candle guttered and drowned itself in its own wax, and Pieter lit another without a word. Outside, in the street, they heard the watchman call the hour, and then nothing but the press, the creak and the thump, the creak and the thump, until the small dark hours emptied out and the first gray light of dawn came down the cellar stairs through the crack of the brewery door.

They finished at daybreak. The sheets lay in a neat pile, warm from the press, the ink still holding the scent of the oil that carried it. Margriet took the topmost sheet and held it to the light. Names, restored. An account. A title.

"We have printed," she said, "in one night, what a century could not kill." And the press creaked beneath her hand like a man drawing breath, and she thought of Jan, and of the color, and of the truth that had waited twenty-five years in the base of a wooden press for two people brave or foolish enough to pull the bar.

Chapter 23: The Proof
They chose one copy. Not the first, which had a fleck of lampblack in the blue of the sea, and not the last, which showed the pins' faint bruise. They chose the one where the gold crosses stood clear and the red routes ran true, and Margriet wrapped it in oiled cloth with her own hands, folding the edges as she had seen Jan fold the sheets he meant to keep.

On the top of the bundle, before she closed it, she wrote in her own hand, with a quill she borrowed from the widow: The Routes of the Good Men, Restored to Memory. She signed beneath it with her initials, M.v.d.M., and the ink of the signature was red.

"That is the proof," Pieter said.

"That is the proof." She held it to her chest a moment, and it was heavy, heavier than copper, heavier than the plates themselves, because it was the truth in a form that could travel, that could be carried, that could outlive whatever happened to them in the morning. "Now the convent."

She went alone, at the hour of Terce, through the winter streets of Ghent with the bundle under her cloak. The city was waking: the fishwives at their stalls, the clatter of the Friday Market, the bells. Nobody looked twice at a woman in a gray cloak carrying nothing she should not have carried.

Sister Beatrijs met her in the abbey's outer parlor, and her face, when she understood, went still as a winter pond.

"You have printed it," Beatrijs said. It was not a question.

"I have printed it. And I need a letter." Margriet unwrapped the proof on the table between them and Beatrijs read it in silence, her finger moving over the gold crosses, and the silence went on long enough that Margriet heard her own heart.

Then Beatrijs looked up, and there were tears in her eyes, and she said, "My great-uncle's name is here. Restored." She wrote the letter of witness then and there, in her careful hand, on paper she fetched from the scriptorium, and she sealed it with the convent's wax and her own signet. She did not sign her religious name. She signed as she had been born, with the name the book remembered.

"You will be punished for this," Margriet said.

"Yes," said Sister Beatrijs. "It is the price. I have thought about the price. It is not too much." She wrapped the proof and the letter together and handed them back. "Carry it carefully. It is the only copy. It is the seed of everything."

The proof was real now, and signed, and dangerous. Margriet walked back through the city with it under her cloak and the whole weight of the erasure on her shoulders, and she thought: now what do we do with it?

Chapter 24: The Betrayal
Koenraad failed to find the press. That was the beginning. He had the shop watched, the street watched, the guildhall watched, and the cellar under the brewery gave up nothing. A press can hide under a city for a long time if the city agrees to look away.

But he did not rage. He waited, and he tightened the screw on Meester Hendrik van Coudenberghe by slow degrees—a word here, a threat there, a reminder that the Guild of St. Luke lived or died by the Church's goodwill in a city where the Church had long memory. Hendrik was a weathercock. He had always turned to the wind. The wind, in the winter of 1473, blew from Tournai.

Margriet saw it coming in small signs and did not believe them. Hendrik came to the brewery on the third Tuesday and asked, too kindly, after her health and her lodging. He asked, too casually, whether she had kept "anything of the late master's" beyond the ordinary. She lied and watched his eyes, and his eyes slid away from hers like water from oil.

"Katelijne," she said to the widow that night, "I think the guild is watching us."

"They are all watching," said Katelijne, and went on with her brewing.

The betrayal came at dusk, on the eve of the fair, when the light lay flat and gray on the water. Margriet was coming back from the convent—the proof had needed reading again, and Beatrijs had marked the crossings of the routes—when she turned the corner of the street and saw, by the brewery door, the horses.

Soldiers. And among them, on foot, his guild robe bundled against the cold, Meester Hendrik, pointing at the brewery gate with a gloved hand.

Margriet stood in the mouth of the alley and understood everything in the space of one breath. He had told them. He had led them. She could see in the set of his shoulders the shape of his surrender—not malice, not cruelty, only fear, the fear of a man who had chosen the winner and found the winner's price.

She had an hour, maybe less. The cellar door faced the brewery yard. The soldiers would circle, or Hendrik would tell them where the hidden door was, and the hour would shrink to minutes.

She did not run to the cellar. She ran first to Katelijne's kitchen, and she said one word: "Now." The widow looked at her once, asked nothing, and moved the ale cask aside to open the passage. Margriet went down into the dark where the press stood breathing, where the plates lay wrapped in oiled cloth, where the proof was hidden under a loose stone, and she began, very quickly, to make the choices that would decide the rest of her life.

Chapter 25: The Escape
There was no time to take the press apart. Margriet knew it the moment her foot touched the cellar floor—the bar, the screw, the coffin, the massive oak frame that had cost Jan years and had carried the plates across a country and a secret for a quarter century. It was too heavy, too slow, too much.

She took what could be carried and nothing else.

The plates went first, into the empty oil pot with the cloth around them. The proof and Beatrijs's letter went into her bodice, flat against her ribs. A handful of the night's sheets—the names, the account—she wrapped in a flour sack. She paused over the press, and in that pause she heard it creak, a long slow sound like a breath let out, and she put her hand on the screw as she had put her hand on Jan's shoulder the night he died.

"Forgive me," she said.

Then she was up the stairs and through the brewery, past Katelijne who did not speak, into the narrow passage behind the brewhouse, and out into the night by the water stairs. Pieter was there—someone had warned him, or he had seen the horses—and he took the flour sack without a word and they moved along the Leie through the dark, two shadows keeping to the deepest of the gloom.

Behind them, the crash of the cellar door. The shouts. Then a sound that stopped Margriet in her tracks and turned her around in spite of herself—the splintering crack of the press, Jan's press, being broken. She heard it like a body falling. Screw, frame, the copper fittings ringing on the stone. The smiths' hammers, if they had brought hammers. The murder of wood.

"Margriet." Pieter's hand on her arm. "The barge. Now."

She made herself move. The barge lay at the quay where the boatman had promised, a low dark shape on the black water. She climbed aboard with the pot of plates in her arms and the proof at her breast and did not look back, even when the alarm bell began, even when the city behind them broke into the shouting and the running of men who had found nothing but a ruined press and a cold hearth.

The barge cast off. The Leie took them. The city gate slid past, and the dark water widened between Margriet and everything she had ever owned—the shop, the paper, the years, and the broken bones of the copper press lying on a cellar floor in the city that had forgotten how to be free.

She did not weep. Not yet. She held the plates and watched the bank slide by, and she began to understand the price of truth, because she had just paid it in the only home she had ever had.

Chapter 26: The Canal Boat
A week of rain and slow water. The barge moved down the Leie with the current, past drowned meadows and willows standing in the flood, and the rain fell without drama, hour after hour, a gray curtain between the barge and the world.

Margriet was the bargewoman's niece now, "Elske," wrapped in a borrowed shawl with her hair hidden in a cap. She sat in the hold among the bales, the pot of plates at her feet, and read the proof by lantern light until she knew it by heart. She read it even with her eyes closed. The red routes ran inside her like her own veins. The gold crosses burned behind her lids.

Pieter stayed on deck, silent, watching the banks. He asked for nothing. He had asked for nothing since Ghent, and she had nothing to give him but the truth of what she carried, and he carried it with her without being asked. Once he came down into the hold, dripping, and said only: "In Ghent they are saying a printer's shop burned. A woman fled. The Church is hunting a map." Then he went back up into the rain.

She thought about that in the dark. The shop burned—or the Church said it burned, to explain the cellar and the hammer and the empty shop. A woman fled. A map. The story was already running ahead of them, faster than the barge, faster than the current. News travels faster than a boat, she thought. By the time we reach the sea, the whole country will know a name for what I carry.

She slept with the proof under her hand and the plates against the bale, and when she woke the rain had thinned and the water had grown wider and salt-touched, and the boatman said they would reach the sea by nightfall.

She read the proof one last time by the last of the lantern, and she said to the dark hold, in a whisper, "Black ink can lie. The color can only remember." It was the first time she had spoken the words aloud since the cellar, and the saying of them felt like a promise she had made to Jan and was now keeping.

Pieter came down and sat across from her in the lantern light. "What will you do," he said, "when we reach the city?"

"Find a press," she said. "Find ink. Find copper. And print it again."

He nodded as if she had said something ordinary. Behind them the Leie ran on toward the sea, and ahead of them the rain cleared into a pale sky, and somewhere beyond the water was Bruges, the free city, where nobody knew her name.

Chapter 27: The Free City
Bruges was a different world, and Margriet felt it the moment she stepped off the barge onto the quay—the noise of it, the light of it, the sheer press of living bodies and goods. Canals cut through the streets, and the streets were thick with a hundred languages, wool and wine and spice in the air, merchants shouting, bells ringing, water slapping stone. The richest city in the North, and she was a woman with a pot of copper plates and a name she had left behind in Ghent.

Nobody knew her. She tested it like a bruise, like a hand pressed to a wound: nobody knew her. The women at the market stalls did not glance at her with the pity or the fear she had grown used to in Ghent. She was nobody. She was free, and free was a kind of drowning.

She stood at the Vlamingbrug, in the press of the crowd, and looked at the belfry rising out of the roofs, and her legs would not carry her any further. She had not wept since the cellar, had not wept on the barge, and now she wept in public, briefly, helplessly, because she had nowhere left to go and nothing left to lose, and the freedom of it was the loneliest thing she had ever felt.

A stall beside her sold printed broadsheets—she saw them before she saw anything else. Lives of the saints, a ballad of the wars, an indulgence for the living and the dead. She reached out and touched the ink with her finger, and the touch was the first thing that had felt like home since the Leie.

The stallkeeper looked at her. "You read, girl?"

"No," she lied, and wiped her eyes with her sleeve, and moved on into the crowd.

But she went the long way through the city, following the smell of ink. It was there, in the air, thin as a thread of smoke over the water—the smell of oil and lampblack and old paper, the smell of a press at work. She had followed it all her life without knowing it. Now she followed it through the scribes' quarter, past the illuminators' workshops and the booksellers' stalls, past the gold of a rubric drying on a page, until she stood before a low door by the canal, and over it a painted sign: a press, and a name she did not know.

She stood a long time in the street looking at it. Then she drew a breath, and knocked.

The door opened, and a woman looked out at her—broad, gray-haired, with ink on her apron and sharp eyes that took in the shawl, the cap, the pot held close, and asked nothing.

"Well?" said Reine van der Poorten. "What is it, then? I have work."

Chapter 28: The Printer's Quarter
The quarter by the Vlamingbrug smelled of ink in the open air, the way other streets smelled of fish or hides. Margriet walked it like a pilgrim walking a shrine. The scribes' shops, the illuminators at their open casements, the booksellers' stalls with their wares chained to the boards; and among them, new and strange, the printers—a window where a printed page hung like a banner, a yard where a press thumped in the daylight and the sound came out over the water.

Reine van der Poorten's workshop stood over the canal, a low room with a good east light. The press inside was old, Margriet saw at once—an honest wooden press, well kept, but tired, the screw worn, the carriage loose in its ways. It creaked when it worked, and the creak was not Jan's creak.

Reine had set her a test without saying it was a test. "Set me a forme," she said, "and let me see a woman's hands at work. A woman is a slow death for a press." She said it flatly, without malice, the way she said everything.

Margriet did not answer. She took the copy, read it, and began to set. Her fingers found the type case by the shapes her hands remembered. The letters went into the composing stick the way water goes downhill, without decision. Across the room she was aware of Reine watching, and of the candle burning down, and of the smell of the oil and the damp stone of the quarter coming through the open door.

She locked the forme, set it in the chase, and pulled a proof, and only then did she look up. Reine took the sheet, held it to the light, and was silent for a long moment.

"Who taught you?" Reine said at last.

"A dead man in Ghent," Margriet said. "I am called Maeyken."

Reine looked at her, and there was something in her eyes that might have been the beginning of respect or the beginning of pity, and she said only: "The forme is good. The press is yours at dawn." She did not ask where the dead man was, or why a woman with hands like these had come alone down the Leie. "Colard Mansion's shop is across the canal," she said, as if it mattered. "The Englishman Caxton prints there. This quarter holds what it holds, and asks nothing. Remember that."

Margriet nodded. She had found, by accident or by the pull of the craft, the one place in the North where the past did not follow her into the daylight.

That night she unwrapped the plates in her rented room, in the dark, and ran her fingers over the engraved routes without lighting a candle, the way a blind person reads, and she thought: this is the only press in the world that was ever broken and this is the city that will print the truth. She believed it the way she believed in God—without evidence, and without giving it up.

Chapter 29: The Refugee
She became Maeyken, compositor to Reine van der Poorten, and the name fit her like a borrowed dress—serviceable, plain, not hers. She rose before dawn with the bells and worked the press until the light failed, and the work was good, and the work was a kind of peace, because in the work she did not have to think about what she carried.

The plates lay wrapped under her bed in the rented room above the tanner's, and she checked them every night, touching the oiled cloth, counting the three weights of them in the dark like a miser counting coin. Beatrijs's letter she kept in her sleeve, next to her skin, and once a week she took it out and read it by candlelight until the wax of the seal grew soft from the warmth of her hand. It was the only paper of honor she owned.

She watched the streets the way a hunted thing watches, without seeming to. She learned the faces of the quarter—the beadle, the fishwife, the canon who crossed the bridge at Terce, the apprentice who ran for Colard Mansion. She learned which doors stood open and which stayed shut, and she never once spoke of Ghent, and the quarter, true to Reine's word, never once asked.

Pieter found work at the docks and lodging two streets off, and he asked for nothing, and she was grateful for that more than for anything. He came to the shop on feast days and stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands, and they said little, and it was enough.

The city was kind and it was indifferent, and Margriet came to understand that the two were the same thing. A woman could disappear here. A woman with a press could be Maeyken forever, could set type and pull proofs and grow old and die with the plates wrapped in oiled cloth under her bed, and the century would stay erased, and the gold crosses would stay buried in copper, and no one would ever know.

That was the temptation, and she named it to herself at night, honestly, the way you name a sin: to stay safe. To stay Maeyken. To let the erasure stand because the erasure was quiet and she was alive.

And every night she answered it the same way, with her hands. She had begun, in the dark after the work, to draw the map again from memory on scrap paper, the red routes and the blue rivers and the small gold crosses, over and over, until her hands knew it the way they knew the type case. She was not going to be able to forget it. She was not going to be allowed to forget it.

She kept the drawings under the mattress with the plates. She had a press again, and it was not hers, and she had a name that was not hers, and the proof burned in her chest like a coal that would not go out.

Chapter 30: The New Press
Reine's press was old, weak, and wheezing, and Margriet grew to hate the sound of its labor the way you hate the sound of a sick animal breathing. It was a good press, honest, well fed with good paper and good ink, but its years lay heavy on it, and every proof it pulled was a small surrender.

She said nothing at first. Then she said, one evening, "The screw is worn. The carriage is loose. It will last you two more years and then it will be a pile of firewood."

Reine looked at her over the candle. "And you would do better?"

"I would rebuild it." Margriet felt the risk of the words as she spoke them, felt the quarter listening behind Reine's eyes. "I know a press that was bound with copper, joint by joint, with brass pins in the tympan that held every page in register. I know how it was made. I can make this one so."

"Copper." Reine said the word slowly, tasting it. "You know the copper press. The one they talk about in Ghent."

"I know a press," Margriet said, "and the man who made it is dead, and I would rather rebuild his work than watch another one die."

There was a silence long enough to hold a breath and a half. Then Reine shrugged—a practical woman settling a price—and said, "My good oil is yours. My son will turn the handles. And if you break my press, you will buy me a new one."

They did it over nine nights, after the work, by candlelight. Pieter came from the docks and worked the timber with her, and Reine's son learned the names of the joints, and Margriet's hands remembered Jan's press so exactly that they seemed to move without her. The copper fittings she had copied from memory, in her head, plate by plate, before she ever touched a hammer; she bought the copper from a tinker and cut it herself, and the smell of the metal and the file was the smell of the cellar in Ghent, and it did not hurt her the way she had feared it would hurt her. It felt like coming home to a door you had built yourself.

The locksmith turned the brass pins to her drawing, and she set them in the new tympan with her own hands, and when the press was whole again—reassembled like a sleeping body waking—she stood back and looked at it, and Reine said nothing, but her son said, "It does not creak the same."

"No," Margriet said. "It is not the same press. It is a new one."

The first proof on the new press was a page of the Psalter, black ink, and it came off the platen clean and true, the register perfect, the letters as sharp as if they had been cut. Margriet held the sheet in her ink-stained hands, steady, and she thought of the plates wrapped in oiled cloth under the bed in the room above the tanner's, and she felt the coal in her chest bank and settle, waiting.

She had a press again. And this one was hers.

Chapter 31: The Color, Printed

Reine said no.

"Print the map, and you bring the bishop down on all of us. The bishop, the duke, the whole weight of the Church." She wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the plates where they lay on the bench, wrapped in oiled cloth. "I hired a compositor, not a heretic."

Margriet did not argue. She had learned that Reine only listened when you gave her something to weigh. So she set the red plate in the press, inked it with the varnish she had made that morning, and pulled a single proof — the routes, vermilion, bright as a wound against the white paper.

Reine looked at it for a long time. Then she said, "Print the truth before we are all dead."

The words sat in the shop like a bell struck once.

They waited for the dusk, when the quarter was quiet and the lamps had not yet been lit. Pieter barred the door and set the candles. The plates came out of their cloth one by one — copper, heavy, cold, the memory of Jan's hands still in the weight of them. Margriet's own hands were shaking as she inked the azurite plate for the blue pass. Reine's oil, pressed from good flax, was clearer than anything she had owned in Ghent, and the blue came up like water — the rivers, the canals, the sea, all the ways a man might flee.

The press creaked. It had a new voice now, this press of Reine's, rebuilt with copper fittings copied from the memory of Jan's press, but the creak was the same: a breath drawn and let out, a heartbeat. Margriet laid the sheet on the points and the brass pins caught it, held it, guided it true.

The gold pass was last. Orpiment ground fine, mixed into the oil varnish, spread thin on the copper so the towns and the safe houses and the small crosses would hold. She cranked the platen down. The whole press shuddered, and Pieter took the sheet as she lifted it free.

The three colors lay over one another in perfect register: red routes crossing the blue water, gold towns and gold crosses marking the houses where the Good Men had slept, one night, one meal, one name saved from the fire. It was the same map she had printed in the cellar of Ghent, and it was not the same. Under Reine's roof it smelled of good oil and honest ink, and the colors were richer than fear had ever let them be.

She held the sheet up to the candle. The shop smelled like a miracle — linseed and copper and the faint green of verdigris, and under it the clean paper of a free city.

Reine said nothing. She took the sheet, and she hung it on the wall, and she did not take it down.

Outside, the bells of St. Salvator rang the hour, and the press creaked once, gently, like a body settling to sleep.

Chapter 32: The Map, Published

They sold it for a groat, and the first sheet went to a wool merchant before the ink was dry.

Reine had argued the price — the color cost more to make than black, the oil alone was worth three times the money — but Margriet had shaken her head. "Too dear and it is a curiosity," she said. "Too cheap and it is trash. A groat is what a man pays to know something he was not supposed to know." Reine looked at her, and then she set the price, and the shop was never quiet again.

Word ran before the paper did. A street cries a rumor; a rumor climbs a canal and finds a belfry. By mid-morning there was a line at the door, and Margriet, setting the sheets at the press, could hear them arguing over the last copies in the street — the red routes, the blue rivers, the gold crosses. A man from the cloth hall hung one on the pillar where the guilds posted their tariffs, and a crowd three deep read it aloud to one another, halting, spelling out the names.

A priest of St. Salvator pushed through the crowd at noon. He was young and red-faced and he tore the sheet from the pillar and held it up.

"This is heresy!" he shouted. "These are the names of the cursed! Who has printed this?"

Nobody answered. The crowd looked at him the way it looks at a barge gone aground — with interest, and no intention of helping.

Margriet watched from the shop door, her hands folded in her apron, her ink-stained fingers hidden. She felt the fear cold in her chest and, under it, something else, something that frightened her more: a small hard pride, like a stone she had been carrying for a year and had only now found was a seed.

The sheets sold out by noon.

That evening Reine counted the money twice, then once more, slowly, as if she did not trust the count to stay put. Pieter came in from the docks with his wages still in his fist and asked, quietly, what the shouting had been about. Margriet told him, and he sat down at the workbench and was silent a long while.

"They tore down one," he said at last. "In Ghent they burned a cartload."

"In Ghent we were hiding," Margriet said. "Here we are selling." She wrapped the plates in their oiled cloth and laid them under the floorboards, where she had made a hollow the size of a man's palm, and pressed the boards back with her heel until they sat flush.

The bells rang the curfew. The shop smelled of ink and candle smoke and the strange clean smell of a sheet that has been handled by a hundred hands in one day.

Chapter 33: The Shock

For three days the city talked of nothing else.

The canons of St. Donatian met and issued a letter, copied by their own scribes and read at the crossroads, denouncing "the map of the accursed" and those who sold it. The letter made the map more famous. The price of a sheet, which had been a groat, rose to a stuiver and then to two, and still the shop could not print them fast enough.

On the fourth day a crowd gathered before the door — not of the faithful, Margriet thought, watching from the shutter, but of the bored and the hungry and the curious, the sort that gathers at any noise and takes its temper from the first man to shout. A stone struck the wood above the sign. Somebody called for the printer to come out.

Colard Mansion came out instead.

He came from his own workshop across the canal, a solid man in a printer's apron, and he stood in front of the crowd with his arms folded and said, in a voice that carried to the back of the square, "A press is a voice. Which of you wants to be the man who cut out the first voice this city ever owned?"

The crowd did not disperse. But it stopped, the way a wave stops, and then it broke and went home in twos and threes, and the stone lay in the gutter until a dog nosed it.

That night the vicar of St. Salvator preached against her by name.

"Margriet van der Meer," he thundered, and the name fell into the nave like a thing dropped from a height. "A woman of Ghent, fled from justice, who prints the names of the damned and calls it memory."

Margriet was not in the church. She heard of it from the woman who sold candles at the porch, who had wept at the vicar's vehemence and then bought a sheet on her way home. The woman was not sure which act had been the sin.

In the workshop, the press thumped on all night. Pieter turned the handle; Margriet fed the sheets; the plates, warmed by use, gave up their colors like a secret repeated too many times to be kept. The smell of linseed and lampblack rode out the windows into the dark, and the city slept on it, and dreamed, some of them, of red roads leading somewhere.

She worked until her fingers were raw and the candle had burned to a puddle. The map was not a secret anymore. It was a fact, like the Leie or the belfry — and like the Leie, it could be dammed, but it could not be unfollowed.

Chapter 34: The Heresy, Open

She had hidden her name so long it had begun to feel like a borrowed coat, warm enough to wear, never quite hers.

The second sheet came to her whole one night while the press rested. She set the text herself, letter by letter, in a new face she had cut that week for Reine's Psalter — a clean book hand, open and legible. At the top of the sheet the map would go, and beneath it, in three short paragraphs, the account of the erasure: how the names were struck from the registers, the books burned, the routes forgotten; how a century had made the dead not only dead but unremembered. And beneath that, where a printer signs, she set the letters of her own name.

Margriet van der Meer of Ghent.

She stared at the line in the stick for a long time. Maeyken would have taken it out. Maeyken had a debt to safety and a habit of hiding. But Maeyken had never pulled a color proof in her own shop.

She printed it in red.

The vermilion was the same pigment that had carried the routes out of Jan's plates, and it came up under the platen like a signature she had been practicing all her life. The gold pass put the crosses in; the blue pass put the rivers under them. When the three were married in register, the sheet was a map and a confession and a title page all at once: The Routes of the Good Men, Restored to Memory. Printed by Margriet van der Meer of Ghent, who was not ashamed of the ink on her hands.

Reine read it standing up, and then sat down without being asked, which in Reine was the height of feeling.

"Maeyken," she said, and stopped. "Margriet." She tested the name like a coin, weighing it. "You were never going to be a compositor."

"No," Margriet said. "But I had to be one first."

The quarter learned of it the next morning, when Reine took the sheet and nailed it to the shop door with four nails, one for each corner, and stood back to look at it, and said nothing at all. People gathered to read. Some crossed themselves; some touched the printed name as if it might be warm; the woman who sold candles at the porch bought three sheets and kissed the paper. A scribe from the cathedral workshop said aloud that he had set type for twenty years and never once seen a woman's name in print.

"Then you have not seen everything," Reine said, and went back inside, and the shop smelled of ink and of the appalled, proud silence of a street that has been shown a thing it will not soon forget.

Margriet printed copies all day. Under her breath, the way she talked to books, she repeated the name to herself: Margriet van der Meer of Ghent, in red, on paper, in the world.

Chapter 35: The Hunt

The news traveled south faster than a man could walk, because paper travels faster than anything, and a name in red travels fastest of all.

It reached Tournai in the person of a merchant who had bought a sheet for the novelty of it and shown it to a canon of his acquaintance. The canon read the account of the erasure, and then read it again, and sent it with his own hand to the bishop's palace, where it lay for a day in the office of the vicar for heresy.

Brother Koenraad read it without changing expression. His eyes were the color of the sea in winter, and they moved across the printed names the way a reader's eyes move across a familiar text, already knowing what they would find. Then he wrote letters. He wrote to the bishop, to the Parliament of Mechelen, and to a clerk in the ducal chancery who owed him a favor. By midsummer he held in his hands a set of documents with seals on them, and he set out for Bruges with a quiet satisfaction, patient as ever, certain that what was printed could be unprinted, given time and the right pressure.

His men arrived first.

Margriet saw them three days later at the Friday market — a fat lay brother in a Dominican habit asking questions of the stallholders, and a lean clerk taking notes. They had a description of her: a woman of two-and-twenty with ink-stained hands. She was standing a yard away buying onions, and she watched the clerk write down the words ink-stained hands, and her own hands stayed perfectly still at her sides.

She walked back to the quarter by the long way, and she was afraid, and the fear made her angry, and the anger made her careful.

The printers of Bruges closed ranks. It was not love — Reine said as much aloud, and the others nodded — but principle: a press on fire draws the whole trade to it, and no man who works in ink wants to be the one who pointed the torch. When the lay brother came to the quarter asking after the woman who printed the colors, every workshop had a different story and a blank face. One said she had sailed for Antwerp. One said she had died of a fever in the spring. One, Colard Mansion, said he did not know her, and said it in such a way that the lay brother knew he did, and knew also that the knowing would go no further.

That night Reine hid the plates under a loose stone in the cellar, wrapped in oiled cloth, with a layer of hops over them for the smell. The quarter became a fortress of silence and ink, and in the middle of it Margriet slept with her own name now — no longer Maeyken, but herself — and dreamed of red roads, and a man with winter eyes who was coming, unhurried, to take them.

Chapter 36: The Shelter

She was hidden by turns, like a press that is dismantled and carried through the night, piece by piece, and set up somewhere new.

Three nights in Reine's attic, between the rafters, with the plates sewn into a cushion under her head. Then a scribe's cellar in the parchment-makers' lane, where the air smelled of lime and old skin and the scratching of pens above her all day. Then the Bruges Beguinage, where a lay sister with her mother's kind of quiet eyes gave her a narrow room and asked no questions — a nod to the Beguine who had taught her Latin, the woman dead these ten years, whose ghost Margriet felt walking beside her down the whitewashed cloister. And then Colard Mansion's workshop, behind a stack of paper so tall it reached the rafters, where she slept among the smell of good cotton rag and the faint copper of the plates wrapped in her bundle.

It was not love that sheltered her. Nobody pretended it was. It was principle, and principle was stronger. A voice, Colard said, must not be cut out — and she was the voice of the press, the only one who could pull the three passes true. They hid her the way a guild hides its charter, the way a city hides its privileges: because what was hidden was what made them a city.

The letter came on her last evening in the Beguinage, folded small and sealed with a blob of wax the color of dried blood. The lay sister brought it to her cell and left it on the bed without a word.

Margriet knew the hand before she broke the seal. Beatrijs had written once before, from the Bijloke, and the letters of her name were like her pens — careful, upright, exact.

"Brother Koenraad has left Ghent," the letter read. "They say he goes to Bruges. His methods are patience and documents; he will not strike, he will wear. He believes the erasure is a holy work, and he will see it done before he dies. God keep you. Do not let the color die. — Your sister in ink, B."

And beneath the signature, in a different hand, a single line: "The abbess has confined me to the scriptorium for a month. It is a small price. I would pay it again."

Margriet read the letter twice, then a third time, and then she hid it in her sleeve, against her wrist, where the mole was, where the pulse was. The paper warmed to her skin. Somewhere out on the road south, a man with winter eyes was coming, and he had all the time in the world.

She lay in the Beguinage dark and listened to the bells, and she thought of Beatrijs paying for the truth with her freedom, a month at a time, in a room full of pens.

The Beguine at the door looked at her in the morning and saw the letter's crease in her sleeve, and said nothing, and let her go.

Chapter 37: The Inquisitor's Arrival

He came in the fullness of summer, when the canals were green with the heat and the market smelled of fish and tar and cut flowers.

Margriet heard him before she saw him — the street went quiet the way a street goes quiet when something official passes, the way it quiets for a cart of corpses or a procession of relics. She was in Colard's garret, and she pressed her face to the shutter and looked down.

Brother Koenraad rode a gray horse, unhurried, in the black and white of the Dominican habit, with his clerk riding behind and a servant carrying the episcopal standard. The bishop's seal hung at his saddle in a leather case, and a second seal, heavier, was carried in a locked coffer on the servant's shoulder — the ducal letters, Margriet knew without being told, from the Parliament of Mechelen. He was courteous. He raised a hand to the crowd as if he were a returning lord, and people uncovered their heads without meaning to, the reflex of centuries.

The sea-in-winter eyes passed over the quarter once, slowly, and she felt them find the garret and pass on, and she did not know if he had seen her and chosen not to look, or had seen the shutter move and filed it away for later. Either way, the patience was the same.

He was lodged at the Franc, and the next morning he came before the magistrates of Bruges in full form. Margriet was not in the hall. But the quarter had eyes, and the eyes came back to her with the tale, word by word, until she could see it as if she had stood in the doorway.

He had laid the letters on the bench before the aldermen — the bishop's seal and the duke's seal — and had asked, in his quiet voice, for the surrender of Margriet van der Meer of Ghent, and of the copper plates, and of every printed sheet of the map. He had called it not a punishment but a mercy: the woman was errant, misled, a soul to be saved from the error she had been taught. The erasure was the Church's right and duty; the map was a lie that would undo a century of holy work. He was patient, courteous, certain, and the aldermen had sat very still, and had asked to see the letters, and had taken them away to the archives to be read at leisure.

The city wavered. Margriet felt it in the air of the quarter — a tension like the heat before rain. The letters were law; the bishop was powerful; the duke was stronger still. A merchant from Antwerp said openly that no city in the North would shelter a heretic against the Church and keep its privileges. Another said that no city in the North had ever surrendered one of its own craft to the bishop and kept its honor. The two opinions carried the argument through the taverns, and neither won.

From the garret window, Margriet watched the gray horse walk the streets each evening, patient, courteous, and she began, quietly, to prepare.

Chapter 38: The Stand

In the morning she went down the garret stairs, and the printers' quarter watched her go and did not try to stop her, because she was the one who had chosen, and her choosing was the whole of it.

She carried the plates wrapped in oiled cloth under her arm, a pot of ink, and the three colors in their little jars. She walked to the cloth hall in the full light of the market, and the crowd parted for her without being asked, the way water parts. On the steps of the hall, where the guilds posted their tariffs, she set up the little press Pieter had trundled behind her on a handcart without a word — the proofing press, copper-fitted, that they had built in the spring.

She printed in the open, in the sun, where anyone could see.

The red pass, the blue pass, the gold pass. She worked without hurry, her hands steady, her ink-stained fingers moving among the pins and the tympan and the clean sheets. The crowd watched the colors come up the way they had watched the map sell out at noon — with a hush, and then a murmur, and then a name repeated. Her name. Margriet van der Meer. She was printing it, in red, on the steps of the cloth hall, in the face of the bishop's seal.

The final sheet carried the map, and beneath it her name, and beneath that the restored names of the erased, the struck-out names the gold plate had given back. She held it up for the crowd to see, and there was a sound from them like a held breath let go.

Then she folded the sheet, put the plates back in their cloth, and walked into the town hall.

They brought her before the magistrates with the Inquisitor's clerk watching from the bench, and the aldermen looked at the small woman with the ink-stained hands and did not know what to do with her, because she had walked in of her own will and asked for nothing.

"You may burn the paper," she said. Her voice was quiet. "The plates remember."

She set the bundle on the table and stepped back. The room was very still. The clerk's quill had stopped. Outside, the market had gone quiet too, as if the city itself were holding its breath.

The oldest alderman looked at the bundle a long time, and then at her, and then he said, in a voice that was not unkind: "Sit down, woman. We will have to talk about this."

She sat down.

Chapter 39: The Trial, Averted

The wrangle lasted the length of a hot summer.

It was not a trial — that was the first thing the aldermen established, and they established it with the careful precision of men who have been sued by the bishop before. There was no crime to try until the jurisdiction was settled, and the jurisdiction was exactly what the Inquisitor's clerk and the city's own lawyers argued about, day after day, in the close air of the town hall, with the doors shut and the citizens of Bruges crowded in the street outside to hear the shouting through the glass.

The bishop's vicar demanded canon law. The Church had jurisdiction over heresy, and heresy was what the map was, and the woman was a heretic, and the case belonged to Tournai. The duke's letters, produced and read aloud, demanded the surrender of the woman and the plates to the Church's authority, in the name of the Parliament of Mechelen.

And the city of Bruges — the city that had fought the count and the Church and the sea itself for its privileges — answered with its charters.

The aldermen read the franchises aloud, and their lawyers read them again, with marginal notes. Trade, the charters said, was Bruges's own. A printer was a craftsman, and a craftsman belonged to his guild and his town, and no person of Bruges could be seized for a debt or a crime alleged outside the walls without the consent of the magistrates. The clerk blinked at the word person, and read on. The map had been printed in Bruges, by a printer of Bruges, under the guild's eye. The bishop's claim was a claim about paper, and paper, in Bruges, was trade.

The city did not acquit her. It refused. It found the technicality, the charter clause, the delay — a missing seal here, a complaint misfiled there, a question of whether the ducal letters had been countersigned for the county of Flanders, which of course they had not, a detail that would take months to resolve, if it were ever resolved at all.

Koenraad stood before them and heard the refusal given courteously, formally, wrapped in the city's best manners, and his winter eyes did not change. He bowed. He took his letters back. And in the autumn, when the case was still unresolved and the bishop's patience was wearing thin, he was recalled to Tournai — his superiors having tired, the rumor said, of a case that made the bishop enemies in the richest city of the North over a piece of printed paper.

Margriet was confined to the quarter. Not to a cell — the aldermen were careful about that — but to the quarter, its streets and its canals and its smells, until the matter should be settled. It was a cage with a wide door.

She was not free. But she was alive, and the plates were under the floor of Reine's cellar, wrapped in hops, undiscovered, and the bells of St. Salvator rang for terce and she heard them, and the press waited.

Chapter 40: The City's Choice

The guild of St. Luke of Bruges had not wanted her. It had wanted, in order: her silence, her disappearance, and then, as the summer wore on, her sheets, which sold for three times what black-ink work sold and drew merchants to the quarter the way honey draws flies. Merchants are not idealists. Merchants count, and what they counted, in the long warm autumn evenings, was the money the color was making and the fact that the bishop's business was not, in the end, the guild's business.

The guildmaster came to Reine's shop in October, on a morning of cold clear light, and he was carrying a ledger.

"The guild has taken a vote," he said. He looked at the sheets on the wall — the map, the names, the account — and he looked at Margriet, and there was something in his face that was almost respect and almost wariness and mostly the careful blankness of a man who has made his choice and will not look back. "The color printers are admitted to the trade."

"All of them," Reine said. It was not a question.

"All of them. Including the woman."

Reine nodded slowly, and then she said, "And the register?"

The guildmaster opened the ledger. The register of the guild of St. Luke of Bruges, which held the names of every master who had ever worked in the quarter — painters, illuminators, booksellers, the new printers — each name entered in a clerk's hand, one to a line, with the craft and the year. He dipped a pen.

"Your name," he said to Margriet. "Spell it for me."

She spelled it. Margriet van der Meer.

He wrote it, in the careful clerk's hand, on a clean line, and the ink was still wet when he closed the ledger and held it out for her to see. A woman's name, entered in the register of the guild of St. Luke of Bruges. The first in the city's memory. The first the guild clerk could find, and he had looked, because the looking was now part of the story.

"There," the guildmaster said. "You are a printer of Bruges. Print the color, and pay your dues, and the guild will back you against the bishop or the duke or the sea itself."

That night the shop was lit against the dark, and they printed for the joy of it — not the map, but a sheet of colored initials, red and blue and gold, page after page, until the press creaked like a heartbeat and thumped like one too, and Reine brought beer and they drank it over the platen, and Pieter turned the handle until his arms ached and would not stop.

Margriet stood in the door of the shop, in the cold October air, and looked at the lit window and the warm smell of linseed and lampblack streaming out of it, and she thought of Jan, who had been afraid to print the color, and of the copper plates under the floor, waiting.

Somewhere, patient, Koenraad was writing letters. It did not matter. The city had chosen the press, and the press was hers.

Chapter 41: The Press Triumphs

The orders came like swallows in spring, one after another. A monastery wanted colored initials for its choir book; a merchant of the Hansa wanted the map, framed under glass, to hang above his counting desk; the aldermen wanted her name set in red on the guild register, just so. The workshop smelled of linseed oil from morning until the curfew bell, and Margriet moved through the smell like a fish through water.

She taught Reine's son the three passes first — how to ink the vermilion plate thin so the lines held, how to feel the sheet settle against the two brass pins. He learned with his hands before his head, and she loved him for that. The color was no longer a secret. It was a craft, like casting a forme or grinding a pigment, and crafts could be taught and kept and passed on.

In the evenings the press creaked like a heartbeat again, regular as breathing, and Margriet caught herself listening for Jan's voice in it.

On a quiet night in October, the first rain of the autumn against the shutters, Pieter came in from the docks with his hands raw from rope and asked, without sitting down, "Will you tell me about him now?"

She knew whom he meant. The name had not crossed her lips since the canal, a week of water and green banks and his body wrapped in sacking.

"Jan van den Bossche," she said, and the syllables felt heavy as copper in her mouth. "He taught me the press. He taught me that a press is a body. He was afraid to print the truth, and he died of it, and he gave me the plates anyway, in his death, the way a man gives a child a knife."

Pieter sat down then, heavily, and said nothing, which was the whole of his tenderness.

She told him about the first color proof, the wonder of it coming off the copper onto the paper; about Jan's voice in the margins of his books, correcting a comma in his sleep; about the way he used to say the press creaked like a breath, and how she had thought him foolish until she heard it stop.

When she finished, the candle had burned to a nub and the rain had stopped. The press creaked once, settling, in the dark of the shop.

"There," she said. "Now he is alive in two places instead of one."

Pieter reached out and put his hand over hers where it lay ink-stained on the table. He did not ask for more. The press creaked again, like a breath let out.

Chapter 42: The Truth Spreads

The map went out of Bruges the way water finds the cracks in a dam — slowly at first, then all at once.

A wool merchant took three copies to Antwerp folded inside his bales, and by the time he sold the cloth the copies were sold too, for more than the cloth. A student carried one to Leuven in his satchel and read it aloud in a tavern by the university until the candles guttered; someone copied it in ink that night. A pilgrim bound for Compostela bought one to give, he said, to a priest in Paris, "so that he may know what the Church burned and then forgot."

Letters began to arrive at the shop addressed to "the printer of the colors," a name she had never claimed but could not refuse. She read them by lamplight, Pieter holding the page steady: a canon in Antwerp asking for the names of the struck-out; a widow in Leuven whose grandfather's name she thought she recognized among the gold crosses; a merchant in Paris who wanted, of all things, a map in his own colors — "vermilion for my house, if it please you, blue for my ships."

Margriet laughed at that one, and then cried a little, and then laughed again, and Pieter did not ask why.

Then a bookseller in Antwerp printed her account without asking leave. She heard of it thirdhand — a copy of the copy reaching Bruges, the typesetter's errors crawling through it like ants — and she held the sheet and felt the strange sensation of her own words having left her body and gone into the world to live without her.

She was not angry. She had printed the truth to be read, and it was being read. She wrote to the bookseller, not to reproach him but to correct the errors, and signed it with her name, and sent the letter, and felt, for the first time, that she was a person the world could answer.

The erased century was no longer silent. It was a rumor, then a question, then a subject of argument in half the cities of the Low Countries. In Ghent, in a convent scriptorium, a nun read the news in a smuggled sheet and pressed it to her heart.

Margriet sat in the workshop at dusk and watched the color of the sky like azurite going grey, and thought: this is what a page does. It goes where I cannot. It remembers when I am silent.

Chapter 43: The Scholar's Word

The canon of St. Donatian came to the shop himself, which no one of his rank had done, and stood in the doorway with his scholar's hands clasped in front of him as if he were presenting a question.

He was old — older than Jan had been — with the yellowed look of a man who had spent his life among manuscripts and candlelight. Margriet set out a chair and a cup of beer and asked what he wanted.

"I have read your page," he said. "The account of the erasure. I have read your map, and I have been told there is a letter of witness from a sister in Ghent."

Margriet stood very still.

"I will not ask to see the letter," he said, "unless you choose to show it. But I have spent forty years among the archives of the south, and I will tell you what I know."

He told her of Montségur, of the fall of the mountain, of the fire that followed — he had seen the records in Toulouse, the ones the Inquisition had not burned. He spoke of the names struck from charters, the families erased from memory, the books fed to the flames by men who believed, sincerely, that they were saving souls.

"You have restored," he said, "what the Church spent a century making unremembered. I am a canon of this church, and I will tell you plainly: the erasure was the crime. The memory is the faith."

She brought out the manuscript then — the woven chronicle in Occitan and Flemish, the struck-out names — and laid it before him. He read for an hour without speaking, his finger moving under the lines, his lips shaping the old words.

When he finished he asked for pen and ink, and wrote, in a hand so careful it looked engraved: I, Lambertus, canon of St. Donatian in Bruges, having examined the chronicle and the map and the letter of witness, declare the record true and the names belonging to history. The erasure is itself the deed to be recorded. He signed it and sealed it with his ring.

Margriet looked at the seal, and then at him. "The Church will not like it."

"The Church," he said, "has a long memory, and it is ashamed of some of it. This page will be there when I am not. Let it speak for both of us."

He left the sheet in her hands, and she carried it into the shop and laid it beside the map, and thought: now the Church's own learning speaks for the erased.

Chapter 44: The Color's Meaning

She had printed the map so many times the plates were growing soft in the lines, and the thought came to her one evening, in the smell of oil and the creak of the press, that the colors had never been explained.

People saw the red and the blue and the gold and wondered; they hung the map on walls and paid for it; but how many of them knew what the color meant? She had known the truth of it since the cellar in Ghent, since the night the gold pass revealed the whole, and she had never once set it down in print.

She drew the key on scrap paper first, in the dark of the shop, the way she used to draw the map from memory when she was Maeyken and had no press. Vermilion for the blood of the erased — the red of the routes they fled along, the red of the fires that chased them. Azurite for the water, the rivers and the canals and the grey-blue sea, the ways of refuge, the ways of flight. And gold for what the Church had gilded over and then struck out — the towns, the houses, the small gold crosses at the doors where a traveler slept one night and was fed and sent on, the names that the strikeout had tried to kill.

Black ink can lie, she wrote in the margin of her draft, and her hand paused. It can strike a name from a register and call it justice. The color can only remember.

She set the key the next morning, and the words of it, and printed it. The sheet was plain where the map was beautiful — a page of explanation, a legend — and she took one copy wet from the press and held it to the light, and thought: now the meaning is as legible as the map.

Pieter read it over her shoulder and said, "It is a sermon."

"It is a page," she said. "A page is my only pulpit."

She printed a hundred and fifty copies. She thought, in the rhythm of the press, about the words she had set — about what the color was, and what it meant. The erasure had been the heresy: the century of striking out, of burning, of teaching children not to ask. And the memory — the restored names, the gold crosses, the routes drawn in red across the water in blue — the memory was the faith.

The press creaked like a heartbeat. She pulled the last sheet, and the ink on her hands was red and blue and gold where the colors had kissed the paper and left their mark on her.

"That," she said to the empty shop, "is what the color means."

Chapter 45: The Map's Journey

In the spring a merchant took a copy south — she never knew his name, only that he carried cloth and asked for the map folded small enough to hide in a bale. She wrapped it in oiled cloth against the damp of the road and gave it to him with her own hands, and he looked at her stained fingers and said, "It will find its way," and she believed him.

Months passed. Summer came to Bruges, the canals going green and slow, the market loud with a hundred languages. She did not think of the copy often; it had gone into the world like all the others, into the great dark of the world, and she had learned not to hold her breath for what came back.

Then, in the autumn, a letter found her.

It came from the south, from a village she had never heard of, in the foothills of the mountains. The hand was old and trembling; the ink was poor; the words were in a French that was not quite French, with the old flat vowels of Languedoc.

We are the family of the weaver of V—. Our grandfather told us of the Good Men who slept in the wool loft in the winter of the burning year. We thought it a fairy tale. My son brought home your paper from the fair, and we saw the gold crosses, and we saw the struck-out names, and we saw our own name — the name our grandfather said was taken from the register — and we wept, my wife and I, over a page.

She read it twice, standing in the doorway of the shop, the letter shaking in her ink-stained hands. Then she read it a third time, slowly, to make the words stay.

The map had returned to the places it remembered. It had gone south along the routes it drew, into the mountains, to the wool lofts and the hearths where the Good Men had slept — and the remnant, wherever it survived, was no longer erased. A name had been given back to a family that had been taught to forget it.

She did not tell Pieter at once. She went into the press room and laid the letter under the plates, under the oiled cloth, where the memory was kept. Then she stood a long time with her hand on the copper, feeling its cold patience, and thought: this is why we print. Not for the money, not for the name. For this. For the name given back.

The bells rang Compline. She went to find Pieter, to show him the letter, to let him read what the map had done.

Chapter 46: The Anniversary

A year since Jan's death. A year since the snow on the Leie, since the tallow candle, since the hand that had taught her to set a forme went cold in hers.

Margriet did not go to mass for him. She went to the church in the dark, before Lauds, when the only light was the sanctuary lamp and the smell of wax and old stone, and she lit a candle at the side altar for the soul of Jan van den Bossche, master printer, who was afraid and who gave her the truth anyway, in his death, the way a man gives a child a knife.

Then she walked back through the cold streets to the shop and did what she had been planning for a month: she printed a memorial sheet in color.

She set it herself. The vermilion plate for his name, set red; the azurite for the border, the thin blue line like a river; the gold for the small cross she placed beside his name, the same cross the map drew at every sheltering house. And then the other names — the names from the chronicle, the struck-out names, the weavers and the wool-lofts and the ones who had sheltered travelers for a night and a meal and been erased for it. She printed them all in gold, restored, the way the gold plate restored them.

The press creaked like a breath. She pulled the sheet and held it up to the candle, and the colors came off the copper onto the paper the way they had come off that first night in Ghent — red, blue, gold, in register — and she thought of Jan's face in the candlelight, and of the fear in it, and of the secret he had carried for twenty-five years like a coal in his chest.

Pieter came in from the docks, smelled the oil, and stood in the doorway without speaking.

"I printed his name," she said.

He came and looked at the sheet, at the red and the gold, at the cross beside Jan's name. After a long moment he said, "He would have wept."

"He never once printed in color in twenty years," she said. "He taught me what to print. I printed it. That is the whole of it."

She hung the sheet in the window of the shop, where the morning would find it, and they stood together in the dark with the press settling around them like a body breathing.

"Come," she said. "We have work. The dead would want the living at the press."

And the press creaked, and the candle guttered, and she set the next forme.

Chapter 47: The Book

She began the book in the autumn, when the light went early and the shop was warm with the press's labor, and she set the first sheet herself.

Not a page of the map, not a broadsheet, but a book — the whole story in order, the color and the map and the erased century and the flight and the free city, with the map and the key as its heart. She wrote the copy in her own hand first, in the evenings, filling the margins with corrections, crossing out and writing again, the ink of her own words drying on her fingers. Pieter read each page as she set it, slowly, his lips moving, and asked her questions she had to think about to answer, and she loved him for the asking.

The book took shape sheet by sheet. She chose the paper carefully — good Meuse paper, thick, that would take the color without bleeding — and she paid for it out of the map's earnings, and she did not count the cost. She set the title page with her own hands, in black and red: The Routes of the Good Men, Restored to Memory, and beneath it her name, Margriet van der Meer, of Ghent, printer.

Her name. Her own name, in print, of her own setting.

She looked at it a long time. She had been Maeyken in Bruges, hidden, borrowed; she had been an apprentice in Ghent, a woman without a guild, a shop without a master, a fugitive with a proof wrapped in oiled cloth. And here was her name, set by her own hand, on a page that would outlive her.

The press creaked like a heartbeat. She pulled the sheet and held it to the light.

"It is a voice," she said aloud, to the empty shop, "made permanent. A woman's voice, printed, in colors, in her own name. Let them burn it; the press can make it again. Let them burn the press; the page remains. Let them burn the page; it has already been read."

She did not know she was crying until the ink blurred on her wrist. She wiped her face with the back of her hand and set the second sheet.

The book would take months. The book would take all her care. She was in no hurry. She had waited her whole life to print this page, and it was only the first.

Chapter 48: The Legacy

She came with her mother, the weaver's wife, on a bright morning in winter, and she stood in the doorway of the shop with her hands clasped behind her back and looked at everything — the press, the forme, the ink stones, the drying sheets — the way a cat looks at a place it has decided to live.

Lijsbette was fourteen, with a face like a loaf of bread, plain and good, and eyes that went wide and bright when she saw the color proofs hanging by the window.

"My mother says you print with colors," she said. "Is it true?"

Margriet held out a proof of the key page. The girl took it in both hands as if it were glass, and traced the gold cross with her finger, and said, "Who does this cross belong to?"

"A man who was erased," said Margriet. "A man the Church made unremembered. The color brings him back."

Lijsbette looked up. "Can I learn to do that?"

It was the whole of the question, and the whole of the answer. Margriet took the girl into the shop and set her to work — not the press, not yet, but the letters, the ink, the slow discipline of the compositor's stick, teaching her the shapes and the names of things: forme, chase, tympan, frisket. The girl learned with her hands first, like Reine's son, and her hands were soon as stained as Margriet's own, and she was proud of the stain, the way a soldier is proud of a scar.

She told her, as Jan had told her, that a press is a body — its creak is a breath, its thump a heartbeat — and Lijsbette listened with her whole face and said, "Then when it stops, it dies."

"Yes," said Margriet. "And that is why we must teach it to speak while it lives."

She taught her the price of printing, too. She showed her the letter from the south, the name given back to a family that had forgotten it. She showed her the proof of the map, wrapped in oiled cloth, and told her what it had cost — the press, the shop, the city, the name hidden in a borrowed life.

Lijsbette listened, and asked no questions, and at the end of the day she said, "I will carry the ink, if you will teach me the rest."

Margriet looked at her, at the girl's dark-eyed seriousness, and saw the craft passing hand to hand, woman to girl, the way it had passed from Jan to her, the way it would pass from her to this child.

The legacy was not the press. The legacy was the printed word, and the courage to print it.

Chapter 49: The Copper, Kept

The letter came in the dead of winter, sealed with the seal of the Inquisition's office in Tournai, and the messenger who brought it would not meet her eyes.

She read it standing in the doorway, the cold coming in around her, Pieter and Lijsbette silent behind her. It was written in the careful lawyer's Latin of the bishop's court, and it said: the copper plates formerly the property of Jan van den Bossche, deceased, are claimed as church property, by right of the old order, and the bearer is instructed to receive them.

She read it twice. Then she folded it, very precisely, and put it in her sleeve, and went to her desk.

Pieter said, "Margriet."

"I know," she said.

She wrote her answer that afternoon, in her own hand, in her own name, and she signed it and set her seal to it — the small stamp she had made, an M and a press, in copper. She wrote: The plates were given to me by Meester Jan van den Bossche in his testament, in the presence of the guild, and they are lawfully mine. They are not church property. They have never been church property. They were hidden from the Church for twenty-five years, and I will not surrender them now. If the Church wants them, let it come and take them; I will be at the press.

Pieter read it and said nothing for a long moment. Then he said, "You have written a defiance."

"No," she said. "A refusal. Defiance is loud. This is quiet, and it will outlast them."

When the messenger returned, she gave him the letter and no more, and watched him ride away through the falling snow.

Then she took the plates out one last time.

She warmed the ink, ground the pigments fresh, and printed the map — the final printing, the plates worn soft in the lines now, the red and the blue and the gold coming up onto the paper like a memory rising. Lijsbette worked the press and Margriet pulled the sheets, and the press creaked like a heartbeat, and Pieter stood guard at the door.

When the last sheet was dry, she wiped the plates clean with oiled wool, one by one, feeling their cold weight in her hands — the metal that had held the memory, the copper that remembered. Then she wrapped them in oiled cloth, as Jan had kept them, and carried them to the floor of the workshop, and lifted the loose board, and laid them down.

The memory kept. The copper kept. Kept, not surrendered.

She let the board fall back into place and stood, and the shop was quiet, and she thought of Jan in his death, and of Koenraad somewhere, patient, writing letters, and she thought: let him come. The plates are where they have always been — under the floor, in the dark, waiting to be printed again.

Chapter 50: The Printed Word

The book was finished on a morning in late autumn, when the light lay gold on the canal and the bells of Bruges were ringing Prime.

They had worked through the night, all three of them — Margriet at the press, Pieter at the forme, Lijsbette inking the plates with a steady hand — and the last sheet was the colophon. She had set it herself, the night before, choosing each word the way a mother chooses a name.

Printed by Margriet van der Meer, of Ghent, in the year of Our Lord 1474, in ink of the three colors, from copper that remembers.

She pulled the final page, and the colors came up onto the paper — red, blue, gold, in perfect register, the vermilion bright as blood, the azurite deep as the winter sea, the gold small and patient at the margins — and she held the sheet in her ink-stained hands and looked at it for a long time.

Outside, Bruges rang with bells. The market was filling with voices in a hundred languages. A peddler was calling out the map; a priest was denouncing it; a child was asking its mother what the gold crosses meant. The whole loud, living, changing city, full of print now — her print — going out into the world to be read and argued over and carried south and north.

Lijsbette set the next forme under her eye, her small fingers sure among the letters. Pieter turned the handle of the press, and the press creaked — a breath, a heartbeat, a voice clearing its throat — and the screw came down, and the platen pressed, and another page was born.

Margriet looked at her hands. The ink was in the lines of them, the lampblack and the red and the blue, the badge of the trade, the stain she had worn since she was seventeen and Jan had first set her to work. The stain would not wash out. She did not want it to.

She thought of Koenraad, somewhere, patient, courteous, certain, writing letters that would arrive in the spring. The Church had not stopped erasing. It would never stop erasing. There would always be a name to strike out, a book to burn, a door to watch.

But this page was printed. This page was finished, and bound, and real, and it remembered.

"The book is done," she said, and her voice was steady.

Lijsbette looked up from the forme. "What will we print now?"

Margriet looked at her — at the girl's stained hands, at the press settling like a body breathing, at the morning light on the wet ink — and she smiled, the first time in a long while that the smile reached her eyes.

"The next one," she said. "And the next. There will always be a next one."

The press creaked. The bells rang. The page dried in the light, and the printed word endured.
