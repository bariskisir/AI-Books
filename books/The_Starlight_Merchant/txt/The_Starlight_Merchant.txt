The Starlight Merchant
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Star Shop

The jars were glass, and the glass was the sound of the shop. Selin rubbed each one with a cloth until it gave back the lamplight — thin and yellow, because everything in the shop was meant to be thin and yellow, everything except what the jars held.

Rows of them, shelf upon shelf, four hundred and some, she had counted a hundred times: each a dome of glass over a dim star. They burned low, low as coals under ash. Some had burned low for decades. The shop kept them in the dark by design; light is easier to price when it has forgotten it was ever whole.

She worked the cloth down a jar's curve, and the star inside rolled over, sluggish, and did not sing. None of the for-sale stars sang to her. They had been dimmed so long that they had learned not to be heard.

Above them all, on the highest shelf, beyond a ladder's last reach, stood one jar that had never been priced and never been sold. Selin did not look at it directly, ever. She felt it instead, the way you feel a fire through a wall — a warmth that was the shop's true weather. It had sat there seventeen years, the whisper said. It was why customers came to look and left with other jars. It was the shop's prize and its secret and, some said, its owner's only unbought thing.

In her chest, under her ribs, in the place where she had always kept a cold shape — a shape the size of a missing thing — the ache flared at the sight of the shelf. She had grown used to it the way one grows used to a stone in a shoe: never comfortable, never forgotten.

She polished the last jar, the one with the chip in its rim. Then she set down her cloth and did what she did every evening. She stood beneath the highest shelf, tilted her face up, and let the warmth fall on her for the length of a held breath.

Behind her the shop door opened. Master Halv came in with the sound of keys — a whole ring of them, the keys to every jar and drawer and room — and he walked his counting, his eyes moving along the rows the way hers had, but where her eyes counted to be sure, his counted to own.

"You'll dim the lamp," he said. He did not look at her.

She stepped away from the shelf and turned back to her cloth, and in the turning she saw it: the high jar, unlit a moment before, had brightened. A pulse of it, like a heart. Like a greeting.

It had done this every night for as long as she could remember. Halv had never once turned his head to see.

Chapter 2: Velia

Velia was a city that lived under glass. The dome arched over it like a bell of cold light, miles across, held up by ribs of iron the color of old weather, and under that bell the city arranged itself by brightness the way water arranges itself by weight.

The terraces of the rich climbed the southern slopes, their windows squares of generous lamplight, their streets pale stone that caught the glow and gave it back. The guild tower rose in the center, needle-thin, its walls hung with signs in red and gold — the red of sales, the gold of licenses — and at its top, ringed in glass, sat the chamber where the registry was kept, where every birth-star was written down like a debt.

The poor quarter lay low against the northern wall, where the dome came down to meet the earth and the light never reached. Selin crossed its edge on an errand, carrying a parcel of wrapped jars for a collector who had paid for them without once asking what was inside. The streets there ran dim and damp. People walked with their heads down, as people do under a sky they cannot reach.

Selin had grown up in that dimness. She knew its exact shade: the color of a candle seen through smoke, the color of a room where a fire used to be. It was not the shop's thin yellow, and it was not the terrace gold. There were as many kinds of dim as there were reasons for it.

She stopped at the crossing where the dome came lowest, where the ribs met the pavement, and looked up.

Above the glass the stars pressed — hundreds, thousands, each one flattened against the dome's curve like moths at a lamp, trying to get in, none of them able. She had seen them every night of her life, and still the sight worked on her chest like a hand.

A person's star, the sermon said, waits where the sky can see it. It cannot come down. It cannot be brought. Only taken.

The cold shape in her chest shifted, sharpened, as if something in the sky had called its name.

"Move, girl," said a carter, and she moved, and the parcel of jars clinked softly, four dim lights in a box, and she walked on through the city of light and half-light, carrying other people's stars, and the ache under her ribs kept time with her steps all the way to the shop door.

Chapter 3: The Apprentice

She had been twelve when Halv came down to the poor quarter and stood in her mother's doorway, and she had been small and fierce and already cold inside, and she had heard it: in the parcels and jars he carried, under the wadded cloth, the stars. Singing. Dimly, the way a voice sings at the bottom of a well, but singing, each one, its own song.

Nobody else heard them. She had tested it, standing close to the collectors and the priests and the guild clerks, and none of them heard anything; they heard glass, they said, and wind. But Selin had heard the stars all her life — the dim low ones of the poor quarter, and, from the day the shop took her in, the one bright one on the highest shelf.

That was why Halv had chosen her. He had never said so, but she knew, the way the stars knew her. Five years in the trade now, five years of counting and polishing and wrapping, five years of learning the prices and the seasons and the red stamps. She had heard the other apprentices say it in the yard: "He hears nothing. She hears them. That's the whole trick of it."

She had made that pride her own, small and hard as a stone. Halv's rules she kept exactly, because pride is a discipline: no touching the jars above the second shelf; no asking about the high one; no names. Stars had names, and the rule was that you did not learn them, because a named star is a person, and a person cannot be sold.

She had never broken the rules. Not once in five years.

That evening, as the shop's lamps came down, Halv finished his counting and turned to her with his coat full of keys.

"Tomorrow," he said, "you will see the harvest."

The words landed in her chest like a coin dropped on stone.

"What do I bring?" she asked.

"Nothing," he said. "You bring your ears. For once, they may be useful."

He went up the stairs, keys ringing, and Selin stood alone in the shop, and under the highest shelf the Warm Star turned in its jar and brightened — a slow, deliberate warmth, like a face turning to look at her. She did not look at it directly. She never did.

But tomorrow she would climb to the dome's crown and see a star taken. And she thought, with a fierceness that surprised her: I will hear it. I will hear the song it sings when it is caught.

Chapter 4: The Bottled Light

The customer came at noon, as customers did — when the shop's thin lamps were at their palest, the better to show the jars against the dim.

He was a collector. Selin knew the type at a glance: the velvet collar, the ring, the way he walked past the small stars without slowing, heading for the wall where the strong lights were kept. He did not look at them as lights. He looked at them as a man looks at a row of locked boxes.

"Show me the Anderley one," he said. His voice was flat, and it made the shop colder.

Selin fetched the jar from the second shelf — an old star, a steady one, taken from a girl in the seed-merchant's row forty years ago, or so the ledger said, sold twice since, returned once, sold again. She set it on the counter and did not look at the star itself. That was the rule for sales. You do not look at the light when it is being priced; a star on a counter is stock.

The collector held the jar up, and the star, sensing him, did what stars do when the wrong person holds them: it dimmed. The warmth drew in on itself, folded small, went grey. It was not a refusal it could voice. A dimmed star simply cannot brighten for a stranger, and a jar is a kind of blindness.

"Faint," the collector said, with satisfaction, as if faintness were a flaw he had paid to see. He named a price. Selin, trained, did not blink. She wrapped the jar — the paper, the cord, the shop's seal — and the star inside the wrappings sang once, a note so small and cold it might have been the complaint of glass.

She entered the sale in the ledger. A line, a price, a red stamp, pressed down with the guild's weight until the ink bled through the page. The ledger said the star was a thing that had changed hands. The red stamp said the sky belonged to the licensed, and the licensed had sold.

The collector took the parcel. "Tell your master I found it wanting," he said, "and that's how I like them."

The door closed. The bells of Velia, far off, struck the hour.

Selin stood at the counter, and the cold shape in her chest turned over, the way a sleeper turns from a light. She had wrapped stars before. Hundreds. She had never once heard one sing at the leaving.

From the parcel, through the door, past the glass, came the weeping — soft, thread-thin, the sound of a light going out on purpose. It went down the street with the collector, fading, and Selin stood in the dim shop until the last of it was gone.

She looked down at her hands. They were not cold. They had held the jar.

Then why, she thought, does it feel like I was the one sold?

Chapter 5: The Warm Star

The shop at night was a different country. The lamps were out, and the jars were the only light — the dim company of the for-sale stars, breathing low, and above them all, the high jar, warm as a coal.

Selin had come back after lock-up to fetch her apron. Then she had stood at the foot of the ladder, and the cold shape in her chest had leaned, the way water leans toward a drain, and she had climbed.

It was forbidden. The rule was no touching above the second shelf. She had never broken it, not once in five years, and she broke it now with her whole body, until her face was level with the highest shelf and the jar stood before her.

The star was brighter than she had ever known it. Up close it was not a light but a presence, a small contained sunrise, warm the way a hand is warm, warm the way a name is warm. It turned in its jar, slow, and she had the staggering sense that it had been waiting for her face to arrive at exactly this height.

"Hello," she whispered. Her voice cracked. She had not said a word to it in five years; the rule had been no names, no asking, and she had kept it like a vow.

The star flared. A pulse of white-gold, so bright the other jars woke and turned toward her, and the ache in her chest opened like a struck bell. It knew her. It had always known her. The shop's prize, the never-sold, the seventeen-year secret — and it burned for her the way a heart burns for its body.

"Selin."

The voice came from the foot of the ladder. Halv, in his dark velvet coat, his keys gone quiet in his pocket, his face unreadable in the starlight.

She came down without a word. The week that followed was scrubbing — the jars, the counters, the floor, the stone of the yard — until her hands were raw and her pride was a thin, worn thing. He said nothing about the ladder. He did not have to. The punishment was the scrubbing and the silence, and she served both.

But the feeling would not leave. It lived under her ribs like a second pulse. The warmth on the high shelf had known her name without being told it. And the cold shape in her chest had fit itself to that warmth as a key fits a lock it was made for.

On the seventh night, her hands still raw, she stood again beneath the shelf — not climbing, just standing, the way she had every night for five years.

The star turned toward her. Brightened. Waited.

She shaped the question she had never been allowed to ask, so soft it was barely breath.

"Are you mine?"

The star flared — a full, blazing answer — and the cold shape in her chest, for one impossible moment, felt warm.

Chapter 6: The Feeling

After the scrubbing came the noticing.

The star on the high shelf had become a weather. It brightened when Selin entered the shop — a slow swell of warmth, unmistakable to anyone who cared to look, and Halv did not look. It dimmed when she stepped back into the yard, a receding tide. The other apprentices began to whisper, and Selin stopped listening to them and began listening to the star.

The dreams came on the week's third night. She dreamed of a gate — the city's north gate, huge and iron-dark — and of a basket, and of an infant wrapped in a shawl, and over all of it a sky pouring with light, a sky that split in two, one piece going up into the dome and one piece going down, into a jar, and a coin passing from a thin hand to a tired one. She woke with the cold shape in her chest aching in a new way: not a hollow, but the negative of a shape, the exact shadow of a thing removed that the body still expected.

She began to ask questions. It was the one thing the trade did not allow, and she asked them anyway, low, in the gaps of the day.

"Where do the gate-born come from?" she asked the senior apprentice.

"The gate," he said. "What do their stars do?"

He looked at her strangely. "Stars do what stars do, Selin."

"Who buys at the gate?"

"The licensed," he said, and his face closed like a shop at dusk.

She asked the bottler, who said the dome was full and the sky was empty and did she want her wages or the sermon. She asked the clerk at the guild counter, a thin man with red stamp-ink under his nails.

"Gate-born," the clerk said, not looking up. "Bought at the gate, born at the gate, sold the first hour. It's in the registry. Half the poor quarter's that way."

"And the other half?" Her voice came out too eager, and she tried to flatten it.

The clerk looked up and studied her — the shop apron, the sharp face, the steady hand — and something in his own face shut, a door closing on a room she was not meant to see.

"That one," he said slowly, "was bought at the gate. Seventeen years ago. First hour. That's all the registry says, and that's all I know."

He turned back to his ledger. But not before Selin saw it: his hand had shaken, once, on the pen.

She walked back through the dim streets with the words beating time with her steps. Bought at the gate. Seventeen years ago. First hour. The cold shape in her chest had a name now, or the edge of a name.

She knew, suddenly, completely, the way she knew the songs of stars: something had been taken from her, and the whole city had been holding it for her, on a high shelf, in a jar, for seventeen years.

Chapter 7: The Trade

The trade had a shape, and Halv taught it to her in pieces, the way a man hands coins one at a time.

Bottling. A star came down from the dome in a jar and never left one. The jar was the trade — the glass, the seal, the cork, the way the neck was made to hold a light that wanted to fly. Pricing. There was a science to it: age, brightness, the station of the person it had been taken from, the fashion of the season. A noble's star sold for what a house paid for a year of keeping. A gate-born's star sold for what a loaf cost, if anyone bothered to sell it at all. The ledger. Every sale, every red stamp, every star's history written in Halv's neat hand, each line a life folded into a number.

Harvest seasons came with the cold — the long nights when the stars pressed hardest at the glass and the crews went up. The auction came twice a year in the guild hall under the tower, and the whole city went to watch the light change hands.

"A star," Halv said once, a jar in his hands, "is worth what someone will pay for it. That's the whole creed. Learn it and you'll never be poor. Forget it and you'll be the poor."

Selin learned it. She learned the prices, the seasons, the stamps, the way a collector's cold pleasure could be read in the flatness of his voice. And she learned, in the practice of the trade, that the stars settled for her.

It was not something she did on purpose. A star came in from a harvest, weeping, wrung, and the senior apprentices handled it with hooks and cloths, and it went on weeping. But when Halv, testing her, put a fresh jar in her hands, the star went quiet. Not dimmer. Settled. Like a frightened bird finding a fist it could trust. She held it, and its light steadied, and the ache in her chest answered with a low, warm note she did not understand.

Halv watched. She felt his watching the way she felt the stars — a pressure of attention, warm and cold at once. He had a strange hunger, that look; it was the look of a man counting a sum he could not quite make right.

"Girl," he said, when she set the settled star back on its shelf, "you have a gift."

She waited. Praise from Halv was like gold from a miser: rare, and heavy.

"I wonder," he said, turning away, keys ringing, "whose it is."

The words followed her up the stairs and into the dim of her room. She lay awake, and she thought of the clerk's closing face, and the registry, and the high shelf, and she did not sleep well, because she was beginning to understand that in the trade, a gift was a thing that could be owned.

Chapter 8: The Harvest

The harvest was a climb, and the climb was the trade with its clothes off.

Halv's crew went up at the third bell of the long night, when the city was asleep and the dome's glass ran with cold. There were six of them — the hooks man, the jars man, the license man, two bearers, and Halv — and Selin went among them, as he had said, with her ears and her hands held tight against her sides.

The dome's flank was wind and ice. They climbed the iron ribs, the lamps of the Watch swinging far below, the glass drumming under their boots, and Selin climbed with the cold shape in her chest burning, because she was going to see a star taken, and she could not have said whether she wanted it or feared it.

At the skylight ring, the sky lay open beyond the glass. Not the pressing moths of the street's view, but the real stars — huge, near, each one a person's light hung up where it could be seen — and the crew did what the crew did.

The hooks man fitted his hook. The jars man readied a jar. The license man said the rite words, the words of the charter, "the licensed own the sky," in a bored voice, the way a man's voice is bored when he says the words that let him take.

The hook went up. It caught, among the lights, one star — a small one, a girl's star, trembling at the glass where its person slept in the terraces — and it pulled.

Selin saw the moment the star understood. It did not scream; stars cannot scream. It sang — a note so high and so broken that the cold shape in her chest turned to water — and it came down the way a leaf comes down in wind, fighting, into the jar, and the cork went home, and the light inside went grey.

"That's a clean one," the hooks man said, and the crew laughed. The sound of men who had done it a thousand times and would do it again.

Selin held herself still. Five years of rules had taught her stillness. She did not speak, did not flinch. She watched the girl's light, jarred, dim, and she heard it weeping — the same sound as the collector's star, but younger, higher, a song that had never once been a sale.

The descent was the climb reversed, all wind and dark. In the yard the crew dispersed. Halv went in without a word. Selin, alone, touched the girl's jar with one finger.

The cold came up through her fingertip like a needle. Not the cold of glass, not the cold of the night. A person's cold. The exact temperature of a light told it is a thing.

She had seen the trade from the inside now, and the inside was this: a girl asleep in the terraces, dreaming, and her light in a jar, and six men paid, and a red stamp to come.

And somewhere in her chest the cold shape turned over, and she thought of the high shelf, and the warmth that had known her name, and she understood what was waiting for her at the top of the ledger.

Chapter 9: The License

The guild tower was the law made tall. It rose above the city, needle-thin, and the registry slept at its top in a glass room that never went dark, and the law that lived there was one sentence, framed in gold, hung where every clerk could see it and every merchant could feel it:

The licensed own the sky.

Selin went up the tower on an errand, and she went up it as a merchant, with the shop's accounts in a folder and the red seal in her pocket, and she did what she had done a hundred times: she asked to see the registry.

Not the sales ledger — that was the shop's. The registry. The book of the born, the book that recorded every birth-star in Velia, written at the hour of birth, read at the hour of sale.

The clerk, a new one, younger than the man who had closed his face at the counter, brought it to the window and set it down. "Gate pages are in the back," he said. "Nobody asks for the gate pages."

"Nobody," said Selin, and turned to them.

She had her birthday by heart — the date, the hour, the story of the gate, the passage, the family that had come in through the iron door with nothing but each other and a baby, and the story always stopped there, just before the what.

The entry was there. It had been there seventeen years, waiting for her eyes.

Selin, born at the gate, first hour, gate-born. Her name in neat clerk's hand, the date, the hour, the quarter where the family had settled. And then, in the margin, in a red so dark it was almost black, a stamp.

SOLD.

And beneath it, in a buyer's hand, the name.

Halv.

The tower was very quiet. The clerk said something, about the hour, about closing. The words did not reach her. She read the line again — SOLD. Halv. — and the red of the stamp seemed to burn, the way a hot thing burns when you are too close not to look.

Seventeen years. The shop. The high shelf. The jar that had never been priced, never been sold. The warmth that had known her name. The shop's prize, the shop's secret. Her.

He had not sold it. He had kept it all those years, and she had stood beneath it every night, and it had burned for her, and he had counted the rest of the inventory with a merchant's eyes and never once looked at the one jar that was not for sale because it had already been bought — bought at a gate, for a passage, for a pittance, in her first hour of light.

She closed the registry. Her hands were steady. The practice of the trade was in her, and it held her upright while the tower swayed.

"Thank you," she said to the clerk, and her voice was a merchant's voice, and she went down the stairs, and in the pit of her chest the cold shape had a name now, and the name was written in red, and it was the name of the man who had taught her the trade.

Chapter 10: The Poor Quarter

The poor quarter lay where the dome met the earth, and the dome had been taking from it for so long that the dimness was in the stones.

Selin walked its streets the day after the registry, and the streets knew her: the stalls where she had begged for crusts, the corner where she had first heard a star singing in a jar behind a shutter, the doorway where her mother had stood the day Halv came. It had rained, and the quarter smelled of wet wool and cold hearths, the smell of rooms where the light has been going out for generations.

The people of the quarter moved in half-life. They walked, they worked, they laughed at the pumps and argued at the thresholds, but their laughter did not quite rise, and their faces did not quite turn to the light, because each of them had given a piece of the sky away. Selin had grown up among them. She had been one of them. She had never once, in all her dim years, seen herself in them — until now.

Her family's door was the color of the quarter, grey and worn, with a latch she knew by heart. She knocked. It opened.

Her sister stood in the doorway, and her sister was a lamp. Bright, whole, burning with the star she had kept — the one the family had refused to sell, twice, in the years after the gate. She threw her arms around Selin, and the warmth of her was like standing too close to a fire, and Selin, who had gone cold in the registry, felt it like a wound.

Her father sat by the cold hearth. He looked up when she entered, and he said her name, low, and did not say anything else. She had never known her father to say very much. Now the silence had weight in it, the weight of a thing carried seventeen years.

Her mother came from the kitchen with a cloth in her hands, and she stopped, and she looked at Selin, and it was the look of the registry — the look of a woman who knows what is written in red ink about her own daughter.

On the shelf above the hearth stood an empty bottle. Not a star-jar — a plain bottle, stoppered, dustless, kept. Kept the way a wound is kept, the way a debt is kept. Selin had seen it all her childhood and never asked. Now she knew, with the whole weight of the registry, what it was: the shape of the thing they had sold, kept empty so the family would remember.

Her mother touched the bottle. She did it without looking at it, the way you touch a scar without looking at it. And she did not look at Selin.

The silence in the room was a fourth person.

"Mother," Selin said.

Her mother took her hand from the bottle, and put it at her side, and looked at the floor, where the light — her sister's light, the only light in the room — pooled and did not reach her.

Chapter 11: The Family

They ate in silence, the four of them, at a table that had never been large enough. Selin's mother set down bread and salt fish, and the lamp was thin and yellow — the cheap oil of the poor quarter, smoke-streaked, stealing from the air. Selin's sister sat across from her, bright as a struck match, and kept watching her with her head tilted.

"You're cold," her sister said. Not unkindly. Curiously, the way a child reads the weather. "Even in summer. Why are you always cold, Selin?"

The ache in Selin's chest turned over. Seventeen years it had lain there, a shape like a missing thing, and she had learned to carry it without naming it. "I don't know," she said. It had been true yesterday.

Her father said nothing. He ate, and his silence was a wall, but behind it something moved — a flinch, a hunted look, the way he kept his hands flat on the table as if they might confess without him.

Her mother's hands were not flat. They found the bottle. It sat on the shelf above the stove, empty, stoppered, dusted every day, kept for seventeen years like a thing that might be needed again. She lifted it down and turned it in her fingers, and the lamplight moved through the glass and found nothing to shine on.

"We came through the gate," her mother said at last, in a voice that had been rehearsed for years and never spoken. "Before you were born. We had nothing. There was a man there, and we paid for the city with something we shouldn't have." Her voice broke on the word something, and she set the bottle down as if it were heavy.

"Paid with what?" Selin asked. Her own voice was strange to her, high and careful. "Ma. What did we pay with?"

Her mother's eyes went to the shelf, to the bottle, to the door — anywhere but Selin's face. Her mouth opened and closed. Her tears came without sound, running down the furrows of her cheeks, and she shook her head. Not no. I cannot.

"Ask the one who holds it," she said.

The silence in the room was colder than the ache. Selin stood. On the shelf, the empty bottle glimmered, patient.

"I will," she said.

Chapter 12: The Sold Star

The shop was dark when she crept back in, but its darkness had light in it — every shelf a row of dim jars, every jar a sleeping star, breathing faint cold light into the glass. The Warm Star on the highest shelf burned softer than the rest, and as she passed beneath it, it brightened. It always did. She had told herself for five years that was a trick of the shelf, a draft, her imagination.

She found the oldest ledger in the back room, where the dust had made its own small hills. The first page was yellow and cracked, the ink faded, but the entries were legible: gate-side purchases, the merchant's first seasons, prices in the old coin. Near the bottom, in a hand she knew — Halv's hand, younger, faster — one entry: a birth-star, gate-born, first hour, passage-price. The sum beside it was a pittance. Less than the jar it was sealed in.

Seventeen years ago. The year she was born. The year her family came through the gate. The registry had told her the buyer's name; this page told her the price of her own light.

Her hands were shaking. She climbed the shelf ladder — the forbidden thing, the week of scrubbing it had cost her once — and the Warm Star watched her come, burning brighter with every rung, until the glass of its jar was warm against her fingertips and its light pooled in her palms like water.

"You know me," she whispered. It had always been true. She had felt it at twelve, standing where she stood now; had felt it dim as she stepped back, and brighten as she drew near, and sing faintly at the back of her hearing every night of her apprenticeship.

A star cannot lie about belonging.

The price they had paid was a pittance. The value was this — the cold shape in her chest, the missing thing, the ache that had followed her every day of her life like a customer to her own heart.

She held the jar. Her light. Hers.

"You're mine," she said.

The star flared. The whole shop woke at once, a hundred jars answering, and the Warm Star burned against the glass like a held breath released, like a voice calling its name across a room full of strangers.

Selin held it, and wept, and did not know whether the feeling was horror or wonder or both — her own light, on the shelf her whole life, singing to her, waiting for her to ask.

Chapter 13: The Truth

The guild tower kept its records in iron drawers, and the clerk who had slipped the words bought at the gate three weeks ago did not want to see her again. His face closed like a door when she asked for the file. But Selin had served the Starlight Merchant for five years, and she knew which questions make a clerk afraid, and she did not leave until the drawer was open.

The file was thin. A birth record. A gate-side transfer. A buyer's name. A red stamp. It was all of her, and it had been written before she could walk.

The clerk's pen hovered. "You understand what this is," he said. Not asking.

"Tell me."

He told her. At the gate, the hour she was born, her star had been taken through the skylight — quick, legal, licensed — and sold. The money had paid her family's passage into Velia. She had entered the city already dim; she had never once held her own light. The buyer was Master Halv. He had registered the purchase, stamped it in red, and kept the star unsold for seventeen years.

"He knew," she said.

The clerk hesitated, then nodded. "A merchant of his standing. He would have known the moment he read the registry." He did not say the rest, but it was in his face: that her light had hung on his highest shelf, and that Halv, who saw everything, had watched her dimness every day and said nothing.

Seventeen years. She had grown up a customer to her own warmth, borrowing light from jars in a shop she served. She had learned to soothe other stars with her hands, and had stood beneath her own and called it the shop's finest.

The ache in her chest had a name now. It was not cold, not a missing shape. It was theft — a thing done to her, taken from her, sold in red ink for passage money while she slept in the city it bought.

She walked out of the tower into the gray light of the guild square, and the city's bells rang the hour, indifferent. She put her hand to her chest, where the cold lived.

"They took my light," she said to the empty air, to the dome, to the stars pressing against the glass overhead, "and sold it."

Her voice held. It was the most honest thing she had ever said.

Chapter 14: The Betrayal

She waited until he slept. Halv slept like a man who had spent his warmth forty years ago — deeply, without dreams, the way the dim sleep. The empty house above the shop had no other sound but his breathing and the distant song of jars.

His office was a room of keys. Keys on hooks, keys in bowls, keys on the ring that never left his belt. She had polished them, counted them, never once asked what they opened. Now she worked through the desk drawers with steady hands and a cold bright heart.

The second drawer held a file. Her file — a copy of the guild's, annotated in Halv's hand. Notes in the margins: the gate, the hour, the passage-price. And one word, underlined, in the oldest ink: MINE.

The third drawer was the hoard. She had expected coin. She found a child's wooden star, painted gold and flaking. A lock of hair tied in ribbon. A torn corner of a red banner. Tickets to a festival no one remembered. Things he could not sell — not because they were worthless, but because they were his. The only warm things a man who could feel nothing had managed to hold.

She was still kneeling with the drawer open when the lamp came on.

Halv stood in the doorway in his shirt, the key ring in his hand, his face carved from cold. "I wondered when you'd find it."

"You knew." Her voice came out small. "At the gate. You bought it. You kept it."

"I kept it," he said, "because it pulled you. You think I chose you out of kindness?" His laugh was dry, splintered. "Seventeen years, and that star sang to you through the glass, and you never once asked whose light it was. You walked in here at twelve with the ache in your chest, and I saw my own."

"Your own what?"

He said nothing. The silence was the answer, and the loneliness in his face almost undid her. Then he said, coldly, "Careful, Selin. You're not a thief's apprentice. You're a thief's rival."

"You kept it," she said. "You knew it was mine, and you kept it on your shelf like a coin."

"It was worth something," he said.

Her fury rose up and burned the pity out of her. She crossed the room and took the key to the vault from his coat before he could move — a small brass key, warm from his body, fitting the lock he had once shown her, to the deep room under the shop.

"I'll take back what's mine," she said.

He did not stop her. He stood in the lamplight, diminished, and let her go.

Chapter 15: Master Halv

The buyer came on a rain-gray morning, a collector from the upper terraces with a velvet purse and a name that opened doors. He had heard, he said, of the shop's prize — the star on the highest shelf, the one that never dimmed, never slept, the one the merchant refused to price.

"Name it," the collector said. "Any figure. I will not haggle."

Halv was counting keys. He did not look up. "It is not for sale."

"Everything has a price, merchant."

"Then you have not met everything." Halv's voice was flat as water. He closed the counting-box and stood, and for a moment Selin saw it — the ghost of a man who could feel, moving behind the glass of his face. "Good day."

The collector left in fury. Selin stood in the dim shop and watched Halv climb the ladder to the highest shelf, watched him take down the Warm Star, watched him hold it. He did not open it. He simply stood there, jar in both hands, and the star's light fell on his thin face, his hollow eyes, his empty hands.

She had hated him for a week. It had been a clean, bright hatred, and it had felt like armor. But standing there she saw the truth of him, and the hatred cracked like ice: he was not hoarding light. He was holding it the way a man holds a hand he is too afraid to shake — because it is the only warm thing he has.

The empty house above the shop. The coins counted and recounted. The keys that opened nothing warm. Forty years, Selin thought. Forty years since he sold his own, and not one day of it had felt like this star in his hands. She saw it in the way he could not put it down.

"Master," she said, and the word came out different than she meant it — not the apprentice's title but something gentler.

He turned, and the crack in her picture of him widened. For a heartbeat he was not the Starlight Merchant, thief and hoarder and cold arithmetic. He was an old man trying to warm his hands at a star he did not dare to open.

Then the mask closed. "Finish the polishing," he said, and carried the jar to his rooms, and the door shut, and Selin stood alone among the dim jars and did not know what she believed anymore.

Chapter 16: The Lesson

The Guild Auction was held in the tower's great hall, and the bidders came wrapped in furs, their breath fogging the cold air as the lots were brought up — jars of dim light, each one a person, each one a price. Selin stood at Halv's shoulder and watched the trade she had been born into.

A gate-born star, second-hand, went for a house on the terraces. A child's star, harvested young, went for a fortune and a name. The bidders' faces were serene and hungry at once, and the stars in their jars wept faintly, singing to people who were not in the room, and no one listened.

"Watch the price," Halv said. "Watch what a thing is worth when someone wants it."

She watched. She watched a star dim as the gavel fell. She watched a woman in silver close her hand around a jar like a prize. She counted the coin, and she counted the cost, and by the end she understood the lesson he meant to teach, and understood also that it was wrong.

"Master," she said, as the hall emptied, "what was your own star worth?"

Halv went white. It was not a figure of speech — his face lost its color like a jar losing its light, all at once, and his hand went to his chest, to the place where warmth should live and did not. He looked at her as if she had struck him.

"Get out," he said. His voice shook. "Go. Now."

She went. But in the corridor she stopped, and the understanding came down over her like cold water: the trade makes thieves of everyone. Every price is a person. Every sale is a theft wearing a stamp. Halv had sold his own light for a license, and she had grown up in the shop that made that sale look holy.

She would not be a thief. She had sworn it in her mother's kitchen, and she meant it now more than ever. But she would not go to the registry, would not buy her own light back with the trade's money. The trade had made her what she was; she would not owe it the rest.

That night she stood in the shop's doorway and looked up at the dome, black and cold against the sky, its skylights glittering with the stars pressed against the glass, the vault of the city's bought-and-sold sky. Somewhere above, at the source, there was a star that had never been priced.

She would climb. She would take what she was owed — honest, at the source, where no law had been bent to call it hers.

"I'm coming," she told the dome, and her voice was small and fierce in the empty street. "To the top."

Chapter 17: The Climb

The dome rose out of Velia like a held breath, a hemisphere of glass a mile across cradling the city's bought sky. At night its flank was black and faintly luminous, the stars within pressing their dim light against the panes, and to climb it was forbidden, punishable, mad.

Selin climbed anyway.

She had learned the old ways from the street-sellers and the night-boys — handholds where the frame ribs joined, ledges the patching crews forgot, a ladder of iron no one guarded because no one was fool enough to use it. She was fool enough. She wore rope-soled shoes and a knotted line and a jar at her belt, and the wind came off the glass like a living thing, trying to unhand her.

Below, the Watch's lamps moved in their slow circles, small and far, the torches of men who never looked up. Above, the stars pressed at the glass — not the distant sky-stars beyond the dome, but the city's own, each one a person, each one a dim lamp in a jar, singing faintly. She could hear them. She had always been able to hear them, and on the dome's face their song was loud and strange, ten thousand small voices against the glass, calling to people who slept in the city below.

Her hands went numb with cold. Her breath fogged the panes. Halfway up, her foot found a pane where frost had made it slick, and she slipped — the world tilted, the Watch's lamps wheeled below, and she swung against the glass with a gasp, catching a rib by the skin of her fingers, the jar clattering against the dome like a knock.

She hung there, heart roaring, and the stars sang to her through the glass, and fury rose and filled her where fear had been. She was not going to fall. She was not going to be a dim girl in a dim city with a cold shape in her chest, polishing other people's light for the rest of her life. She was going to the top of the world, and the stars were going to see her.

She climbed.

The wind tore at her coat. The cold found every seam. Twice more she nearly lost her grip, and twice more she climbed on, the ache in her chest burning like a borrowed flame, the song of the stars around her like a crowd cheering her upward.

And then, impossibly, the curve of the dome flattened, and the skylight ring stood before her — the crown of the world, the place where the glass opened to the true sky, the rim of the biggest bottle in the world.

She pulled herself onto the ring and stood, shaking, on the top of Velia.

Below, the city slept in its dimness. Above, beyond the glass, the real stars blazed. And she was alone at the top of the world, with the open night at her hand and her own stolen light burning in her chest like a vow.

Chapter 18: The Sky-Dome

The skylights were the dome's scars, ring after ring of them around the crown, each a round pane on a hinge, each one a door in the cage. Selin crouched in their shadow, and wonder came over her like a wave and almost knocked the cold out of her.

The true sky. Not the dome's glass with its dim pressing stars — the actual night, open and endless, the real stars blazing beyond the reach of any jar. She had never seen them like this. From the city they were rumor, myth, a merchant's tale. From here they were a fire in every direction, uncountable, unowned, singing.

She could hear them. She had thought the song was only for the bottled stars, the dim ones, the stolen. But out there the song was vast and free, and it was not a weeping. It was a greeting.

Below, a Watch horn sounded — close. She flattened herself against the glass, heart hammering. A patrol crossed the near terrace, lamps swinging, boots on stone, and she counted their steps until they passed, and the cold of the panes bit through her coat, and she did not move until the sound was gone.

The rite words came to her then, unbidden — the guild's harvest liturgy, learned at Halv's side, words that named a star a thing and a person a source and a price a law: By the charter, the licensed own the sky; by this hook, by this jar, I take what is stamped mine.

She hated them. She felt them in her mouth like a theft already done. The trade had made her a thief by teaching her its words; she would not be a thief now, not here, not even honest at the source.

She opened her hands. Empty hands, no hook, no jar lifted in command. She spoke her own words, the first words that had ever been hers in the sky's presence:

"Come to me, if you choose."

The sky was silent. The cold burned. Below, the Watch's lamps went their slow way, and she thought she had failed, that no wild thing would come for a dim girl on a dome —

And a star moved.

Small, bright, fierce, it broke from the great wheel of its sisters and drifted across the black, and it came to her side of the glass and hovered there, watching her through the pane like a bird deciding whether to land.

It was wild. It had never been bound, never bottled, never priced. It looked at her — she could feel it looking, warm and curious and utterly unowned — and she did not dare to breathe.

Chapter 19: The Harvest, Illegal

The skylight was heavy with cold. She lifted it on its hinge, and the open night rushed in — wind that smelled of nowhere, dark that had never touched a pane, and the star, close now, bright at her shoulder, haloed in its own light.

Her heart was a drum. This was the forbidden act, the thing she had been raised to call theft, and she could feel the guild's words on her tongue, the hook in her hand if she had brought one. She had brought no hook. She had brought only the jar, and the words she had chosen, and a hand held out, open.

The star hovered at the threshold of the skylight, wild and unbound, and it looked at her. Then it crossed the glass.

It came to her hand. It settled there, small and warm and blazing, and she felt it — the first light ever offered to her, the first thing in seventeen years that had come because it chose, not because it was sold. She cupped it against her chest, and it sang. Not the dim weeping of the bottled stars. A bright, climbing, living note, a voice with no cage in it.

She should have put it in the jar. That was the act — the catching, the sealing, the taking. But her hands would not do it. She held the open jar, and the star looked up at her, and the song in it was so whole, so free, that she could not bear to close a lid over it.

She lowered the jar around it anyway. The dome was no place to argue with the wind; there was a descent to survive, and the Watch below, and a city that would call this theft. The glass slid home with a click, and the star went on singing inside it. Not weeping. Singing. As if the jar were a room and not a cage, as if it trusted the hands that held it.

She climbed down with the world swaying beneath her, the wind tearing at her, the jar warm against her ribs, her dim ache burning bright with guilt and wonder together. Guilt: she had done the thing she hated; she had taken a wild thing and put glass around it, and the trade's shadow was on her hands. Wonder: it had come to her. It had crossed the open sky to her hand and chosen, and no hook in the world could have forced it.

On the ground, in the dark angle where the dome met the wall, she crouched and opened the jar and looked at the star, and the star looked back, unafraid.

"I didn't steal you," she whispered. "You came."

It sang. She felt warmth in her chest where the cold had always been, and she did not know how to hold it, and she wept a little, and she ran.

Chapter 20: The First Star

Her room was a box under the shop's eaves, narrow and cold, and she had never once in five years brought a star into it. Tonight she set the jar on her windowsill, and the star rose out of it — she had not closed it properly, or it had chosen not to be closed — and hovered above the sill, small and bright, and sang.

She sat on the floor and watched it. It had a temper, she learned that first night: when she tried to see it clearly it dodged, playful; when she turned away it darted close; when she spoke it flared, and the light of it warmed the little room like a hearth. It was not like the bottled stars, dim and weeping and patient. It was quick. It was curious. It had never learned to wait.

"Where were you born?" she asked it, and it could not answer, not in words — but the songs of the open sky were in it, and as she listened she began to understand. This star had never been bound. No registry, no stamp, no jar, no person. It was the youngest child of the First Star — the mother of all stars, the myth, the tale that merchants told children and then forgot. And if the First Star was the mother, this small bright thing was her last child, unbound, wild, unowned by anyone in the world.

It had never had a person. All her life she had thought of stars as belonging to people — she had grown up inside that law. But this one had no owner. It had drifted the open dark for who knew how long, and it had crossed the glass to her hand and chosen, and the choosing was the whole of it.

She put her hand over her chest, where the cold shape had lived for seventeen years, and the cold shape was not gone — but there was warmth around it now, the first real warmth she could remember. Her own light was still on Halv's shelf. But something bright had moved into her life, and it was not a person's star. It was not a thing. It could not be bought, and she could not own it, and it had come anyway.

This changes everything, she thought. If a star could choose — if the sky was full of wild light that gave itself — then the trade was not the only shape of the world. There was another way to be with light. There was this.

The star drifted to her hand and settled, warm, and looked up at her with its whole bright being.

"Who am I to you?" she asked it.

It flared. The room filled with warmth and light, a wave of it, a tide, and for the first time in her life, standing in it, she understood the answer without needing words: whatever it was, it was hers, and she was not alone.

Chapter 21: Kian

She carried the jar under her coat through the low streets, and it was like carrying a lit coal against her ribs — warm, alive, impossible to hide. The wild star thrummed through the glass. Two nights now it had watched her from her room's shelf, unbound and unowned, the youngest child of the First Star, and she could not sit still. She had to walk. She had to feel the night on her face and the great cold glass of the dome overhead and the city's bells marking hours she no longer counted.

On the corner where the lantern-sellers gathered, a boy her age was folding paper into little lit shapes — glow-lanterns, cheap imitation light, hollow where a real star was full. He looked up as she passed, and his hands went still.

"Hold on," he said. "Hold on."

She turned, ready with a sharp answer, the way she always was. But he was staring at her coat, at the place where the jar pressed against her and a faint, unmistakable light leaked through the cloth.

"That's not lantern light," he said quietly. "And that's not a lantern."

Her heart climbed into her throat. She took a step back. "It's nothing. A trinket—"

"It's a star," he said, and said it the way you name a thing you have seen before. Not greed. Recognition. "You've got a star in there, and it's alive, and it's not in a vault, and you're carrying it like it's yours."

She should have run. She should have lied better. But he had called it alive, and no one called them that. "You know about stars?"

"I know the sky," he said. "I'm Kian. I sell light that isn't real, to people who can't afford the real thing. And I climb at night." He nodded up toward the great glass curve of the dome. "The Watch buys my lanterns on the high walk. There are ways up. Secret ways. Nobody knows them but the thieves and the starless."

The word sat between them. Starless. The city said soulless. She had heard it all her life, from the poor quarter and the rich alike, pitying or fearful.

"They call you that," she said. "And you still sell light."

"Somebody's got to," Kian said, and grinned, and there was something warm in it that had no star behind it at all, which should have been impossible. "The way I figure it, the light isn't the problem. It's the owning." He looked at the glow through her coat again. "That one's not owned, is it."

It wasn't a question.

"No," she said.

"Then whatever you're running from," Kian said, "you don't have to run it alone. If you need the sky, I know the ways up."

Chapter 22: The Ally

They watched Halv's shop from a bread-oven's shadow across the street for three nights running, and between them they built the truth of it. Kian knew the city's hours — when the Watch turned its lamps, which alleys echoed, where the rooftops joined hands. Selin knew the shop: every lock, every sill, the way Halv counted his jars before he bolted the door and carried his keys up to the empty rooms above.

"He counts twice," she said. "Once when the lamps go out, once when the bells strike midnight. Then he sleeps, and he sleeps like a man who has been cold a long time — deep, and hard to wake."

"He's your master," Kian said.

"He's the one who bought my star at the gate when I was an hour old," Selin said, and the words came out level, which surprised her. "Seventeen years on his highest shelf. He knew. He always knew."

Kian was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Then we're not stealing. We're returning."

They named the wild star together, out of sight of the shop, on a roof where the dome's cold light fell blue on them. She opened the jar a hand's width and let Kian look.

It had been unbound since the First Star gave it life — a thing that had never belonged to anyone, that did not know how. It drifted to the glass the way it always drifted toward her and sang a single low note that sounded almost like a question.

"Wild," Kian breathed. "A wild star. I've heard the old ones whisper about them." He looked at her. "It chose you."

"It came when I called."

"Then it's not caught. It's given." He laughed, soft and wondering. "The city says I'm empty, you know. Soulless, they call it, like I'm a jar with nothing in it." He tapped his chest. "I say I'm full of myself. And that star there's full of itself too, and it never needed anyone's license."

That was when the plan changed. Selin had been thinking of replacement — a star of her own, honest, at the source, to stand where her light should have stood. But watching Kian's face in the blue light, she understood. A cage that opened for one star was still a cage.

"We're not taking a star for me," she said slowly. "We're taking them all."

Kian's grin split the dark. "That's what I said. We're not catching anything, Selin. We're opening something."

Chapter 23: The Plan

The Guild Auction came once a season — the night the richest houses of Velia gathered in the guild hall to bid on stars, and every merchant in the city, Halv first among them, was required to attend. It was the one night the shop stood empty.

They planned it on a cold rooftop, their breath fogging, the wild star singing softly in its jar between them.

The route: from the lantern-sellers' corner up the chimney-walk to the laundry roofs, across the seam where two buildings had grown together, and onto the shop's gable. The roof held a sky-window Halv never watched — a narrow pane opening onto the attic where the old jars were stored. Kian knew it from his climbs. Selin knew it from years of sweeping the attic floor beneath it.

The lock: her stolen key, warm from Halv's coat pocket in her own. The vault sat under the shop floor, behind the counter where she had stood a thousand times — a square of iron with one lock, and the old man's secret: he kept the vault key on the same ring as the shop's, because he had owned the shop forty years and no one had ever thought to rob a merchant of empty light. She had held that ring. She had felt the vault key's particular weight. She had not returned it.

The Watch: rounds every quarter-hour along the Guild Way, but the shop stood on a side street the Watch passed only at the hour's turn. Ten minutes of clear dark, if the bells cooperated.

The release: every jar, every star, uncorked and sent up through the sky-window into the open dome. Not one. All of them. "A cage that opens for one star is still a cage," Selin said, and Kian nodded, because they had both said it now, and it had become true.

The risk: if they were caught, the guild would call it the worst theft in a century. The harvesters' law did not forgive the taking back of what had been taken. And the wild star — if it was found unlicensed and unbound, the youngest child of the First Star, it would be sealed in the deep vault and never sung again.

"Then we don't get caught," Kian said.

They made their oath on the wild star's light — the only light they had that belonged to no one. She set her hand on the jar; he set his over hers. The star flared, warm, through the glass, and it seemed to them both that it was not agreeing so much as laughing.

The night was fixed. Three nights hence, when the auction's bells rang the season open, they would climb.

Below them the bell-tower struck the hour, and somewhere deep in the dome, faint as a held breath, the stars began to sing.

Chapter 24: The Night

The auction bells rang at midnight, and all of Velia's licensed light went to the guild hall — merchants, clerks, harvester crews, the richest houses of the city, and Halv, who left his shop at a quarter to the hour, counting jars once, then twice, then hanging his keys on his belt and walking out into the cold.

From the rooftop across the way, Selin watched him go. Her heart beat so hard she could feel it in her throat.

Kian came up beside her on the chimney-walk, silent as he had promised, a coil of rope over his shoulder and a dark glow-lantern in his hand. "The Watch is at the corner," he breathed. "We go now, or we go at the hour's turn."

"Now," she said.

The city was asleep and awake at once — lamps burning low, bells echoing, the far sound of the auction's crowd like weather. They crossed the laundry roofs, and the shop's gable rose ahead of them, dark as a shoulder in the night. Kian's hands found holds in the stone as if they had grown there. Selin followed, her breath fogging, the stolen key cold and heavy against her ribs.

The sky-window was stiff. They worked it open by inches, and a Watchman passed below in the street, his lamp a moving pool of light, and they froze — she could feel Kian's stillness beside her, the two of them pressed flat against the slope of the roof like shadows — until the lamp moved on.

Then they were inside, dropping into the attic among the old jars, and Selin knew the shop by heart even in the dark: the smell of dust and glass and cold, the stair, the counter, the rows and rows of bottles.

The stars were awake. They knew. The moment she came down the stair, the shop's darkness began to glow — hundreds of dim lights waking, each jar holding a small singing thing, and the sound of it, soft as rain on glass, filled the dark. She had heard them dim for years. She had never heard them all sing at once.

Kian stood in the doorway of the vault, which stood open — the lock had given to her key like a stone giving to water.

"It's full," he breathed.

Selin stepped past him, and there, on the vault's highest shelf, brighter than all the rest, the Warm Star waited. Her star. Her own light, seventeen years under glass, singing to her through the shelf the way it had sung all her life. Her hands shook as she lifted the jar.

It flared when she touched it — warm, familiar, like a hand finding her hand in the dark.

Outside, the bells struck the half-hour.

"Home," she said, and opened it.

Chapter 25: The Freeing

The star rose from the broken seal like a held breath released, and hung before her, level with her heart, and there was no jar left between them.

Face to face at last.

The warmth of it was like standing at a hearth after years of cold, and it poured into her chest like a tide, into the place where the ache had always been, and the ache — the cold shape that had lived in her since the gate — began, for the first time in her life, to be full.

If it leaves, she thought, am I still me?

And she understood with a certainty that filled her all the way through: it would not leave. It could not. It was hers, and it had always been hers, and it had waited seventeen years under glass, singing to her through the shelf, and she had never once been alone in her dimness. It was already hers.

"Kian," she said, and her voice was strange to her, thick and bright. "The jars. All of them."

They uncorked them one by one.

He took the far rows; she took the shelves nearest the counter. Each jar opened with a soft sigh of glass, and each star rose — a point of light, a small warm breath — hung a moment as if remembering what air was, then drifted toward the open sky-window where the cold night waited.

The first dozen went up in silence. The shop grew brighter with every one. The stars did not weep now; they sang, a rising tide of small clear notes, and the old dark corners of the room filled with light like a room filling with water.

Then the wild star, freed from its jar, rose to the highest point of the room and turned in the air — and all the freed stars turned with it. It was the youngest child of the First Star, and it had never been owned, and it did not lead from command. It led from joy. The others followed it up through the sky-window in a long bright ribbon, and the night above the shop began to fill.

Selin and Kian worked until there were no jars left, until the shelves stood empty of glass and the vault stood open and dark, until the shop was empty of darkness.

Then she climbed up after the last of the stars, and on the shop roof, with her own star hovering warm at her shoulder and the sky above filling with everything she had set free, she stood beside Kian and watched them go.

Chapter 26: The Release

The dome was a glass cup holding the night, and into it the stars poured — the first migration in a century. Selin and Kian stood on the shop roof, breath fogging, faces turned up, and the light came up past them in ribbons and rivers, hundreds of small bright points leaving the shop's dark throat and rising into the great cold bowl of the sky.

Below, the city still slept. Lamps burned low along the terraces; the poor quarter lay in its usual dim; the guild hall's windows blazed with the auction's greed. No one in all of Velia looked up — no one except the two of them on the roof, and the stars themselves.

The wild star led the dance. It had never had a person, never a jar, never a cage; it moved through the dome like a note of music finding the song, and the freed stars followed it in long bright arcs, arranging themselves, finding old places, remembering. They had been born in this sky, most of them. The dome had been their home, and the shop their prison, and now they came back to the glass the way water comes home to the sea.

Selin's own star stayed near her.

It had not joined the others. It circled above the roof, a warm steady light, low enough to touch, circling her, waiting, patient, the way it had waited seventeen years on a shelf. It would not force the joining. It was hers, and it knew it, and it let her come to it.

She reached up a hand, and it drifted to her palm and sat there, warm, singing a low note she felt in her chest like a second heartbeat.

"Look," Kian breathed.

The dome was alive. The light moved through it in slow currents, and the singing rose — hundreds of voices, small and clear, harmonizing as if they had practiced for a hundred years, because in a way they had. They had sung alone in their jars. Now they sang together, and the whole glass cup of the night rang with it.

Beauty and grief, both at once — the beauty of a hundred lights dancing, the grief of every dim face she had ever seen in the poor quarter, every cold ache, every sold child, all of it lit now in a way it had never been lit before.

And then, far off, thin as a thread through the singing, the Watch's horns sounded.

Kian stiffened. "They've seen it. Everybody's seen it — it's pouring up into the glass like dawn."

The horns came closer, a second call, then a third.

Selin's star rose from her palm and circled above her, and it called her name — her name, in a voice made of light and warmth, the voice of the piece of her that had waited all her life.

The horns were nearer now.

Kian looked at her, and she looked at him, and the singing went on around them, and her star called her name again, and there was no time left to decide anything — only to meet what was coming.

Chapter 27: The Sky Alive

The sky had become a body.

Not a scattering of lights, but one living thing — the dome filled edge to edge with stars, breathing, turning, moving in slow currents around a still center. And at the center, where the highest point of the glass curved toward the open night beyond, the wild star hung: the youngest child of the First Star, alone, unowned, the quiet heart of the turning sky.

It was the first time the city's sky had been alive since the dome was sealed. The stars arranged themselves around their center not by law or license but by memory — finding old places, old neighbors, the houses and streets their people slept in below. Each star hung where its person slept. The dome became a map of Velia's dreaming, every light a sleeper's name.

On the shop roof Selin watched, and her chest ached — not cold now, but full, too full to hold.

The horns had reached the Guild Way. They came closer with every breath, and below, windows were opening, lamps being lit, voices calling out. The city was waking to its sky for the first time in a century, and some of the voices were wonder and some were fury, and all of them were coming this way.

"We can run," Kian said. "The chimney-walk, the laundry roofs — I can get us to the low quarter before they reach the street."

Selin looked at the sky. At her star, circling above her, warm and patient, waiting for her choice.

"If we run," she said, "they'll think we were thieves."

"Weren't we?"

"No." She said it like a door closing. "We opened the jars. That's all. The owning, the selling, the dimming — that was the theft. All of it, for a hundred years." She turned to him. "If we run, we hide from the truth. I'm done hiding. It took me seventeen years to find it."

Kian looked at her a long moment. Then he nodded, slow, and something in his face settled.

"Then we meet them," he said.

The horns were at the corner. The Watch came up the street in a clatter of lamps and steel, and torchlight flooded the shop front, and they found the two of them standing at the roof's edge, high above the street, not running, not hiding — just standing in the light of the freed sky.

The captain's voice carried up. "You two! On the roof! Down here, now!"

And Selin's star, as if in answer, descended — a warm steady light from the turning sky down to her shoulder, where it settled against her ear like a hand laid on her cheek, and she was warm, all the way through, for the first time in her life.

Chapter 28: The City Wakes

Velia woke to a sky that had not been empty in a century.

The bells rang first — every bell in the city, the tower bells and the street bells and the guild's own great bronze — ringing not the hour but the news, a wild clanging that pulled sleepers to their windows. And when they looked up, they saw it: the dome full of stars, the sky alive, lights moving in slow currents above the terraces and the poor quarter alike, each one hanging where a person slept.

The city's voice rose in a hundred registers at once. The rich leaned from their balconies in wonder and fear, counting their sold stars against the moving lights. The guild sent runners and clerks, and the guild's voice was fury — the inventory, the vault, the loss, who had dared. The Watch moved through the streets, and the Watch's voice was horns and boots. And the poor quarter cried — a sound like rain breaking, like weeping turned to joy, because for the first time in living memory the dim streets had light over them, and the dim people saw it and understood nothing and everything at once.

Selin and Kian were taken gently.

The Watch captain was a tired woman who had seen the dome dark for forty years, and when she found them on the roof with the freed sky turning above them, she did not draw steel. She looked at the star on Selin's shoulder, where it sat warm, and she looked at the sky, and then she said, "You'd better come tell the guild what you've done. You'll get a fair hearing or a fast rope, and I don't yet know which."

They walked through streets that were filling with people, everyone looking up, everyone talking at once, and the light of the freed stars fell on them like slow rain. The auction had been abandoned mid-bid; the guild hall stood open and full, masters and merchants and clerks crowding the doors.

Someone had been sent for Halv.

He came down the long street from his empty shop, in his thin dark coat, pockets full of keys that no longer fit anything, and the crowd parted for him the way it always had, because he was the Starlight Merchant and the licensed owned the sky. He came to name his apprentice.

And Selin stood in the guild hall, her star on her shoulder, warm as a held hand, and watched him come. His shop was empty behind him. His shelves bare, his vault open, his forty years of hoarded light streaming overhead through the dome.

He stopped in the doorway, and he saw her, and he saw the star on her shoulder, and something in his old cold face went very still.

Chapter 29: The Confrontation

Halv's rage was quiet, which made it worse.

He did not shout. He crossed the guild hall's stone floor with the terrible stillness of a man holding fire inside his coat, and the crowd fell back from him as from a cold front. When he reached Selin he stopped, and his voice was low and shaking.

"Ruined," he said. "My stock. My vault. Forty years of light, gone up into the glass like smoke. The guild is howling, the buyers are demanding restitution, and my apprentice stands in the middle of it with a star on her shoulder." His hand came up, trembling, and pointed at her. "Explain it."

"Master," Selin said, and her voice was calm because she had practiced it in the dark for days — the calm of a thing finally said. "You knew."

"Knew what?"

"About me." She reached into her coat and brought out the registry page and the old page from the ledger, creased from being carried against her heart all night. She held them out. He looked at them and did not take them. "Gate-born, first hour. Bought at the gate for passage into the city. A pittance." She read his name from the page, and it was the hardest thing she had ever said. "Halv. Kept unsold seventeen years. You knew it was mine. You always knew. You apprenticed me because my star pulled you — because you kept it warm on your shelf, and I kept it warm by standing under it, and you wanted to watch."

The guild murmured behind them. Halv's face had gone the color of old paper.

"A star cannot lie about belonging," Selin said. "That's the first thing you taught me. And mine has been calling to me through your glass my whole life, and you heard it. You had to have heard it."

The star on her shoulder flared — warm, bright, undeniable — and the light of it fell on Halv's face, on the ledger, on the red stamp that had sold her.

"It was mine, and you knew," she said. "That's the whole of it. The harvest is theft, whatever the law calls it. You taught me that too, Master. I just never knew I was the lesson."

For a long moment Halv stood very still. Then, slowly, as if his hand moved of its own will, he reached toward the star on her shoulder — not to seize it, but to touch it, to hold the only warm thing he had ever been able to keep.

He stopped. His fingers hovered a hand's breadth from the light, and they did not close. He looked at his own hand as if it belonged to someone else, and then he looked at her, and the rage was gone from his face, leaving only the cold hollow underneath.

"The bottle you couldn't sell," Selin said, "was a person. So were you, once."

Chapter 30: Master Halv's Fall

The guild moved quickly, because the guild had been frightened, and frightened men make fast judgments.

They stripped him in a single sitting: license, shop, standing. The levy of the inventory's worth — a century of hoarded light, priced by the ledger and counted against him in gold — emptied his accounts and then some. The clerks stamped it all in red, and the red stamps fell like small wounds, and Halv stood through it without speaking.

The crowd's verdict came next. The merchants who had bid against him, the collectors who had bought from him, the guild masters who had profited from his trade — they all found their distance from him, the way water finds its level. Those who had sold to him now spoke of his greed. Those who had taught him now spoke of his ruin. The hall emptied around him as if he had been the one caught thieving, and in a way, he had.

At the doors, the poor quarter's faces waited. Not to jeer — they were not cruel, and they had seen too much dimming to take joy in another's. But they watched. Some of them had bought passage with their children's light; some had sold the way Halv had sold, and they wanted to see what happened to the man who had done it well and long.

The man diminished. That was the word Selin kept coming back to, standing at the edge of the hall — not fell, not broke, but diminished, the way a light does when the jar goes on. He shrank into his coat, and the coat, which had always fit the Starlight Merchant, hung suddenly too big on the man who had lost the sky.

He gave her one look as they took his keys. Not hatred. Exhaustion — forty years of cold, used up at last, leaving nothing behind but a tired man holding the empty ring where his keys had hung.

They sealed the shop. She watched them nail the boards over the door of the Star Shop, over the counter where she had stood a thousand times, over the empty shelves where the jars had been. The last thing they took was the keys, and they put them in the guild's strongbox, and the Starlight Merchant walked out into the square with his hands open and empty and a coat suddenly too big for the man inside it.

The freed sky turned overhead, and Halv stood alone in the middle of the square, looking up at the light he had hoarded all his life, and did not seem to know it.

Chapter 31: The Confession

The guild hall held its breath. Selin stood with her star burning on her shoulder, and across the marble floor Master Halv stood diminished in his dark velvet coat, the pockets empty of their keys.

He had been asked to speak. The guild masters expected a defense, a lawyer's song, some artful turning of the theft into commerce. What he gave them was nothing they could price.

"Forty years ago," he said, and his voice was a thing Selin had never heard — unarmored, naked of its ledger-warmth — "I was twenty-three, and poorer than the rats in the guild tower. The license cost everything. Not money. I had no money. It cost a star — and I had a star."

A sound went through the crowd like wind through dry glass.

For Selin, the words rearranged the world. She had seen his ledger a hundred times, priced and stamped and closed. She had never once imagined a page with his own name at its top — SOLD, first hour, for a license. She thought of the drawer of things he could not sell, and she understood it at last: not a hoard of light, a hoard of warmth. He had spent forty years trying to buy back a feeling with other people's stars.

"The registry has the entry. Gate-born, like her. First hour. SOLD. My own hand signed it. I walked into the harvest dome with a jar, and I put my own light in it, and I sold it for the right to sell other people's." He looked at his hands. "And I have not felt a warm thing since. Not the sun on the glass. Not a coin. Not another person's hand. I hoarded light because I could not feel it — like a man who keeps bread though he has no mouth."

Selin's anger cracked. It did not shatter; it split down the middle, and through the split came something else.

"You kept mine," she said. The hall stilled. "Seventeen years. You bought it at the gate for passage-money and you kept it, unsold, on your highest shelf, singing to me through the glass. Why?"

He met her eyes. For the first time she saw him fully — not the Starlight Merchant, the keys, the coat. A cold man, all the way through to the middle, standing at the edge of the only warm thing he had ever been able to hold.

"Because it was warm," he said. "The only warm thing I could hold."

The silence was vast. Somewhere a bell began to toll the hour.

"I didn't take your star because I was greedy," Halv said, and his voice broke on the last word. "I took it because I was cold."

Chapter 32: The Loneliness

The shop was sealed. The guild had hammered boards across the door, but Halv had the deep vault — a door behind the counter that the seal could not reach, because the seal-makers did not know it existed. Selin did. She had watched him for five years, and she knew every key-shaped silence in the place.

She found him sitting on the cellar stairs in the dark, the dust of the empty shelves sifting down like fine snow. The rows of bottles were gone, every one, freed or taken as evidence. Only the dark remained, and the shape of a man who had forgotten how to be warm.

"Here," she said. She set down a lantern, and in its light she saw his face — not diminished now, but emptied. A man hollowed out by his own keeping.

"Why did you come?" he said. Not cruel. Tired, the way the long-cold are tired.

She sat on the stair below him. Her star perched on her knee, a small bright weight. "Because you kept mine for seventeen years," she said. "Because when you could have sold it a thousand times, you didn't. I want to know why you came to get me at the gate."

"I told you. It was warm."

"Then you know what you're missing." She watched him. "You're the only man in Velia who knows exactly what he sold, and you kept the receipt."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh and might have been the cold cracking.

"Show me the vault," she said.

He went still. "There's nothing left in it."

"Show me."

For a long moment he did not move. Then he stood, and his hand found a key Selin had never seen — brass, warm, worn smooth by forty years of worry — and he led her down into the deepest room of the shop.

It was not a vault of riches. It was a shelf, and on the shelf one jar. Inside it, a star — dimmer than any she had ever seen, a coal almost gray, but awake, and singing the faintest thread of a song.

"Yours," she said. It was not a question.

He nodded. "Forty years I've kept it. I've held it like a man warming his hands at a star, and I've never dared —" He stopped.

"To open it."

"To be warm again." His voice was barely a breath. "I don't remember how. I'm afraid it won't know me."

Selin looked at the jar, at the gray star singing its thin song, and she felt the shape of the ache in her own chest — the one that had just been filled — and knew it by contrast.

"I won't open it for you," she said gently. "Not yet. You have to want it first. Really want it. Or it'll just be another cage with a different door."

The lantern guttered. In the dim light, Halv's face hung above the last bottle, and the star inside it turned toward him — patient, waiting, the way a held breath waits.

Chapter 33: The Pardon

The guild hearing was held in the same hall as the fall, and the crowd was twice as large. The poor quarter had come. They lined the galleries in their dim clothes, and their dim faces — so many of them — turned toward the marble floor where Halv stood, stripped of license and coat-trim and pride.

The masters had called it a trial. Selin had learned the truth of trials in five years of trade: they were ledgers with voices. But this one had a strange book.

One by one, the poor quarter stood.

A lamplighter, old, with the gray shine of a harvested star barely breathing in his chest. "He let my daughter in cheap," he said. "The gate-fee was passage and a star. He took the star and let the fee stand at nothing, and she's whole today because he did. I'm not saying it was kind. I'm saying it was the only kindness on that gate."

A fishwife. A glazier. A woman with an empty bottle on a cord at her neck, speaking of the night Halv walked the quarter, thirty years gone, and gave the dim ones jars that held light, free, to warm their hands against.

The masters shifted. The ledger of his crimes had another column no one had stamped.

Then Selin rose, her star a small sun on her shoulder, and the hall went still.

"Theft is theft," she said, and the word fell like a gavel. "He took my light. He took a hundred lights. He built his life on bottles, and the bottle is a cage, and I will never call what he did anything but stealing."

Halv did not flinch. He looked as if he had expected no less.

"But," she said, and the hall leaned forward, "he gave it back. Every jar. Every star. His shop. His license. And now himself. There is a difference between the man who takes and the man who gives back everything, and himself, and asks for nothing." She looked at him, and the crack in her anger widened into something she could almost hold. "A thief who pays back the whole debt has paid. I say let him keep his name, and his empty hands, and his city."

The crowd's silence broke into a murmur like a rising tide.

The chief master raised the stamp. The hall waited. Selin watched it come down over the page where his license had been struck — and she saw the color as it landed.

Not red.

Gold.

Halv stood very still. Then, for the first time in forty years, the man who could not feel wept — quietly, like rain on glass, like a jar uncorked.

Chapter 34: The Stars

They came to her at the shop, in the deep night after the pardon. Not as the world saw them — points of light pressed at glass — but as they were: voices, hundreds of them, threading through the open skylight like a river of song.

Selin stood in the center of the empty shop and listened. Her own star glowed at her throat. The freed stars circled above her, arranging themselves, finding their old places, and she understood that they were not visiting. They were choosing.

"You," said a voice that was a hundred voices, warm as a held breath. "Gate-born. Dimmed. We have watched you since you stood under glass and sang to us."

"They were in jars," she said. "They couldn't hear me."

"They heard. A star cannot lie about belonging, and we have learned to listen through glass." The voices rippled. "You freed us. You opened the cages. Now the sky has a question for you."

Selin's breath caught. "The sky?"

"The sky is a trust, not a thing. It was held wrong for a century. We would hold it right." The hundred voices bent toward her. "We choose you: keeper of the sky's trust. You will keep no star. You will keep the sky — the belonging, the singing, the open night. You will be the hand that uncorks."

Something in her chest — the place where the ache had lived, warm with her star's nearness — answered before her mind could. "Yes."

The voices sang. And out of the night, through the skylight, came a small, wild, bright star — the First Star's youngest child, the one that had chosen her on the dome, the one that could not be owned. It had traveled. It had gone to see the open sky it had never seen. Now it returned, and it settled on her shoulder like a weight of pure warmth, and it sang to her.

"You may travel," Selin said softly, stroking its light. "You will return. Choosing, not owned."

The wild star flared.

"You speak the sky's tongue," said the hundred voices. "Then hear the trade's true purpose, keeper: not to own light. To keep it. The dome is not a vault. It is a hand, held open."

Selin stood in the dark shop, ringed by the light of a hundred freed stars, and the sky spoke her name in a hundred voices — Selin — each one carrying the next, and her own star sang the last syllable, holding it like a bell.

Chapter 35: Selin's Star

The night after the council, her star did not perch on the shelf where she had set it. It hovered at the center of the room, above the table, and it sang — a different song, lower, closer, a song she had heard all her life through glass without knowing what it was.

"Now?" she said.

It flared. Yes.

She had imagined the joining as a door thrown open, a tide, a roof lifted off. She had not imagined it as a question.

The star descended slowly, giving her time. It hovered at the exact place where the ache had always lived, and it waited. She felt the shape of her old wound like a socket, and she felt the star like the light that belonged in it.

"It's you," she whispered. "It was always you. Singing to me through the shelf. All seventeen years."

The star trembled. It had waited under glass for her — bought and kept, never sold — and it had sung to her from its high shelf every night she stood beneath it, and it had never once stopped believing she would come.

"What if it hurts?" she said, afraid for the first time.

The star sang, and the song was warm. It cannot hurt, it seemed to say. It can only be over.

Selin closed her eyes. She thought of the gate, the passage, the mother's empty bottle, the cold shape she had grown up calling her own name. She thought of dim faces and the registry's red stamp. And she thought of the small bright thing hovering before her, patient, faithful, seventeen years of song.

"Yes," she said. "Come home."

The star met the socket of her ache and fitted itself into it, and the warmth did not arrive like a tide — it arrived like a tide arriving all at once, in the same breath, from every direction at once, and Selin gasped and fell to her knees with her hands pressed to her chest.

It was not a feeling. It was the end of a missing. For seventeen years she had carried a cold shape, and the shape was not empty — it had always been the exact negative of this light. The star flooded the hollow, filled every cold corner, and the ache that had been the background of her whole life simply stopped being there, the way a held note stops when the singer breathes.

She wept. She laughed. The light poured through her and out of her, and she was bright, bright in a way she had never been, warm in a way she had no words for, full in a way that made her want to give the whole world exactly what she held.

"Lost years can't be returned," she said, kneeling in a room that was suddenly too small for her joy. "I know that. I know."

The star sang in her chest.

"But you were with me all along. All along." She lifted her hands, and light spilled between her fingers, and she held her own light in her own hands for the first time in her life.

"We're home," she said.

Chapter 36: The Reunion

The first night whole was not what Selin had expected.

She had expected fireworks — a blaze, a white-hot standing-in-sunlight. Instead, walking home through the sleeping city with her star warm under her ribs, she felt everything, and the everything was too much and too dear and she had to stop in the middle of the square and press her hand to her chest and breathe.

The cold. She had never felt cold before, not really — the ache had swallowed weather whole. Now the night air on her face was a shock, sharp and alive, and she stood in it, feeling the world with bare skin.

The bells. She heard the little hour-bell of the poor quarter, the one she had grown up with, and it was beautiful, and she was crying.

Her star sang to her — not through glass now, not muffled by a shelf — and every note landed somewhere she could feel it.

"Stop that," she said, laughing and crying at once. "I can't — I'm not used to — "

It sang anyway, pleased.

She walked her old streets, and the dim faces of her childhood passed her, and she looked at them now with new eyes: at the gray shine in their chests, at the half-light of their half-lives, at the cold ache she had just shed. And she understood the price of the trade as she never had from the ledger — not in stamps and sums, but in every dim face she had ever seen, the price was that.

She knelt at the fountain in the old square and put her hands in the water, just to feel it.

"Seventeen years," she said to her star, to the night, to herself. "I was dim for seventeen years and I thought that was just — that it was me. That I was cold the way some people are tall. I never knew I was missing. I never knew I was stolen."

The star sang, and it was not a comfort — it was a confirmation. Yes. You were. And now you're not.

"I will never let it happen to another person," Selin said into the night. "Not one. Not ever. I will unmake the whole trade before I let one more child grow up thinking the cold is her name."

It was a vow. She said it plainly, the way the gate had been crossed plainly, the way the jar had been filled plainly. And the star in her chest went still, then flared, and she felt it take the promise into itself, so that it would be carried in her light forever.

She watched the sky until the gray before dawn, and when the first light came — the real first light, warm and pale and hers — she stood, and she shone, and for the first time in her life she was not afraid of it.

Chapter 37: The New Merchant

The shop opened on a morning of bells.

Selin had repainted the sign herself, standing on a ladder while Kian held it steady and pretended not to be frightened. The old words — STARLIGHT MERCHANT — came down under a scrape of turpentine, and the new words went up in gold:

THE HOMECOMING.

"They'll come," Kian said, setting his paper lanterns in the window, their imitation light flickering beside the real thing. "The ones who need it."

"Or they'll throw rocks," Selin said. "That's the other possibility."

He grinned. "You've got a star now. You can see rocks coming from further away."

The first customers were not buyers. They came through the door as if testing whether it would hold them, and they stood in the light and wept.

A seeker — a woman who had never been dimmed, who had come because she had heard there was a place now where the jars were opened. She bought nothing. She stayed an hour, listening to the freed stars sing from the shop's high shelves, and she left lighter than she came.

A starless mother, carrying a baby born with no star at all, holding it out in the doorway like an offering. "They say you've got a wild one," she whispered. "They say it chose you. Is it true what the city's saying — that light isn't owned?"

Selin looked at Kian, who stood very still, and she answered the woman with the truth. "Yes. It's true."

The mother left holding her child closer, and her face was a different kind of lit.

Then the ashamed ones came. The collectors. Men and women of the terraces in their fine dim coats, carrying jars in both hands, setting them down on the counter and stepping back as if the glass might burn them.

"I'm sorry," said the first, a gray-haired man with a gray shine in his chest. "I bought it forty years ago. I never opened it. I couldn't. Take it back."

Selin took the jar. The star inside turned toward her, and she heard it — a thin, tired thread of song. She set it on the shelf with the others, and the man watched her with something cracking open in his dim face.

"You'll give it back to its person?" he said.

"I'll find them," she said. "That's the trade now."

She mended a jar. She opened a skylight. And that evening, for the first time under her roof, a star went home — the gray man's star, matched through the registry to a fishwife in the poor quarter, who fell to her knees in her own doorway when the light found her chest.

Selin stood at the shop door and watched the warmth fill the street like a tide, and her star sang inside her, and she thought: this. This is the whole trade.

Chapter 38: The Trade, Changed

The new ledger was gold-embossed, but it was the old ledger that mattered.

Selin had taken the great red book of sales — seven hundred pages of names and stamps — and she had turned it into a ledger of return. Page by page, she matched every sold star to its person. The registry gave her the births. The stamps gave her the buyers. The stars themselves gave her the rest, because a star cannot lie about belonging, and she had learned to listen.

Kian kept the door and the street. He knew who had sold what, who had starved their light, who had bought in grief and hoarded in guilt. His lanterns became a signal: one lit on the shop's sill meant the ledger was open, and the seekers came.

The work was simple, and it was everything. No hooks. No jars. Only openings — a skylight lifted, an uncorking, a flight of light across a kitchen and into a waiting chest. Selin matched. She opened. And the homecomings happened, one after another, each small, each total.

The rich came back shamed and left glad. A collector who had bought a child's star for a wall of trophies returned it to a weeping mother in the poor quarter, and when the child's light found her, the collector stood in the street and wept too, though his own chest stayed gray.

The lonely came for the singing. They did not want to buy. They sat in the shop's amber light with the freed stars turning overhead, and they listened, and some of them, for the first time in years, felt the cold in themselves go quiet for an hour.

"She says the old words are dead," Kian told a crowd at the door one evening, the paper lanterns swaying. "The guild's creed. A star is worth what someone will pay." He shook his head. "Selin says a star is worth everything — and it cannot be bought."

Inside, at the counter, Selin closed the old ledger's last open page. Seven hundred pages of theft, each one now answered by a homecoming. She ran her hand over the red stamps — so many of them — and she felt the shape of the trade in her chest: the ache it had made, the light it had hoarded, the dim faces it had built.

Then she closed the book, and she set it on the high shelf, beside a row of jars that stood empty and open — not cages, just glass, remembering.

She opened the new ledger. The first page was blank, and gold, and waiting.

"Write the first name," Kian said.

Selin touched the page. Her own name rose in her mind — the first star returned — but she shook her head. The book would not begin with her.

"The fishwife," she said. "The one from last night. She was the first to go home."

And under the gold light, in the shop that used to sell light, she wrote the first entry in the ledger of return: a name, a star, a homecoming. No stamp. No price. Just the truth of it.

Chapter 39: The Harvest, Ended

The hearing was short. After the pardon and the flood of returns, the guild's resistance had the thin, resigned quality of a candle in a draft. They knew. Everyone in Velia knew.

Selin stood at the head of the hall, her star burning at her throat, and she read the old charter aloud — "The licensed own the sky" — and then she tore it, once, down the middle, and the sound of it was the sound of a cage opening.

"Harvesting is theft," she said. "Whatever the law called it. The law is done calling it that."

The masters voted with their hands, and the votes for keeping were fewer than she had feared. The licenses were dissolved. The registry of birth-stars became a registry of return — no red stamps, only the blue of finding. Harvesting was made illegal forever, and the word illegal landed in the hall and stayed there, and no one argued.

The last crew disbanded in the yard below. She watched them stack the hooks — the long, bright, cruel instruments of the old trade — and she saw the star-hooks carried away to the smiths, where they would be melted down and beaten into door-knockers and weather-vanes, things that opened doors and told the wind's direction instead of pulling light from the sky.

"The skylights," she said, and the clerk wrote it down, and she felt the word travel through the city like a bell. "Order them open. All of them. The dome is not a vault."

From the poor quarter came a sound she had never heard from that direction: a cheer. It rose thin and broke and rose again, a hundred dim voices finding something to sing about. The dim faces at the doors of the guild hall looked in at her, and they were not angry. They were lit with something new.

Selin let herself look once at the edge of the crowd, where a man stood apart in a coat that no longer had keys in its pockets. Halv. Quiet, small, and — she thought, squinting against the light — almost warm. He did not come forward. He did not have to. He watched the hooks go to the smiths, and the skylights ordered open, and the charter torn, and something in his gray chest turned, slowly, toward the light.

The clerk set the great book before her, open to the last page of licenses. She took the gold stamp — her own now, warm in her hand — and she struck the last license from the book, one clean blow, the way a bell is struck at the hour.

The hall was silent. Then the poor quarter's cheer came through the windows like weather, and Selin stood at the head of the hall with the book of the old trade closed under her hand, and the sky, beyond the glass, waiting to be opened.

Chapter 40: The Sky, Open

They opened the dome together.

It took the whole city, which was the point. The masters threw open the great shutters of the guild tower. The Watch unlatched the service doors. The glaziers and the lamplighters and the children of the poor quarter came up the winding stairs with the levers, and Selin and Kian climbed to the dome's rim, and every hand in Velia pulled at once.

The skylights had been opened before, one by one, for the trade — a jar's-width, a thief's-width. This was different. The great shutters of the dome's crown swung outward on their hundred-year hinges, and the whole curve of glass lifted, and the night sky came in.

Selin had stood on this rim once, alone and furious, with a stolen jar and a stolen plan. She remembered the wind, the cold, the stars pressed at the glass like moths. Now the wind came through the opened dome and there was no glass between her and it, and the cold was a living thing, and the stars were not pressed against anything.

They hung. They hung in the open dark, in their hundreds and thousands, the freed stars mingling with the fixed ones, and she could not tell which were the ones she had uncorked and which had always been there — which was the point. Light is not owned. The sky was no one's, and so it was everyone's, and it poured down over Velia like a tide of weather.

Behind her, below her, the city breathed light. The poor quarter was lit at last — not by paper, not by imitation, but by returning stars in warm doorways, by children pointing up with both hands, by windows where dimness had lived for generations now glowing like held breaths. The bells began, all of them, the little hour-bell and the great tower bell together, ringing the sky in.

Kian stood beside her on the rim, and his paper lanterns swung from his belt, and his face was turned up, and he was laughing.

"Look at it," he said. "No jars. No locks. No one owns any of it."

Selin stood with her star warm inside her and the open night above her, the wind cold on her face and the light pouring down on the city she had dimmed in and never known she was dim in. She thought of the gate, and the passage, and the red stamp, and the empty bottle on her mother's shelf.

Then she thought of what came after.

"It's ours," she said. "All of it. And it's nobody's."

The sky, listening, brightened. The city below breathed light. And on the dome's rim, with the cage fully broken, the Starlight Merchant stood in the open wind and let the whole sky see her.

Chapter 41: The Poor Quarter

The streets had changed, and they had not changed at all.

Selin knew every crack in the paving, every stair that froze in winter and every doorway that held a draft. She had grown up dim in these streets and thought the dimness was weather. Now she walked them with her star warm under her ribs, and the weather was gone.

The quarter was lit. Not by the old lamps — the flickering tapers and the imitation light that had been the poor quarter's whole sky — but by stars. Returning stars. They moved in doorways and above rooftops, found their people and settled into waiting chests, and where they went, warmth went. Children pointed up with both hands. Old women stood in their doorways with tears running down faces that Selin had only ever seen gray, and the gray was lifting like a tide going out.

Her old neighbors came to her. A woman who had sold her first child's star to eat, and had just now — this morning, in her kitchen, over cold tea — felt the ache in her chest fill and overflow. She took Selin's hands, and her hands were warm, and she could not speak, and she did not have to.

"You," she kept saying. "You."

More hands found her. The lamplighter who had spoken at the pardon. The fishwife whose star had been the first to go home under Selin's roof. They pressed around her like a tide, warm hands on her shoulders, her arms, and Selin stood in the middle of it, shining, and let herself be touched by a hundred people who had been cold all their lives and were cold no longer.

Somewhere a flute began, thin and bright, and another answered it. The festival of return was beginning — the quarter's own invention, no guild seal on it, just music and lit windows and people looking up.

It was joy. It was the whole of it. And yet.

Selin turned down the street she knew best, and the music faded behind her, and her star went quiet in her chest, listening. The houses here were the same. The door was the same door, the one she had left at twelve and never once gone back through in five years — not out of anger, out of a silence that had no name.

Her childhood door.

Behind it, she knew, the only dim place in the lit city was waiting. The mother's empty bottle on the shelf. The father's silence. The sister's bright, unasked question. And Selin stood in the street of her own childhood, the festival ringing behind her, her hand lifted to knock, and she could not make her hand move.

The only dim place left.

Chapter 42: The Family, Found

The door opened before she knocked.

Her mother stood in it, and behind her the house was dim — dim in a way the rest of the quarter was no longer dim, the way a room is dim when the fire is out and no one has remembered to relight it. The empty bottle was on the shelf where it had always been, seventeen years, gathering the dust of guilt.

"Come in," her mother said, and her voice was the voice of a woman who had rehearsed the words for seventeen years and still could not get them right.

Selin stepped over the threshold. Her star pressed against her ribs, bright, and the dim house felt it — the walls seemed to lean toward her, the way a cold room leans toward a fire.

The father sat at the table, not looking up. The sister, twelve now and bright with her own star, stared from the doorway of the back room, her mouth open.

"You're — " the sister said. "You're shining."

Selin laughed, and it came out wet. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm not used to it either."

The mother had not moved. She stood between Selin and the shelf, and her hands were clasped in front of her, and she was trembling.

"Sit down," she said. "Please. Sit down. There's — I have to tell you — "

"I know," Selin said. "I've known for a year. I've known since I read the registry. Gate-born, first hour, SOLD. Passage." She said the word carefully, the way you say a word that has been a wound. "You sold my light to buy us in. The hour I was born."

The mother made a sound — small, old, the sound of a bottle being uncorked after seventeen years.

"We sold your light," she said. "And we have been cold ever since. We meant to buy the city, and we bought — " She gestured at the dim house, the empty bottle, the years. "We bought this. We bought the cold. Every time I look at that bottle, I see the hour. I hear the gate. I hear the merchant's keys."

Selin looked at the bottle — empty, glass, a cage that had never held anything but grief.

The father spoke. It was the first time in five years Selin had heard his voice. "We refused twice," he said. "When your sister was born, and when they came back for her light, years later. We refused both times. But we could not refuse the first time. You were the price of everything. We paid with you."

The sister came forward, her own star bright at her throat, and stood beside her mother. "Why does she get to be cold?" she asked. "Why did you sell her and not me?"

The mother's face crumpled.

Selin crossed the room. She took her mother's hands — cold, trembling, the hands of a woman who had held an empty bottle for seventeen years — and she held them, and she felt the warmth move out of her own chest and into those hands, the way light moves through glass.

"Then let's be warm now," Selin said.

Chapter 43: The Star, Given

That night Selin gave what had been sold.

She stood in the middle of the dim house and let her star come out — not the small glow at her throat that she wore like a lamp, but all of it, the whole of her, the light that had waited seventeen years under glass and found her at last. It poured from her chest and filled the room, filled the corners where cold had lived for generations, and the dim house became a lit house, warm as an oven, bright as the inside of a jar.

Her mother watched with her hands pressed to her mouth.

"It was always yours," Selin said. "They took it from me, and I took it back, and now I'm giving it to you. All of it. For the night. For as long as you need."

"No," her mother said. "You can't give what's — "

"It was sold," Selin said. "Sold things can be given. That's the whole new trade." She took the empty bottle from the shelf — the guilt-vessel, the seventeen-year wound — and she held it in her hands, and she looked at her mother. "You kept it empty. You kept the shape of what you lost. Let me fill it. Not to cage it. Just to show you it was never empty. It was waiting."

She did not need a hook. She did not need a jar's-width of skylight. She cupped the bottle in her hands, and her star bent toward it, and light spilled down into the glass like water into a well, and the bottle filled.

Her mother took it like a child takes a thing too precious to understand. It glowed in her hands, warm, and she held it and wept, and the tears were warm, and she felt them.

The family sat at the table. The mother held the full bottle. The father sat with his hands open on the wood, and the light lay on his face, and something in his silence cracked, slowly, like ice in a thaw. The sister's own star drifted out of her chest to meet Selin's — two lights circling the room together, singing to each other, the song of the sky they had both been born under.

The mother set the bottle in the center of the table, and the four of them sat in the light of it, eating bread, saying the true words at last, saying them over and over until they went easy.

At the window, later, the family stood together, looking out at the sky — the open sky, full of stars, no glass between them and it. The mother held the full bottle against her chest like a child.

Selin watched her, and her star sang inside her, and she thought of the shop, and the ledger of return, and all the dim faces still waiting for their homecomings.

It would take the rest of her life. She hoped so.

Chapter 44: The Bottle, Empty

The last bottle stood on the counter of The Homecoming.

Halv had brought it himself, carrying it through the streets in both hands like a man carrying a sleeping bird. He set it down and stepped back, and Selin looked at the dim gray star inside it, the coal almost cold, the faint thread of song, and she understood.

"Forty years," she said.

"Forty years." He was thinner than she remembered, and his coat was clean and mended and had no keys in the pockets, and his hands hung at his sides, empty and open. "I kept it when I couldn't feel it. I kept it when I could. I think — " He stopped. "I think I was waiting for the shop to be a place where bottles are opened. It is now."

Selin lifted the last bottle. The star inside turned toward her — it knew her, the way all stars knew her now — and it sang its thin, patient thread.

"Are you ready?" she asked. "You have to want it. Really want it."

Halv was silent a long time. His face worked. "I've wanted it for forty years," he said. "And been afraid of it for forty years. I don't know if there's a difference anymore."

"There is," Selin said gently. "Wanting is a door. Being afraid is a room. You've been living in the room."

He looked at her, and something in his eyes — the gray, old, empty thing — cracked a little at the edge. "Then open the door for me."

She uncorked the bottle.

The star rose. It lifted out of the glass like a held breath finally released, and it hung above Halv's head, and it trembled — not with cold, with hesitation — and it did not descend.

It waited.

Halv reached up a hand. The star leaned toward him, almost touching, and then drew back, and he lowered his hand, and his face went gray with something worse than cold.

"It won't," he said. "It doesn't know me. Forty years — "

"It knows you," Selin said. "It can't lie about belonging. It knows you better than you know yourself." She watched the star hover, brightening, aching, patient. "It's not that it won't. It's that it can't force itself. A star never forces. It waits until the wanting is real, and yours is still — tangled. You kept it in a vault. You kept yourself in a vault. It will come when you open both."

The empty bottle stood on the counter, uncorked, a cage that held nothing.

"Is it lost?" Halv asked. "If it doesn't come now — "

"It isn't lost." Selin smiled. "It's free. It's waiting. And it will wait for you as long as it took you to wait for it." She looked at the empty bottle. "This isn't a loss, Master Halv. This is mercy. Emptiness is a promise. It means there's room."

She left the empty bottle on the counter, where anyone could see it — the shop's sign, its own quiet heraldry: no cage hangs here anymore.

And above Halv, his star hung in the air of the shop like a held breath, waiting, patient, not gone.

Chapter 45: The Night

The second night was nothing like the first.

Selin remembered the first — the plan, the theft, the stolen key, the jars glittering in the dark while the stars wept quietly, knowing what was coming. Fear and fury and the cold shape in her chest. That night had been an uncorking.

This night was simply the night.

She and Kian sat on the roof of The Homecoming with their legs over the edge, the tiles warm from the day's light, and below them Velia glowed. The dome stood open, its great shutters swung wide, and the sky was full — not pressed at glass but hanging, deep, layered, alive. The city's light came up to meet it: returning stars in windows, lanterns in doorways, the poor quarter a low warm tide of gold where the dim gray had been all her life.

Kian's lanterns swung from the eaves and from strings strung between the houses — his whole paper sky, imitation light, and Selin looked at it and loved it, because it was what the poor quarter had made when it had no stars, and it had been beautiful then too.

"There's a word for this," Kian said. "When you're not doing anything and it's enough."

"What's the word?"

He thought. "Tuesday," he said.

Selin laughed, and her star laughed with her, a bright pulse inside her chest. It had come out to ride her shoulder tonight, and the wild star was with them too, returned from its travels, circling the roof in slow bright loops, choosing, not owned.

No plan. No theft. No hurry. The bells rang the hours below them and the sound came up soft and full, and the stars sang, and Selin could hear them now — all of them, a chorus, the whole sky speaking at once, and she understood each voice, and none of it asked anything of her.

"This is what it was for," Kian said, and his face, turned up to the open night, was lit by a hundred real stars and one paper sky. "The climb. The theft. The night you scared the Watch half to death. All of it — for this. For a night when there's nothing left to do."

Selin looked at the sky, and the sky looked back, and it brightened — the whole dome of it, listening, answering.

"For this," she agreed. And the stars sang, and she sat on the roof of the shop that used to sell light, with her star on her shoulder and her friend at her side and the open night above her, and she had never once in her life been more at home.

Chapter 46: The City

The city had a new song, and it rang itself all day.

Selin walked through Velia and heard it in pieces, the way you hear a song from inside the room it's being played in. The bells led it, of course — the little hour-bell of the poor quarter, the great tower bell, the small bright bells of the market terraces — but they were only the bones. Underneath, the city hummed.

Guild masters stood at their windows in the tall towers and watched the sky. They had come down from their ledgers. Some of them, gray-chested still, had begun the slow work of return — the registry was open, and the blue stamps were being struck, and the names were being matched. Selin had watched a collector walk out of the guild tower that morning carrying a jar like a man carrying his own sin, heading for the poor quarter to give it back.

Children pointed at the sky. That was the true chorus — the children, who had grown up under a dome they were taught to call a ceiling, pointing up at the open dark with both hands, learning the word stars before they learned the word cage.

In the market, a boy was reunited with his star.

Selin saw it happen. The boy was twelve, dim, selling fish, and a woman from the registry led his mother by the hand, and the jar came out, and the uncorking — small, total — and the boy's light found his chest and he dropped his fish and stood in the middle of the market and shone, and the whole square stopped to look at him, and the market came back to life around him like water closing over a stone.

A collector learned to give. A lonely man sat in The Homecoming and listened to the freed stars sing. The poor quarter's festival had spread up the hill, and the terraces of the rich, where the light had always been purchased and hoarded, were lit now with returning stars, and the rich stood at their windows and wept at the beauty of their own city.

Selin passed through it all, part of the light, wearing it the way other people wore coats. Her star sang inside her. The wild star circled somewhere above, a small bright comma in the open sky.

At dusk the bells stopped their separate hours and rang together, one long tolling chord, the city's new song, and every window in Velia stood open to hear it.

The sky, listening, brightened. The city, listening, rang.

Chapter 47: The Shining

The festival of return was the brightest night Velia had ever held.

Selin stood in the square with her star burning at her throat and the wild star on her shoulder and a hundred freed stars turning overhead, and she had to learn, all over again, how to be seen. She had spent seventeen years being dim and unremarkable — a girl who faded into the poor quarter's gray. Now she was the brightest thing in a bright city, and every eye found her, and she wanted, for the first hour, to hide.

"It's a lot," Kian said, appearing at her side with a paper lantern on a pole. "Being the light. Everyone looks at the light. The trick is to remember the light is just — carrying. It isn't you. It's what you carry."

"Easy for you to say," Selin said. "You're not the one — "

He laughed, and he was shining.

It stopped her. Kian, starless-born, the boy the city called soulless, stood in the middle of the festival with his paper lantern raised, and he was shining. Not with a star — he had no star, he had never had a star, the registry had no entry for him at all — but with something else. It came out of his skin, his eyes, his open laughing face, a warmth that owed nothing to the sky.

"You're — " Selin said.

"Full of myself," he said. "I told you. The city says I'm empty. I say I'm full of myself. Turns out that's a light too. Different kind. Doesn't come from the sky. Comes from — here." He tapped his chest. "They couldn't take it because they never knew it was there."

The city glowed around them. Everyone reunited shone — the fishwife, the boy from the market, the dim generations of the poor quarter, all lit, all whole. And the starless boy shone brightest of all, and the crowd felt it without understanding it, and children gathered around him and his paper lantern, and he told them the story of the starless boy and the sky that belonged to no one and everyone, and they laughed and held his hands.

Selin watched him, and understood the last piece of the trade. Light isn't owned. It's carried. And the ones with nothing to carry can carry the most.

At the edge of the crowd, an old man stood alone, watching.

Selin met his eyes across the square — Halv, small, quiet, a coat too big for the man he had been. His chest was still gray. But his face, in the light of a hundred returned stars, was almost warm.

The crowd moved between them, shining, and Selin stood with Kian in the middle of the bright square, and the city shone around them, and the starless boy out-glowed them all.

Chapter 48: The Legacy

He came to the shop on a quiet morning and laid three things on the counter: a ledger, a ring of keys, and his hands.

"The history," Halv said. "The ledger, every page. The keys — to the vault, the dome, the registry door. And my hands, which were the thieves' hands, and are now — " He looked at them. "I don't know what they are now."

Selin touched the ledger. It was the old one, the red one, seven hundred pages, and she had closed it forever and it had not stayed closed — here it was again, in Halv's careful keeping, a history someone had to own so it wouldn't be repeated.

"You kept it," she said.

"I kept everything," he said. "That was the trouble." He sat on the stool by the counter, and his star — free now, hovering somewhere above the shop, waiting, patient — turned toward the roof, and he felt it and did not look at it. "The trade isn't wealth, Selin. I thought it was. I hoarded light like money, and the hoard was the whole of me, and the hoard was empty." He looked at her, and his gray eyes were clear. "You'll give it because you can feel it. That's the whole trade. I kept light because I couldn't feel it. You'll give it because you can. That's the whole trade."

"Your last lesson," Selin said softly.

"My only lesson," he said. "It took me forty years to learn one sentence. The dome isn't a vault, it's a hand, held open. I held it closed until it went dead in my grip." He pushed the keys toward her. "Yours now. The shop, the trust, the sky's keeping. I was the Starlight Merchant. You're — you're something better. You're the keeper of the sky's trust. The trade passes through me to you, and it comes out clean on the other side."

Selin took the keys. They were warm from his pocket, worn smooth by forty years of worry, and she closed her hand around them and felt the weight of everything they opened.

"What will you do?" she asked.

Halv smiled, thin and real. "There's glass needs tending. The dome's a hundred years old and nobody's ever loved the panes — only the light inside them. I've spent my life keeping bottles. I think I'd like to keep the glass itself now. A keeper of panes. Something that holds light without claiming it."

He left the keys in her hand and walked out into the morning, and above him, patient, his star waited.

Selin stood at the counter of The Homecoming with the ledger and the keys warm in her palm, and she watched him go, and she made a promise to the empty air, and to the sky, and to herself.

I will give it. Because I can feel it.

Chapter 49: The Sky, Shared

The law was proclaimed on a morning of wind.

The whole city came. The guild masters in their tall towers. The poor quarter in its festival clothes, lit now, warm now. The Watch, standing at ease, no longer guarding the skylights, because there was nothing left to guard. And Halv, at the edge, in his coat with no keys, watching.

Selin stood on the guild steps, the open sky above her, and read the words she had written and the city had voted into being.

"The sky belongs to no one and everyone. No license shall be granted for the taking of light. No hook shall be drawn, no jar filled, no birth dimmed. The dome is not a vault. It is a hand, held open. The sky is not owned. It is shared."

The words went out like bells, and the city received them, and then the city did the work itself. Every hand that could reach a lever reached one. The great shutters of the dome, opened before, were thrown open again — all of them, forever, the hinges drawn and the bars carried away to the smiths, where they would become door-knockers and weather-vanes, things that open and things that point the way home.

The cage was fully broken.

And then, in the open sky, Halv's star came home.

It had waited — a year, patient, hovering above the shop, above the man, a held breath. It had waited for the wanting to be real. And Halv stood in the middle of the square, his hands empty and open, and he looked up at the open sky, and he said — not to anyone, to the sky, to himself — "I want it. I want to be warm again. Please. I'm ready."

The star descended.

It came down through the open dome, through the light, and it found the gray socket in his chest where his warmth had been torn out forty years ago, and it fitted itself into it, and the warmth of forty years arrived all at once.

Halv cried out — a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh — and fell to his knees in the middle of the square, and the warmth flooded him, the missing shape filled, and he wept, and he laughed, and he was held. Selin caught him. Kian took his other arm. And the three of them stood in the open sky, and the sky held them all, and the whole city watched a man who had been cold for forty years learn, for the first time, how to be warm.

"To be," Halv said, and his voice was young in a way it had never been, and his star blazed in his chest like a newborn sun. "I'd forgotten what it felt like. To be."

The sky, listening, brightened. The city, watching, shone. And the homecoming, at last, was complete.

Chapter 50: The Starlight

The years passed, and the sky stayed open, and the stars kept singing, and Selin kept answering.

She was the Starlight Merchant now — the old name, reclaimed and turned, hanging over the shop that had been The Homecoming and was now both, the words painted in gold over the door: THE HOMECOMING, and below it, smaller, the starlight merchant. People used the two names as if they were one.

The shop lit the city's lamps. That was the strange and simple wonder of it: light that was freely given, starlight caught in nothing, poured into no jar, shared from open windows into a hundred dark rooms. The lamps of Velia burned on starlight, and no one owned any of it, and everyone carried it.

The reunions went on. There were always more — a starless-born child learning what the starless boy had taught the city, that light isn't owned, it's carried; a collector returning a jar fifty years late; a dim old woman finding her light in a kitchen, over cold tea, the way Selin's mother had. Each one small. Each one total. Each one a homecoming.

Her mother kept the bottle. It was filled, still, with the light Selin had poured into it that night — given, not sold — and it sat on the shelf not as a wound but as a keepsake, like a pressed flower or a wedding cup. The mother held it at festivals, like a child. The father spoke now, halting, warm. The sister grew up bright, and asked the questions, and became, in time, a keeper of the registry, matching stars to their people, writing in blue.

Kian made lanterns. Paper, cheap, beautiful, imitation light — and the city hung them alongside the real thing, and no one could say which was more dear. He had out-glowed them all that one night, and he never stopped.

Halv kept the glass. He walked the dome's rim in all weathers, a keeper of panes, and his star burned in his chest, warm, and he was seen, sometimes, standing at the curve of the great glass with his hand flat against it, looking up at the open sky as if he could not quite believe it was his.

And on one last night, Selin climbed the dome alone.

She was older now, her steps surer. She climbed the winding stairs past the Watch's empty posts, out onto the rim, and stood where she had stood as a furious girl with a stolen jar and a stolen plan. The wind was cold. The sky was open. The stars hung in their thousands, freed and fixed, and she could not tell which were the ones she had uncorked and which had always been there — which was the point.

Her star came out of her chest and stood on her shoulder. The wild star came circling in from the dark, chose her, sang.

What is a star worth? she asked the sky.

Everything, sang the sky. And nothing.

You cannot buy it, sang the stars. You can only carry it.

And Selin stood on the rim of the open dome, the wind cold on her face, the sky full of lights, and in her chest the warm star — home.

The stars sang. And she answered.
