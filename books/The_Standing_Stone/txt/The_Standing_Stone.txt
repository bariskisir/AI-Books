The Standing Stone
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Noon Shadow

At noon the Long Meadow held its breath. The village lay behind its hedges and its stone walls, doors shut against the high sun, the mill quiet, the well rope still. Even the dogs slept. Only the meadow was awake, and the meadow was not sleeping—it was waiting, the way it waited every noon, for the shadow to tell the truth.

The Standing Stone stood at the meadow's heart. Taller than a house it was, and older than any name the village could remember, and at noon its shadow lay in the ring of worn earth that had been worn by eight hundred years of noon shadows, one after another, as faithful as the sun. The ring was the oldest record in the world. No one had made it. It had been made the way a path is made, by feet, by eight hundred years of feet—or rather by one shadow, coming back to the same place every day.

Alba lay in the long grass at the meadow's edge, her chin on her arms, her eyes half shut against the brightness. The flock was scattered around her, heads down, resting in the heat, the ewes folding their legs under them in the shade of the few thorn trees. She was not asleep. She was watching the shadow the way a herder watches weather—loosely, patiently, all the time, without seeming to.

She had watched it all her life. Every noon of her sixteen years she had been somewhere in this meadow, and the shadow had been there, lying in the ring, exactly as it lay now. It was as much a thing of the world as the sun that made it. You did not think about it any more than you thought about the sky.

And yet today, watching it, she felt the small cool place in her chest where her questions lived stir and widen. The shadow lay in the ring. Of course it lay in the ring. It always lay in the ring. It was the shape of trust, that worn groove of bare earth, the same shape every noon, and the village put its whole weight on it without ever noticing.

Alba closed her eyes against the glare and listened to the bees working the clover and the great quiet under the sound of them, the quiet where the stone stood. She did not know she was counting the noon shadows. She was simply lying in the grass, the youngest herder, the smallest of her father's children, watching the shadow the way herders watch weather, on a noon that was the last true noon there would be for a while.

Chapter 2: The Meadow

The meadow was the village's great room, and late spring was its best dress. The grass came up thick and green to the knees of a standing man, seeded with clover and the low white flowers the village called star-meat because the sheep grew fat on them. The Long Meadow it was called, and long it was—a full morning's walk from the stone walls of the village to the first rise of the Wold, with the stone standing at its center like a held note.

Alba came to the fold before first light, as she came every morning. She let down the bars and counted them out—one hundred and four ewes, forty-two lambs, the old ram whose horns curled like a winter moon—and the sheep walked past her in a river of wool, patient, unhurried, the way sheep are, and the dog went with them, black and white, herding from behind, gathering the stragglers without fuss.

The meadow had voices, if you knew how to listen. The bees at the clover. The larks that went up out of the grass in a shock of song and hung above the meadow like flung seed. The wind in the thorn trees at the edges, and under it all, always, the enormous quiet where the stone stood. Alba knew them all the way she knew the faces of the flock, and she could tell, without turning her head, the moment the dog had moved a ewe from one part of the meadow to another, by the change in the sound of the grazing.

The stone, seen from the flatness of the meadow, was not a pillar and was not a rock. It was a sleeper. That was what the eye kept wanting to say and the mind kept refusing to believe. The bowed head. The fold of an arm. A shoulder where the noon shadow tipped into the ring. It was taller than a house, and the grass came right up to its feet and could not quite cover the place where the earth had given under its weight. It had been lying there, asleep, longer than the village had been awake.

Alba let the flock spread into the morning. She sat on her heels where the grass was shortest and watched them graze, and watched the stone, and did not think of anything in particular. That was the whole of the herder's life, some days: the counting, the watching, the long patience of the grass. She was content in it. The flock had taught her to wait, and waiting had taught her to see.

Chapter 3: The Eight Hundred Years

Every child in the village knew the story of the stone, because every child had been told it, and every child told it to the next, the way a ball is handed on.

Once, the children said, there was a giant, the last of the giants, and he walked the world when the world was young. He walked along the top of the sky, where the wind is, and he held the turning of the seasons in his hands—spring when he turned east, summer when he turned south, and so on, the whole round, year after year. But the old world fell, and the sky-edge broke, and the giant came down, wounded, into the meadow that would one day be the Long Meadow, and he lay down there and would not get up. He promised the first village—so the children said—that he would hold their time for them, and the village promised him something back, though no one now remembered what. And he fell asleep keeping it, and he slept so long that stone grew over him, and he is still there, asleep under the stone, and the shadow is his breath.

The old women's version was longer and quieter and had no giant in it at first. They said the stone had stood there before their grandmothers' grandmothers, and the noon-ring had been worn into the earth before the oldest of the old could remember it being worn, and that the ring was a tally—eight hundred years of noons, each one marked by the shadow coming back to the same place, one after another, like marks cut into a tally stick. The stone kept the count. That was what it was for. When the sun stood at its standing-still and the shadow lay in the ring, the world was telling the truth, and the village could trust the day to keep its length and the fields to ripen.

Alba had heard both versions all her life, and she had never once thought to ask whether they were true. The stone was older than the stories about it. It was furniture of the world, like the hill and the well, so old that asking about it was like asking about the sun—the question had no handle to grip it by.

But she had talked to it, all her life. When she was small she had asked it why the grass grew, and whether the lambs remembered their mothers, and what the wind said. It had never answered, and it had never hurt her, and it had only ever been still. That was how she knew it was not a thing. Things did not make you feel, when you talked to them, that they were listening.

She counted that as nothing. Every herder talks to the quiet. It is the quiet you work beside.

Chapter 4: The Drift

It happened on a noon like any other, which is how such things always happen.

The day was bright and still. Alba had brought the flock to the center of the meadow for their midday rest, as she did when the heat came on, and she was sitting in the grass with her back to the stone, eating the bread and cheese her father had wrapped for her, watching the shadow of the stone with the idle part of her mind, the way she watched everything.

The shadow lay in the ring. It always lay in the ring. And then, without her knowing she had seen it, a small cold thing went off in her chest, like a stone dropped into still water, and she turned her whole attention onto the shadow and looked at it properly.

It was out of the ring.

She did not believe it. She looked again, and the ring was there—the worn groove of bare earth, curving as it always curved—and the shadow was there, and they did not quite match. The shadow lay a finger's width out of the groove, to the west, toward the Wold. A finger's width. No more. You would not see it unless you were looking for it, and no one was looking for it, because it had never happened before in eight hundred years.

Alba got up slowly, the bread still in her hand, and walked to the ring. She knelt at the edge of the worn earth and laid her hand flat against it, palm down, and looked at the line of the shadow where it crossed her fingers. It did not cross where the ring said it should cross. It lay a little beyond, in the uncut grass, and the grass there was bent, as if something had rubbed it.

She took a stick and pushed it into the earth at the shadow's end, hard, so that it stood straight up at the very point of the shadow, and she walked back to her place and sat down and watched. The stick stood in the grass like a question.

All the long noon she watched, and the shadow crept down the stick's height, and when the afternoon came on and the shadow began to stretch, she pulled the stick out and hid it in the fold of her skirt, because she did not want anyone to see it and laugh, and she did not want it to be true.

The sheep had changed their grazing. That was the other thing. All her life the flock had rested in the long noon under the stone's shadow, in the corner of the ring, in the cool. This noon they had grazed wide of it, keeping a little distance from the ring, as if the shadow's new place troubled them, or as if they knew before she did that something had moved.

Chapter 5: The Finger's Width

The next noon it was the same, and the noon after that, and the noon after that.

Alba brought the flock in and sat in the same place and watched, and the shadow lay out of the ring by a finger's width, in the same new place, as settled as if it had always lain there. She measured it with her thumb, the way you measure a cloth, laying her thumb along the gap between the groove and the shadow's edge. A finger's width. No more, no less.

She put the stick back in the ground, and she kept the flock away from the ring's corner, the way the sheep themselves had taught her, and for three days she watched the shadow and said nothing, because she wanted to be sure, and because she was not sure she trusted what she saw. Herders know about the shifting of shadows. The sun climbs and the sun falls and the year leans toward the Wold and away from it, and shadows move with it, and the ring is wider than the shadow anyway, wider than a finger, and a finger's width might be nothing.

But the ring was the ring. The shadow had lain in it for eight hundred years. And what she saw was not the shadow moving with the season. It was the shadow leaving the ring.

On the fourth noon she told her father.

He was at the fold, mending a gate, and she stood in front of him with her hands behind her back and said, plainly, the way she said everything: "The shadow is out of the ring. A finger's width. Toward the Wold."

Tobin the herder straightened and looked at her, and she could see him decide to smile. "Shadows shift," he said. "The sun is high now. Come autumn you'll see it lie back."

"It is not the sun," Alba said. "It is the stone."

Her father looked at her a moment longer. He did not laugh. He put his hand on her head, the way he had done since she was small. "You watch it, then," he said. "You watch it close. Tell me if it moves."

She told the headman too, because her father said she might, and Old Rolf heard her out over the well rope, and he smiled the way her father had smiled, kindly, and said that shadows shift with the season, and that the ring had stood eight hundred years and would stand eight hundred more.

And Rafe, her eldest brother, was at the well. He laughed, the laugh of a big brother at a small sister's fancy. "A finger's width," he said. "Alba, you'd find a storm in a puff of wind."

She did not argue. She was the youngest of seven, and she had learned early that words spent on laughter were words wasted. She nodded and went back to the flock, and she stopped talking about it. But she watched the shadow every noon, and measured it with her thumb, and said nothing, because no one was listening, and because she was certain, in the small cool place in her chest, that the shadow was moving and that the ring knew it.

Chapter 6: The Hand's

A week of noons, and the shadow went out of the ring by a hand's width.

Alba was the one who saw it first, because she was the one who had been watching, but she was not the one who spoke of it. What she saw was a hand's width, measured flat, her whole hand laid between the groove and the shadow's edge, and she stood looking at it for a long time, and she did not think, I was right. She thought, It is moving faster now. And that frightened her more than being right ever could have.

That noon she did not bring the flock in to rest in the meadow's heart. She kept them at the edge, by the fold, where the shadow's long reach could not touch them, and she stood at the bars and watched the stone, and she saw the mill stop.

The mill had stopped at noon since before anyone could remember—the midday rest, the turning down of the great wheel while the sun stood still. But this noon the miller came out and stood in his yard and looked, and did not go back in. And the well rope hung still. And the doors of the houses opened, one by one, and the people came out into the lane and stood, and then they began to walk, without any word passing, down the lane and out through the gap in the wall into the Long Meadow, all of them, together, the way a flock moves without being told.

Alba watched them come. Old Rolf the headman at the front, and the miller, and Sander with his miller's flour still on his arms, and the old women, and the children running ahead. They stopped at the ring's edge, where Alba's stick still stood, and they looked at the shadow, lying a hand's width out of the groove, and no one said anything for a long moment.

Then Old Rolf spoke, and his voice cracked on the first word, like a boy's, and he had to clear his throat and begin again.

"It was in the ring," he said. "It has always been in the ring."

And no one answered him, because there was no answer. The shadow lay in the grass, in a place no noon shadow had lain in eight hundred years, and the grass there was bent and pale where it had rubbed for a week, and the ring lay empty, a worn groove holding nothing, like a hand holding air.

Someone said the stone had moved. Someone said the sun had moved. Someone said the world was coming untethered, and the days would not keep their length, and the fields would not ripen.

Alba stood a little apart, at the fold bars, and watched them all looking at the empty ring, and for the first time in her life she was afraid to ask a question. Because the shadow had not moved. She had watched it, measured it, all week, and the stone stood where it had always stood, sunk in the same earth, warm at noon. It was the shadow that had gone out of the ring. And she did not know what that meant, but she knew it was not the sun.

Chapter 7: The Fear

The fear had a voice by evening, and it filled the meeting hall.

They came in from the meadow when the light went long, and they lit the lamps, and they argued, because arguing is what people do with fear when they cannot hold it. Old Rolf stood at the head of the hall and let them talk, and the talk went every way at once.

Prop the stone up, said the miller. If it is leaning, prop it, and the shadow will come back. No, said the smith, it is not leaning—look at it, it stands as it has always stood. Move it, then. Put it back. You cannot move eight hundred years. Make an offering—the old ways wanted offerings when the world went wrong. We have no old ways, said someone; we have a ring and a shadow and a stone, and the stone is failing.

Burn the meadow. That came from the back, from a voice no one could name, and the hall went quiet, and then went loud again, worse than before, because burning the meadow is what you say when you are very afraid and very small and the thing in front of you is very old.

The day that never ends, someone said. That was the deepest fear, and it went around the hall in whispers, the way cold goes around a house. If the shadow no longer lies true, then noon no longer lies true, and if noon lies, the days will not keep their length, and the day will stretch and stretch and never end, and the fields will not ripen, and the children will not know winter from summer, and the world will come untethered and drift, the way the shadow drifts.

Alba sat against the wall, in the dark at the back of the hall, and she did not speak. No one had asked her to speak. She was the youngest herder, the one who had been laughed at for a finger's width, and the fear had grown too big for her small voice to matter in it. So she listened, and she watched, the way she had been taught by the flock.

Elder Greta sat at the front, by the fire, and said nothing at all.

Greta was the oldest woman in the village, past eighty, thin as a reed, with the last fragments of the old tongue held in her mouth like seeds in a dead hand. She had said nothing at the meadow, and she said nothing in the hall. She sat with her hands folded on her stick and listened to the argument the way the stone listens, and once, when the talk rose highest, Alba saw her look up, her eyes going past the lamps, out to where the meadow lay under the dark, to the stone.

And when the hall emptied at last, the people going out into the night with the fear still on them, arguing into the dark, Greta stayed. She sat a long time by the dead fire, and Alba, going out last with the lamp, looked back, and saw the old woman's face in the dim, turned toward the meadow, toward the stone, as if she were waiting for someone who would ask it properly for her.

Chapter 8: The Covenant

In the morning the fear was smaller and the shame was larger, and the village gathered in the meeting hall again, quieter now, and it was Old Rolf who spoke the word everyone had been avoiding, the word that had hung in the air all night like smoke.

"Covenant," he said. "There is a covenant with the stone. There has always been a covenant."

And the hall was silent, because it was true, and because no one could say what was in it.

They tried. The children's story—the giant who held the seasons, who promised the first village his standing, and was promised something back. But what? No one knew. The old women's version—the noon-ring as a tally, the stone keeping the count of noons. But what was the count for, and who was it owed to? The old songs—the fragments the women sang at midsummer, words without meaning now, half-understood sounds handed down like worn stones. The agreement was remembered the way a dream is remembered: bright in the morning, gone by noon, and no one could reach back and hold it.

"I have the fragments," said Old Rolf. "Three words, and half a song. It is all anyone has." And he looked at Elder Greta, and the hall looked at her too, because everyone knew that the fragments lived in her keeping, that she was the last mouth the old tongue had.

Greta sat by the fire as she had sat the night before, and for a long moment she did not speak. Then she stood, very slowly, leaning on her stick, and she said, in her thin old voice, the words of the old tongue, one by one, so that they would last:

"Vae," she said. "Mund. Aeld."

And then, in the common tongue, she told them what the words meant, as her grandmother had told her, as the words came down:

"Vae is a promise. Mund is the heart-memory, the thing held in the heart so it is not lost. Aeld is the shadow, the dark that goes with a body and tells where it is."

She stopped, and the hall was very quiet.

"There is a name," Greta said. "There was a name. The covenant was a name." Her hands were shaking on her stick. "The stone has a name. And it has been so long since it was spoken that no one living knows it."

The hall breathed. Alba, at the back, felt the small cool place in her chest open wide, and she understood, without knowing how, that Greta had been waiting for this, and that the fear in the hall and the quiet in the old woman were the same thing seen from two sides.

"The stone holds up its end," said Greta. "It has held up its end for eight hundred years. And we have lost the name that was our end." She looked out at them all. "The covenant is not broken by the stone. It has been broken by silence."

Chapter 9: The Sending

So the village came to the thing it feared, because there was nothing else left to come to.

If the covenant was a name, and the name was lost, then someone must go and ask the stone what the name was. That was the whole of it, and it was simple, and no one could do it.

The strong men looked at their boots. The miller examined his hands. The smith studied the far wall of the hall. The herders—Tobin among them—found urgent business at the fold. They were not cowards; they were men of sheep and iron and grain, and a stone that answered questions was outside everything they knew how to meet. A wolf you could meet. A flood you could meet. A shadow that had left its groove for the first time in eight hundred years—what did you do?

The elders talked among themselves in low voices, and then Old Rolf stood and said what they had concluded.

"It wants a child's heart," he said. "The old stories say the stone answers the one who asks properly, and a child asks properly—a child asks because it wants to know, not because it wants to win. It wants the youngest. The smallest. The one who is not afraid."

And the hall looked around, seeking the youngest, and found her at the back, against the wall. Alba saw the look come to rest on her, and she remembered that she had told them first. She had stood at the well and told them, and they had smiled, and Rafe had laughed, and now the shadow lay a hand's width out of the ring and they were all looking at her, and she was not afraid.

That was the strange, quiet truth of it. The strong men were afraid and hid it; the elders were afraid and covered it with words; and Alba, who had talked to the stone all her life and never once been hurt by it, was not afraid. The stone had only ever been still. It had only ever held noon true. It was the one thing in all the world that had never lied.

"Yes," she said, plainly, the way she said everything, and the hall let out its held breath, and Old Rolf nodded, and no one argued. The choosing was the whole of it: the youngest herder, the smallest of her father's children, the one who asked questions all her life and was not afraid of answers.

Her father walked with her to the meadow's edge. He did not speak, and she did not need him to. He put his hand on her head, the way he had done since she was small, and stopped where the grass began.

The whole village stopped there. They walked her to the meadow's edge, and they stopped, as if the meadow itself were the boundary of what their courage could cross, and they stood in a line along the wall and watched her go on alone.

Alba walked into the long grass. She did not look back. The flock was at the fold, watching her too, and the stone stood at the meadow's heart, warm at noon, patient, waiting, and she walked toward it.

Chapter 10: Alba

The village was behind her, and the flock was at the fold, and the stone was ahead, and she walked, and because she was a herder, and because walking and thinking are the same thing to a herder, she thought about her life, the whole long and short of it, as she went.

She was the youngest of seven. That was the first thing anyone said of her: Tobin's youngest, the afterthought, the one who came when the others were grown and gone, and so was left to the flock. Her mother Elka had died when Alba was small—a winter fever, the same that took the old ram—and she remembered her mother only as warmth, and a voice, and the way the house had smelled of bread. What she remembered better, and longer, was the flock. The flock had raised her. The sheep had taught her patience the way mothers teach it, by standing still in the weather until the weather passed.

What she knew was a herder's knowledge, and it was a long and a quiet list. She knew sheep—their faces, their tempers, the hundred-and-four ewes and the forty-two lambs and the old ram, each by name and by history. She knew weather: the smell of rain in the wind off the Wold, the way the larks went down before a storm, the exact hour of the grass's standing water in the first light. She knew the meadow, every fold and hollow of it, the thorn trees and the star-meat clover. And she knew the stone. She knew its shadow the way other people knew a clock. When the shadow lay in the ring, it was noon, and noon was the moment when the world told the truth, and the village set its whole life by it—the mill, the meals, the fields, the turning of the seasons—and Alba had read that clock since she could see.

She was not afraid of the stone. She had thought about that, walking, the way you think in the open air. And the answer was simple. She was not afraid because she had asked it questions all her life, and it had never hurt her. When she was small she had asked it why the grass grew, and whether the lambs remembered their mothers, and what the wind said. It had never answered, and it had never hurt her, and it had only ever been still. You do not fear the thing that has never hurt you. You fear the things you have not asked. The strong men at the hall were not afraid of the stone. They were afraid of what it might say if they asked—afraid of the answer, afraid of the name, afraid of their own silence.

Alba was not afraid, because she asked questions instead of fearing answers.

She came to the ring and stood at its edge. The shadow lay a hand's width beyond it, in the bent pale grass, and the ring lay empty, and she looked at the worn groove, the oldest record in the world, the tally of eight hundred years of true noons.

"Hello," she said to the stone. And she sat down at its feet, in the lee of its shoulder, where she had sat a thousand times, and she waited.

Chapter 11: The Youngest

Being the youngest of seven, she had learned early that no one waited for her to finish speaking. The table talk went on over her head; the orders went past her to stronger hands. So she listened, and because no one expected her to answer, she heard everything. The sheep taught her the rest of it — a ewe does not need to be heard to know the weather; she watches the grass bend and the sky's edge and the way the other ewes are standing. Alba had watched all her life, and watching had made her patient, and patience had made her brave, though she had not known that until the meadow.

Rafe laughed when she told them about the finger's width. Her eldest brother, big as the mill's beam, laughing with his mouth full of bread, and the whole table laughing with him — all except her father, who went on eating and said nothing. It was Rafe who did not laugh now. He had gone silent the day the drift was a hand's width, and his silence had shame in it. She saw it in the way he did not meet her eyes, in the way he stood at the edge of the crowd when the village sent her, and she understood that he was afraid to speak her name, afraid she would hear in it the apology he did not know how to make.

Her father had walked her as far as the fold. "You saw it first," Tobin said, and that was all he said on the matter, and it was enough. It was more than enough. Among seven children a quiet faith from her father was the whole of a blessing, and she carried it with her across the meadow like a stone in her pocket, warm and small and certain.

The elders had said the stone needed a child's heart. She had thought about that on the walk, the flock left behind, her staff over her shoulder. And now, standing at the stone's foot, she understood the other gift of being youngest, the one nobody had named aloud: she was small enough to fit into the lee of its shoulder. The shadow of the great bowed head lay over her like a roof. She had stood here a hundred times, a thousand, asking questions no one answered, and the stone had never once hurt her. It had only ever been still.

She sat down in the lee, her back against the stone's warmth, and settled herself to wait the way she had waited all her life for her turn to speak.

Chapter 12: The Stone

Now that she studied it, the stone was not a pillar. It was a figure — a head bowed, an arm folded, a shoulder where the noon shadow tipped and fell. Standing close, she saw grass growing in the folds of its limbs, in the hollow of its elbow and the crook where the great head met the chest, small bright lines of green like the seams of a sleeper's blanket. It had been rooted so long that moss had thatched the bent arm, and a bird had nested in the cup of its shoulder and flown off at her approach, and the nest was still there, and she did not touch it.

She had known the stone all her life the way you know a hill — a fact of the world, neither face nor name. Now she looked at it the way she looked at a ewe gone quiet, for signs. The bowed head. The folded arm. The weight of it, heavier than the meeting hall, heavier than the mill, heavier than all the village's houses together, and yet it gave off no threat. At noon she pressed her palm flat against its side and the warmth came back at her, sun-warm and patient, like the flank of a resting animal.

In the evening, when the sun went down behind the Wold, the warmth was gone. The stone gave back the day's heat slowly and then not at all, and its coolness had a different quality from the air — the cool of a deep place, a root, a held depth. She learned its moods by temperature. Warmth was its peace. And she noticed, even then, though she could not yet name it, that the warmth at noon was less than it used to be, the shadow thinner, as if the stone were saving its heat for something.

She sat at its feet and waited. The presence of it was like a held breath — the meadow held itself around the stone, the bees keeping their distance, the wind flattening in its lee. She had talked to it all her life. Now she was learning to sit with it, which was different. Sitting with was a kind of asking that did not use words.

The first day she walked around it slowly, the way you circle a sleeping thing to learn which way it lies. The second day she sat. The shadow moved across the grass with the sun, and she moved with it, keeping to its cool side, and by evening she knew the stone as she knew a favorite ewe — by weight and heat and habit, and the particular way it stood.

She was ready, then, to ask.

Chapter 13: The Question

The questions she had carried came out in the order she had carried them, and each one was wrong.

"Why are you moving?" she asked, at noon, alone. The shadow lay a hand's width out of the ring, west toward the Wold, and it did not answer. The grass held its silence. She waited until her legs ached and the bees went back to their work, and nothing had happened.

"Are you angry?" she asked the next day. The shadow did not stir. It did not so much as shiver at the word angry, and she felt foolish, and then she understood that she had been foolish, which was a beginning.

"What do you want?" she asked on the third day, and for a moment the shadow shivered — a thin ripple across its length, like a sleeper turning in a dream — and then it was still, and she knew that whatever the shiver was, it was not an answer to what do you want. The stone did not want. It was not a question the stone could hold.

She sat in the lee of its shoulder through the afternoon, turning the three questions over in her hands the way she turned wool, looking for the knot. They were all the same question, she saw at last. Why. What do you want. Are you angry. They were questions of fear, every one — and fear asks, and fear gets nothing, because fear is not listening. All her life she had asked the stone questions out of curiosity, questions that did not need answering, and it had been still and had never hurt her. This was the first time she had ever come to it afraid, and the stone had closed, the way a sleeping thing closes to a shout.

So she stopped carrying fear. It took her a day to put it down. She woke on the fifth morning with it gone, the way weather clears, and she walked to the meadow without the questions in her mouth, only the old ease of her childhood, the habit of talking to the thing she had always talked to.

At noon she stood in the ring, in the place where the true shadow used to lie. She was alone. She put her whole heart into the asking, the way you put your whole weight on a gate you are sure will give.

"What are you called?"

The shadow rose.

Chapter 14: The Name

It rose like a thing lifting its head. The shadow that had lain flat on the grass since before her grandmother's grandmother was born stood up off the ground, and the air in the meadow went still.

It wrote. The shadow moved over the grass and wrote — tall dark letters, one after another, in the script of the old tongue, letters shaped like no letters she had ever seen, tall as a man, and the grass held them. That was the marvel, the one she would remember all her life: the grass held them. Each letter stood dark in the green, held for a moment, long enough to read with her eyes, and then let go, fading as the next letter came up, the way one note of a song gives way to the next.

She read them with her eyes and lost them as they faded. The old writing came too fast, and she had no letters of her own to catch it with. She made a small sound, the sound of losing something, and her hand went down — her staff was in her fist — and she scratched into the packed earth of the ring with the staff's iron tip, catching the first letters as they came. The dirt held them better than the grass. She worked the staff in a frenzy, copying each letter before it could fade, her mouth moving, counting under her breath.

There were more letters than she had fingers. She dropped to her knees and drew with the charred twig from the fold's fire, the one she used to mark wool, onto her palm — small, cramped, her palm sweating, the char smearing. Too many. The shadow went on writing, patient as weather, and she went on losing.

Then she remembered the birch bark in her tunic, the flat white piece she kept for wrapping her noon bread, and she pulled it out and knelt in the ring and drew the letters on it with the charred twig, fast and frightened, her hand shaking, terrified of losing them again. She had lost the first ones — she knew it even as she wrote — but the shape of the name was coming together, the letters mounting like a flock counted twice.

The shadow finished. It wrote the last letter and stood still a moment, and then it lay down, settling back into the place where the drift had put it, and the meadow let out its breath.

She sat in the grass with the bark in her two hands, letters all over it, dark on white, and she counted them, and she did not know a single one of them, and she held them anyway, tight, the way you hold a thing the world has given you once.

She ran. Greta's cottage, the whole way across the meadow, the name in her fist.

Chapter 15: The Old Tongue

Elder Greta's hands shook when she took the bark. Alba had seen those hands steady at a loom, steady at a doctoring, steady at the midsummer fire for sixty years, and now they shook, and the shaking told her more than any words could.

Greta did not speak. She carried the bark to the window where the light came in and held it up, and Alba saw her mouth move, soundless, tracing the first letters. Then she sounded them, one by one, aloud — the old tongue coming alive in her mouth for the first time in decades, and her mouth knew it even where her mind had forgotten. The letters came out crooked and slow, and then, as her mouth remembered, less crooked, less slow.

"Vae," she said. "Vae-el..." and her voice broke.

Alba did not understand. The letters had made a word, an old word, and Greta had wept. Greta put the bark down very carefully, as if it were a living thing, and covered her face with her two hands, and her shoulders shook, and Alba stood in the cottage light and waited, because that was what you did with grief — you waited beside it.

When Greta lifted her face, her cheeks were wet and her eyes were clear, and she was smiling — a worn, astonished smile, the smile of a person handed back something they had given up for dead.

"Vae," she said again, slowly, teaching her own mouth. "Promise. The first letters are vae. Promise. My grandmother left me three words, girl. Three words and a song, and the first of them was vae. I have carried it sixty years and never once heard it spoken back to me, and here it is, in your hand, on your bark."

She took the bark up again, and her voice steadied, and she went on sounding the letters, and each one came into the cottage like a thing given a home. The old tongue filled the room. Alba watched her mouth and learned the shapes as Greta said them, and knew she was watching something be born again that had been a long time dead.

At the end Greta was silent, holding the bark, the whole name sounded out, lying between them in the air.

"Now," Greta said, in a voice worn thin with holding back, "now we find out what it means."

Chapter 16: The Meaning

Greta read the bark out twice more, sounding the letters until they ran smooth, and then she set to work the way Alba's father worked a fleece — slowly, at the edges, finding the grain.

"The old tongue builds words the way a wall builds stones," Greta said. "Vae. I know this one. Promise. An agreement." She tapped the bark. "And this part, here — mund. Heart-memory. The thing kept in the chest of the chest, what the body keeps when the mind forgets. My grandmother said mund is what the sheep have when the snow is gone and they still remember where the good grass was."

Alba waited.

"And between them, folded small," Greta said, "aeld. Shadow. The old tongue softens its joins, and shadow folds down to a thin letter in the middle of the name." She sounded it whole. "Vae-aeld-mund. Vaelmund."

The name settled into Alba, the way a stone settles into a pool, all the way down.

Greta was quiet, and then she spoke. "It means the one who remembers the agreement. The one who holds the shadow of the promise in his heart-memory. And do you see what this is, girl? My grandmother's three words. All three of them, kept apart sixty years, and they were never three words at all. They were one name, broken, so that no one would find it unless they found it whole." She looked at the bark. "The name is not a label. That is what I see now, and I have been blind not to see it. We name a thing to put a handle on it. But this name is not a handle. It is a duty. He is Vaelmund because that is what he does. He IS the remembering. The agreement is held in his body the way mund is held in the heart, and we have walked past him three hundred years without one word of it."

Greta was silent a long moment, and then she told Alba what she knew. It was little, and it was old, and it came in fragments, the way the old tongue came. There had been a world before this one. In it there had been great ones — the giants, Greta called them — and they had held the order of time, the turning of the seasons, the standing-still of the sun at noon. Then that world had fallen, the way a tree falls, and the giants had fallen with it, and time had run loose. Seasons ran mad. Days lost their length. And into that mad world the first village had come — starving, season-mad, desperate.

"And into that village," Greta said, "came the last of the giants. Wounded. Down off the Wold. And he made them a promise, and they made him a promise, and the one who remembers both of them is standing in our meadow."

She looked at Alba across the bark.

"So now we know what he is. The next question is what we promised him."

Chapter 17: The Remembering

The understanding came to Alba in the night, walking the edge of the fold with the flock asleep, and it came whole, the way a lamb comes — all at once, after all the waiting.

The drift was not the stone breaking its promise. That was the fear the village had carried, and it was wrong. The stone had not moved. It had not loosened its hold on noon, had not let the days run slack, had not failed them. It was the stone that was going out of the world — fading, like a word no one says anymore, thinning like a name on a slate no hand refreshes. The shadow had not walked away from the ring. The shadow had grown thin. The stone was not leaving. The stone was being forgotten, and the forgetting was doing it.

A covenant has two sides. She turned it over, and it was so plain she wondered why no one had ever said it — or perhaps they had, and no one had listened, the way no one had listened to her about the finger's width. The stone had held his half for eight hundred years. One side holding a promise alone cannot hold it long. Even a giant — especially a giant, who had given everything he had to the holding — cannot hold a promise alone forever. The name was the other half. The name was what the village owed, and the village had stopped paying, and the debt had been compounding for three hundred years in the only currency that cannot be replaced: memory.

She stood at the fold's edge and looked across the dark meadow to the black shape of the stone, and she was not afraid. That surprised her, and then she understood it. Fear had belonged to the first shape of the thing — the fear that the world was coming untethered, that the covenant was broken, that the stone had turned against them. But that shape was wrong. The true shape was the saddest thing she had ever known, and it was not frightening. You cannot fear a thing that is fading for love of you. You can only hurry to hold it up.

The whole of it lay clear in her in the dark: the stone holding noon for eight hundred years, the village forgetting, the giant going out of the world like a word unsaid, and the one thing that could call him back — the name — spoken again.

It gave her the next question. Not why. Not are you angry. Not what do you want. The proper question, the one the covenant waited for: what are we required to give?

She walked back to the fold and slept, and in the morning she went to Greta's cottage and asked it.

Chapter 18: The Agreement

Greta was waiting for her as if she had known she would come. The records chest stood open on the table — a chest of dark wood, iron-bound, that Alba had seen all her life in the corner of the cottage and never once thought to ask about. That was what the forgetting did, she saw now. It made the important things into furniture.

Inside the chest, under the tallies and the fragments and the bones of old quills, was a scroll. Greta lifted it out with both hands, as if it were a child, and laid it on the table, and together they unrolled it. It was old beyond Alba's reckoning. The leather was cracked and brown, and the writing had faded to the color of the leather itself, so that for a moment she thought it was blank — a scroll that had outlived its own words. Then Greta brought the lamp close, and the words came up out of the dark like fish rising to light.

There were two hands on the scroll. At the foot, one in the old tongue — tall, dark, sure, the letters the shadow had written, the letters on her birch bark. The giant's hand. His mark, at the foot of his own covenant.

Above it, and beside it, the other hand — the old common tongue, the letters she had learned from her mother, and it too was half-faded, thinner than the first, as if written in a fainter ink, or held less firmly.

Greta read it aloud, translating as she went, and the agreement came out in the plain words of their own speech, and Alba heard it whole for the first time.

The stone gave. His standing, and his time — every noon he would hold, stone-still, so that the days would keep their length and the fields would ripen true. His strength, rooted into the meadow. And his own first name — the name he had carried in the old world, the name that was his alone — that he gave up, so that nothing of his old life would pull at him, so that he could hold noon and nothing else. What he kept was only the name of his duty. Vaelmund. The one who remembers the agreement.

The village promised. One thing only, and it was small, and it was everything: that at midsummer, every year, all together, they would speak his name aloud — the name of his duty, since his own was given up — so that he would not fade, so that he would be held in the world by their remembering. The rite was set down in plain words: midsummer, noon, the village gathered, the name spoken three times.

Alba read it twice. She read the giant's mark and the village's mark, and she saw what the agreement really was — not a law, but two parties holding each other's need.

"Three hundred years," she said. "We let it go three hundred years."

Greta said nothing. She was looking at the mark of the giant's hand.

Chapter 19: The Fallen Giant

It was Greta who told the story, sitting at the table with the scroll open between them, and the story came out of her the way the old tongue had come out — crooked at first, then finding its shape. Much of it she knew; the rest she read, halting, from the scroll's opening lines, which were themselves a telling.

There had been a world before this one. In it the giants had walked the sky-edge — the rim of the world, where the horizon meets what is beyond — and they had held the turning of the seasons. That was their work and their duty and their nature: the order of time moved through their bodies the way sap moves through a tree. They turned the year. They held the sun at its noon standing-still. They kept the days to their length. The world kept its time because they held it.

Then the world fell. Greta did not know how, and the scroll did not say, or the telling was lost, the way so much was lost. But the old world came apart, the way a frost breaks a field, and the giants fell with it. Some fell and were gone. Some faded, like words out of a language. And time ran loose. The seasons ran mad — winter and summer trampling each other, days that would not end, nights that would not lift, the sun standing still too long in one place and not long enough in another. What had been held was now unheld.

Into that mad world the first village came — the village that had been here before the village, starving, season-mad, desperate. Its people had forgotten the names of the seasons. They planted when the ground was wrong and reaped when the fields were bare, and their children were thin, and their old ones went before their time.

And the last giant came down. Wounded — the scroll said it, and Greta's voice faltered on it — down off the Wold, into the Long Meadow, and stood where the stone stands now. He was the last of his kind, the last one left to hold anything. And he looked at the starving village, and he made them the offer. The bargain was struck at the foot of the hill, there in the meadow, on a day of the mad time when the sun would not stay still.

He would give them his standing. He would root himself in the meadow and take on the stone, and hold noon true for them, so that the days would keep their length and the seasons would remember their order and the fields would ripen. He would hold it as long as his body could hold.

In exchange, they would do one thing. They would remember him. They would speak his name at midsummer, every year, all together — so that he would not fade into the nothing that takes the forgotten.

"He knew," Greta said, very low. "He knew what he was giving up. He gave his time, and his standing, and his own first name. And he asked only to be kept."

Chapter 20: The Holding

Alba lay awake that night and turned it over: what it meant, to hold noon.

The giant had not simply stood. He had rooted — slowly, willingly, taken into stone, the way a river takes into the shape of its bed. It had not happened in a day. The scroll said he grew into it, the stone growing over him like grass over a path, until the man was gone and the shape remained: the bowed head, the folded arm, the shoulder where the shadow tipped. He had chosen the shape he kept. He had kept the shape of a sleeper, so that he could rest while he held.

And the holding itself. That was the part she could not grasp and could not stop reaching for. Eight hundred years of noons. Every day the sun climbing to its highest, and the shadow of his body lying true in the ring, holding the day to its length, holding the year to its turning. The village's time held in his body — the days they lived, the harvests they reaped, the children they raised, all of it standing, in a way she could not have explained and did not need to, on the giant's patience. He had held noon for eight hundred years. The noon-ring was the tally of it, worn into the earth by the shadow lying true — the oldest record in the world.

His long patience. She thought about patience, she who had been taught patience by sheep. The flock taught you to wait for the thing that would come in its own time. But this was different. This was waiting for nothing — holding a thing steady that had no need of being held, for a people who had stopped remembering him three hundred years ago and would never have known, in all that time, if he had stopped. He had not stopped. He had gone on holding, alone, one side of a covenant no one held the other end of.

And he had slept, and he had dreamed. The scroll said it, or Greta had read it in the old words: he dreamed of the sky-edge he used to walk. The rim of the world. The place where the horizon meets what is beyond. All his long rooted years, asleep in his stone, he walked the old sky in his dream and held the village's noon with his body, both at once.

Alba lay in the dark above the fold and felt the whole weight of it come down on her — not crushing her, but settling, the way a load settles on a back that can carry it. He had given everything. Everything except one thing, which the village had been given to keep and had lost.

She would find it again. That was all there was to it now. She held the name on the bark under her pillow like a seed, and in the morning she rose early, and before the flock, before her father, she went to the meadow and said the name to the stone, low and alone, the first of the keeping.

Vaelmund.

Chapter 21: The Fading

Alba went to the stone at noon and pressed her palm to its flank, and the warmth that met her was less than the warmth of a week ago. Not by much. By the feel of a hand held against a cheek at evening. But the stone had been warm as a sleeper's back all her life, and now it was warm the way a thing is warm that is beginning to think of cold.

She told Greta, and Greta nodded, slow, as if the news had been expected.

"Warmth is mood," Greta said. "You told me the shadow is the cry. The cold is something else. It is the fading. The same thing, said differently."

Alba had come to understand the drift. It was not the stone moving. It was the stone thinning. She had thought of the world as a place that held things — held the meadow, held the flock, held her mother's voice even after Elka was gone. But the world did not hold everything. A word, when no one says it, goes out of the language. There is no place for it after. It does not die loudly. It is simply not there one morning, and no one can say where it went.

The stone was going out that way.

"It is not dying," Greta said, when Alba tried to say this. "Dying is the end of a thing. This is different. This is being forgotten. A thing that is forgotten does not end — it is walked away from. The village walked away from the stone, one generation at a time, until the name was gone from the world and only the stone was left, holding the agreement alone."

The stone kept its promise, Alba thought. It stood and held noon true for eight hundred years, and the village held nothing back. The covenant was not a law. Greta had said that. An agreement is two people holding each other's need. One side holding a promise alone cannot hold it forever. Even a giant.

She sat in the lee of the stone's shoulder, and the thin shadow lay across her like a cold cloth. She had asked it the proper question, and it had answered. It had given her the name, and the name was on the bark in her pocket, and the bark was warm against her hip. She had a hold on it now. It could not fade while she held it. But one girl's memory was a small thing to set against three hundred years of silence, and the stone was going out of the world a finger's width at a time.

"I will hold it," she said to the stone, low. "I have your name. I will teach it."

The stone did not answer. It had answered once, with all it had, and the shadow lay in the grass, thinner than it had been, waiting.

Chapter 22: Elder Greta

Greta's cottage smelled of wool and woodsmoke and the old paper of the chest, and Greta herself smelled of the same things, so that Alba could not tell where the woman ended and her house began.

Greta had been the last child of the last keeper. Her grandmother Marelda had been keeper before her, and Marelda's mother before that, back and back, and the line of the keepers had run thin as an old thread until it came to Greta and stopped. No one had named a successor, because no one had remembered there was a thing to succeed to.

Marelda had left Greta three words and one half-remembered song. The words Greta had kept in her mouth her whole life: vae, which is promise; mund, which is heart-memory; aeld, which is shadow. She had not known what they belonged to, only that they were the last of something, and that they must not be lost, so she had said them to herself in the dark like a child saying a charm, for seventy years.

The song was worse. It had a tune and a shape, but the words had been worn away until only the shape was left, like a field where the grain has been cut and carried and only the stubble remains. Greta could hum it. She could not say what it was for.

The chest she had kept without knowing why. It was hers, so she kept it — dusted it, guarded it, never opened it far. It was the way a dog keeps a door it has never seen opened. There was a weight in her that knew the chest mattered, and a silence in her that had never asked why, because asking had always frightened her a little. To open the chest would have been to admit that the old world was gone and only fragments were left, and she had spent her life holding the fragments without letting herself look at what they were pieces of.

She had been waiting, she told Alba now, at the table with the bark between them — waiting not for the stone to speak. The stone could not speak. It had only its shadow, and its shadow had been silent for centuries, and Greta had not expected it to speak. She had been waiting for someone who would ask properly. For someone who would come to her with the question the fragments were the answer to, so that the words in her mouth and the song in her throat and the chest against the wall would at last be useful.

"I am old," Greta said. "I have carried this so long I forgot it was a burden. It is not a burden, Alba. It is a trust that was waiting for hands that could carry it."

She put her hand over Alba's on the table, and her hand was dry and warm, and there were tears on her face, and she did not wipe them.

"You asked properly," she said. "That is the whole of it. That is the keeper's whole craft — to ask, and to wait for the answer, and to make the words useful again."

Chapter 23: The Records

They opened the chest fully, the two of them, at Greta's table by the west light.

Alba had seen the covenant scroll — the oldest thing, the two hands, the old tongue and the old common tongue, the bargain. But the scroll was only the top of the chest. Under it lay the keepers' books, and when Greta lifted the first one out, Alba saw what the covenant had become in the hands of those who kept it: not a story, but a ledger.

The ring-keepers' tally books. Five centuries of them, each page a year, each line a noon — a mark for every noon the shadow had lain true in the ring. Alba turned the pages, and the marks ran on and on, thousands of small careful strokes, one per day, one per noon, for five hundred years. The ink changed color and the hands changed shape as the generations passed, but the marks did not stop. Some keeper or keeper's child had come out at noon every day, year after year, and looked at the ring, and marked the book. It was the longest attention the village had ever paid to anything.

Then, three hundred years ago, the marks stopped.

Alba turned the page, and the ruled lines were empty. The page after that was empty too, and the page after that, to the end of the book and into the next book, blank to the cover. The gap was like a wall you walk into in the dark. Five centuries of care, and then nothing — not an ending, not a note explaining, just a day when someone did not come out at noon, and then no one ever did again.

"The tallies stopped," Greta said, "and the name went with them. They are the same stopping. When the keepers stopped looking, they stopped remembering, and when they stopped remembering, the words died, and when the words died — " She stopped, and her hand lay on the empty page.

"The chain of the forgetting," Alba said.

"Yes. You can break a chain by finding any link. But we are at the far end, and all the links behind us are gone."

Under the tally books were the fragments of the rite — not a book, but loose papers, some in the old tongue, some in the old common tongue, some half of each: a line about midsummer, a line about noon, a line about the children being first. Half a sentence about the three times. The words were the kind that are written down by people who expect everyone to know what they mean, and so they mean very little now, like a note that says "as always" when no one can remember the always.

There was one page with a drawing — small figures standing in a ring around a long shape, and above them, in the old tongue, a single word that Greta read aloud, sounding it slowly: the name. The name was written here. It had been written all along, in the chest, waiting, and the forgetting had not destroyed it. It had only hidden it.

"The rite is here," Greta said, "in pieces. We must gather the pieces."

Chapter 24: The Search

They went house to house, the girl and the elder, and the village let them in.

It had never occurred to Alba that the village was full of the rite's feet — the parts of it that had outlived the words, the way a path outlives the people who made it. Everywhere they asked, they found the customs standing, motherless.

The old women who kept the midsummer bonfire knew the fire must be laid with the driftwood gathered from the Wold side of the meadow, and that the fire must burn from the noon meal until dark, and that no one must speak across it while the sun stood at the top of the sky. They did not know why. They knew it because their grandmothers had known it, and the why had been a word that had gone out of the language before they were born.

The noon meal in the meadow — the whole village eating together on the longest day, on the grass, facing the stone — that was kept too, faithfully, though no one could have said what the meal was for. It was just what you did, the way you do a thing that has always been done, and the children sat facing the stone without knowing they faced it.

And the children's ring-game. That was the strangest, and it made Alba's heart lift. The children of the village played a game around the stone at midsummer, running in a circle and chanting a rhyme that had no words in it — a string of sounds, nonsense syllables, passed from child to child for so many generations that no one remembered they had ever been anything else. Piper could say it in his sleep. He said it for Alba at her request, standing in the lane, and she listened with her eyes closed, and under the nonsense she could hear the shape — three beats, three times, and a long sound held, like a held breath.

She told Greta, and Greta went still.

"The ring-game," Greta said. "The children's ring. The name, said around the stone, three times. The words wore away to sounds, and the sounds were kept, because children keep what they are given."

They came to the end of the search at the meeting hall, with the fragments spread on the table and the village's customs written down in Alba's careful hand, and they laid the two things side by side — the words from the chest and the feet from the houses — and they fit. The feet of the rite had been kept, all of it: the day, the place, the fire, the meal, the children's ring. Only the words were lost, and now the words were found again.

"The rite is whole," Greta said. "All these years the village has been doing it, feet and hands, and only the mouth was missing. We have the mouth back now."

Chapter 25: The Rite

The rite was a simple thing, once the pieces were laid together. That was the first surprise — that a covenant eight hundred years old should hang on so small a ceremony.

Midsummer. Noon. The village gathered in the noon-ring, the children in front, where the ring-game had always put them. The name spoken together, aloud, three times: Vaelmund. That was all. The stone held noon, and the village held the name, and the name given back to the air once a year was the whole keeping.

Alba wrote the rite down in the common tongue, in her careful hand, in a new book, so that it could not be lost to silence again. And she wrote the new clause, which had never been written before: whoever holds the name must teach it to the next keeper, and the rite must never again be kept by silence. It was the thing the old covenant had lacked — the agreement that the agreement would be spoken of. The old world had trusted that the words would carry themselves, and the words had not carried. The new book would not make that trust again.

They called the village to the meeting hall that evening, and Greta told them what the stone was called.

The name went around the room like a struck bell. The adults turned it on their tongues, and it did not fit them at first — the old tongue is made of sounds the common mouth has unlearned, and Vaelmund came out wrong in ten different ways, stumbling over the roll of the v, catching on the long heart of it. Old Rolf the headman said it three times, and each time differently, and the village laughed at him, gently, and he laughed at himself, and tried again.

The children took to it at once. Piper got it right on the second try, clean as a stone dropped in a well, and the adults fell silent to hear him, because the old tongue sounded like what it was — the stone's own language, coming up out of the grass.

They practiced in the evenings, the whole village, at the end of the day's work. Alba heard the name being tried at the well and at the mill, in the lanes, over fences. Sander the miller's son, who had said all along that a stone is a stone, repeated it to himself as he ground his flour, not to believe in it but because his children were learning it, and he would not be beaten by his own children. The name was in the village now, as the grain was in the fields, being sown.

On the night before midsummer, Alba walked out to the stone in the dark and stood in the noon-ring, where the shadow would lie true again, or not, and she said the name alone, soft, as practice for the morning. The stone did not answer. But the warmth, she thought — the warmth at her palm was a little more than it had been. She could not be sure. She went home with the name in her mouth like a seed, and slept.

Chapter 26: The Midsummer

The longest day came up gold over the Wold, and the village was in the meadow before the dew had dried.

Alba had never seen the Long Meadow so full. Everyone was there — the herders and the millers, the old women at their bonfire, the children already at the ring-game, running their circles with the name half-formed on their tongues. The noon meal was spread on the grass at the meadow's edge, cloths and loaves and the summer's first apples, and the bonfire was laid with the driftwood from the Wold side, exactly as the custom said, though now they knew what the custom was for.

And the stone stood in the middle of it all, and its shadow lay a hand's width west of the ring, and would not move.

They had known it would be there. Alba had told them. But knowing and seeing are different things, and the village looked at the gap between the shadow and the groove — the bare rubbed earth where the shadow had lain for eight hundred years, now showing grass — and the fear that had been put down in the meeting hall stood up again, quiet, in the eyes of the adults.

Old Rolf the headman made his speech at the foot of the stone, and it was a halting speech, not like him. He had never had to speak a sentence he did not believe, and now he said the words of the covenant in the order Alba and Greta had taught him, and he stumbled on them the way he stumbled on the name, and stopped, and started again, and the village stood with him in the middle of his stumbling, holding the sentence up.

"We hold his name," he said. And he said the name, and the village heard it in his voice, and a few of them said it back, low, before he had finished, and that helped him. He finished without a stumble.

Then there was nothing to do but wait for noon.

The morning was the longest morning Alba had ever lived. The shadow shortened toward the ring, and the village watched it the way you watch a slow river, willing it, and the shadow came on by the ordinary rule of the sun, and lay down a hand's width short of the groove, and stopped.

The meadow held still. The bees went on in the clover. The children, by custom, had stopped their game and stood in the ring, facing the stone, small and quiet, and the bonfire waited, unlit, and the noon meal waited, uneaten, and noon came over the top of the sky, and the shadow did not move.

Alba stood at the front, where the children were, because the rite put the children first, and she was of an age with them in the eyes of the stone. She had the name in her mouth, ready. She could feel the whole village behind her, one breath.

She looked at the shadow, lying out of the ring, thin, thinner than it had been — and she thought of the stone going out of the world like a word, and her throat closed, and she opened her mouth, and she said the name.

Chapter 27: The Speaking

"Vaelmund," Alba said, and her voice was small in the great meadow, but it was the first time the name had been said at noon in the ring in three hundred years, and the meadow heard it.

The shadow trembled.

It was not the trembling of the air on a hot day. It was a trembling from within, like a held breath that has been held too long and is about to let go. The whole village saw it, and a sound went through them — the sound people make when something they love stirs after they had thought it dead.

Then the village spoke the name with her.

"Vaelmund," they said, all together, and the name went over the meadow like a wave over a field of grain, the old tongue alive in a hundred mouths at once, and the shadow lifted.

Lifted. Off the grass. Alba saw it leave the ground, the whole long shape of it rising, as if the stone had drawn itself up — and for a moment the shadow stood tall and dark beside the stone, taller than any shadow she had ever seen, tall as the sky-edge giants must have stood — and then it sank down again, still out of the ring, still thin, and the meadow was silent.

The rite was the name spoken three times. They had said it twice. Alba felt the village behind her gathering itself for the third time, and she saw the children's mouths already open, and she said the name the third time with her whole heart.

"Vaelmund," said the children, high and clear, their voices rising over the adults' like larks, and the whole meadow said it with them.

And the shadow drew back toward the ring.

It moved a finger's width at a time, the way it had drifted, but backward now, as if it were walking back through the weeks, through all the days of its going. It came in off the grass where it had lain thin, and crossed the green edge where the ring had begun to heal, and no one breathed. The meadow held its breath. The flock held its breath. The sun stood at the top of the sky and did not move, and the shadow came home, finger's width by finger's width, and settled into the worn groove of the noon-ring, and lay there, exact, true, home.

For a long moment no one moved. The shadow lay in the ring as it had lain for eight hundred years. The stone was warm — Alba felt it against her shoulder, warm as a sleeper's back, warm as it had been before the drift — and the meadow was so still you could hear the bees again, returning, as if the world had started again.

Then Old Rolf, the headman, said the name once more, softly, to himself, the way you say a thing you have only just learned how to keep, and the village heard him, and a hundred voices said it with him, soft as breathing.

And the shadow lay in the ring, and did not move, and the stone slept on, remembered.

Chapter 28: The Name, Aloud

What had happened, Alba understood only slowly, in the ringing quiet after — was that the name had passed from mouth to mouth.

The rite was not the standing in the ring. The rite was the speaking: the name leaving one mouth and entering another, passing through the air of the meadow, held for a moment by every voice in turn. A name does not live in one place. It lives in the going. And for eight hundred years the name had had no one to pass through, and it had starved, the way a fire starves without air, the way a seed starves without ground — and now it was passing again, mouth to mouth, and it was fed.

The village had not understood this as they spoke it. They had understood that they were saying the stone's name, and that the shadow had trembled and lifted and come home, and that was enough for them. But Alba stood at the front of the ring and heard the name move through the village like a current through water — out of her mouth, into Old Rolf's, into the old women's, into the children's, out of the ring and back again — and she understood that the whole act of remembering is a passing of a thing from hand to hand, so that no one holds it alone.

Greta was crying.

Alba had seen Greta weep before, at the name on the bark, in her cottage. But this was different. Greta stood in the ring with the rest, and the tears ran down her face, and she did not wipe them — her hands were open, holding the name — and she sang the name when she said it, on the note of her grandmother's half-remembered song, the tune that had kept its shape through all the years without its words. The words and the tune met again in her throat and were whole, and she wept with her mouth full of them.

Eight hundred years of silence breaking. Alba heard it as a sound — a great sheet of quiet tearing, the first rain after a long drought, the moment in a room full of people when everyone who has been pretending not to wait stops pretending. The silence the village had kept without knowing it, the silence of the name unsaid, had broken at noon in the meadow.

And the stone had answered. The shadow trembling was the stone's answer, the first it had given in three hundred years — the shudder of a sleeper who has been called by name and stirs toward waking, and then settles back, content, because the call is the thing that matters, and the keeping of it.

The name passed mouth to mouth until it had gone around the whole village, and the last child said it, and the shadow lay true in the ring, and the meadow was full of the sound of the name, and it was not loud. It was the sound of a promise being held, and it was the loveliest thing Alba had ever heard.

Chapter 29: The Shadow, True

Afterward, no one could say exactly how long the return had taken. Some said the shadow had walked home in the space of a single held breath. Others said it had taken the whole slow length of the afternoon, and they had watched it all. Alba thought both were true — that the shadow had come home at once and forever, and that they had watched it come home a long, long time, because watching it was a thing the village needed to do, needed to finish.

She watched it herself, standing in the ring where she had stood for the speaking, and she saw it as she had seen the drift — as a walking back through the weeks.

The shadow had gone out of the ring a finger's width at a time, one noon after another, like a slow river leaving its bed. Now it came back the same way, backwards, and Alba could almost see the weeks passing under it — the thin grass of the drift's early days, the bare earth of the middle, the worn groove at the end — and the shadow stepped back through them all, correcting each day's going, until its edge met the ring's edge and lay down along it, and the groove that had been empty for a season was full again.

It was exact. Alba had known the ring her whole life, every worn inch of it, and the shadow lay in it as the water lies in a cup, not a hair's breadth out. As the afternoon wore on and the shadow's shape followed the sun's turning, the edge of the dark followed the groove around, hour by hour, faithful as it had been for eight hundred years.

The village stood and watched the shadow touch the ring, and knew it was home. That was the word that came to them, to all of them, at once — home. The stone was not a monument, not a mystery, not a fear. It was a sleeper, and the sleeper's shadow lay in its own worn bed again, and the village stood around the bed and watched it sleep, and the watching was a kind of keeping, and the keeping was a kind of love, though no one said the word.

Alba knelt at the ring's edge and put her hand into the groove, where the shadow lay. The earth was packed and smooth and warm — the oldest record in the world, she had thought once, and now she knew it better: the record of a promise kept, in the only ink a giant has. She left her hand there a moment, holding it, and the shadow held her back, the way a thing that has come home holds the people who waited for it.

When she stood, the village was already moving, quietly, toward the meadow's edge, where the noon meal waited, and the bonfire waited, and the afternoon of the longest day lay before them, gold and true. The shadow lay in the ring, and the ring was full, and the stone slept on, remembered, and home.

Chapter 30: The Return

The afternoon feast was the harvest feast and the midwinter feast and every feast the village had ever kept, all poured into one afternoon.

They ate the noon meal at last, on the grass, facing the stone, and the food tasted like food tastes when you have been certain — though no one said it — that you might not eat together like this again. The bread came around and the summer apples, and the miller's wife laughed at nothing and could not stop, and Old Rolf told the story of the shadow coming home with his hands shaking, and the village laughed with him, giddy, the way you laugh when a weight you did not know you were carrying is lifted, and you find you are light.

The children ran the ring. They had been doing it all day, but now they ran it with the new words in it — the nonsense syllables gone, the name in their place, three times around and the long held sound — and their voices rang off the stone, and the stone did not mind. The shadow lay in the ring, and the children ran around it, and the oldest record in the world was a playing field again, and that, Alba thought, was the best thing of all — that the covenant was a thing children could play in and mean it.

The headman stumbled on the name. He had said it in the rite, and now, with the bonfire lit and the afternoon going golden, he said it again to the old women, and it came out wrong — the v flat, the long middle lost — and the village laughed, and he laughed, and he tried it again and got it right, and the village clapped him, and the clapping was not mockery. It was the sound of a village that had learned a new word and was proud of its own mouth for holding it.

Alba slipped away at dusk.

The feast went on behind her — the bonfire burning, the song starting up, the children's voices — and she walked out alone across the meadow to the stone, and stood in the lee of its shoulder, where she had stood a hundred noons, and put her hand on the warm stone, and said the name softly, alone, the way you speak to a friend when the others have gone.

"Vaelmund," she said. "You are remembered."

The shadow lay true in the ring. The stone was warm. It did not answer — it was asleep, deep in its long dream of the sky-edge it used to walk, and it was a good sleep now, a kept sleep, a sleep with a name over it.

She stayed until the first stars came, and the bonfire's glow stood at the meadow's edge, and behind her she could hear the village singing — and she heard, in the song, the name, woven in, because the village had taken it into its mouth for good. She walked home across the meadow, and the shadow lay in the ring behind her, true, and she carried the name home in her mouth like a seed, ready for the planting.

Chapter 31: The Morning

The next morning, Alba woke before the light. The meadow lay gray and still under the last of the stars, and the stone stood at its heart like a dark hill, and her first thought was of the shadow, and her second was that today was an ordinary day, and ordinary was the miracle.

She went to the fold. Tobin was already there, counting the sheep by feel in the half dark, and he did not say good morning. He said, "The ring," and nodded toward the meadow, and she understood that he had been out before light to look at it.

The whole village came that morning, one and two at a time, not called but moved. Old Rolf stood at the meadow's edge with his hands behind his back. Sander the miller came from his stones, and the mill was stopped, and no one minded. They looked at the ring of worn earth where the shadow had lain for eight hundred years, and at the shadow itself lying in it, true, and they did not touch it, because you do not touch a thing that has come home.

Alba went about her work. She milked, she drove the ewes out, she walked the meadow's edge. The dog worked beside her, easy. And all morning she watched the shadow creep across the grass the way she had watched it all her life, only now she watched it the way you watch a friend's face after a long trouble.

At noon the whole village stood in the meadow without being called. Nobody said the name. They did not need to. They watched the shadow touch the ring's worn edge and lie in it, exact, and the watching itself was the answer.

Piper was the first to break the quiet. He ran his small finger along the groove of the ring, where the earth was packed and dark, and he looked up at the stone, and he said, softly, "Vaelmund," the way you say a word you are still learning to love.

And that was all. The ordinary day resumed. The mill turned. The well was drawn. The children were called in. Alba walked the flock home in the long evening and saw, from the meadow's edge, the shadow stretched out like a sleeper, at rest, and she carried the name in her mouth the whole way, like a seed.

Chapter 32: The Noon, Again

The first noon after the speaking, the village stood in the meadow unbidden. No one had called them. The work simply stopped at the height of the day, and they came, from the mill and the well and the fold, and stood in the grass around the ring.

Alba stood at the stone's shoulder. The sun stood at its standing-still. The shadow lay in the ring, true, and where it had drifted a hand's width out of the groove there was a faint line in the grass, a scar of the moving weeks, and the shadow covered it now, and the covering was the healing.

They did not speak the name aloud that day. That had been done. What they did was stand, and watch, and be watched back by the stone's long silence, and the silence was not the fear it had been. It was a quiet answer.

Then Piper began the ring-game. The old women had kept it for years, the children circling the stone at noon, and for years it had been a game with no words in it, the words lost. Now Piper said the name in the middle of the circling, and the other children said it after him, stumbling, laughing, and the ring-game had its words again.

Sander stood apart, his arms crossed. Alba watched him. He did not look at the stone; he looked at his own two children, running the ring, their mouths shaping the strange syllables, and something in his face loosened, the way a held breath lets go.

The shadow did not move. It lay true in the groove. The children ran their circuit and their shadows ran with them, small, and the stone's shadow stayed where it belonged, and the meadow was full of the ordinary sound of noon: bees, the flock, wind, the mill going round beyond the trees.

The headman cleared his throat and said, "It is true, then," and the village heard the wonder in his voice, the way you hear relief in the voice of a man who has looked at a thing and found it whole.

Alba walked home with her father. The dog went between them. Tobin did not say much; he was a man who kept his words for the animals. But at the fold gate he put his hand on her head, heavy and warm, and said, "You asked properly, girl," and that was all, and it was everything.

Chapter 33: The Village

The fear went out of the village the way frost goes out of a field, not all at once, but by the hour, until one morning they woke and found it gone, and could not say when it had left.

The mill turned under a true noon. The well was drawn. The sheep came in from the meadow and the count was right, and no one counted with a tight throat. The stone stood where it had always stood, and its shadow lay where it had always lain, and the village began, slowly, to live at its foot again instead of watching it.

Sander the skeptic was the changed one, and everyone saw it. He had said, at the meeting hall in the bad days, that a stone was a stone and a shadow was a shadow and a covenant was a word for cowards. He said little now. But Alba heard him at the mill, grinding the afternoon grain, and the name came out of him half-sung, the way men sing while they work, Vaelmund, soft, as if to himself. He stopped when he saw her, red to the ears, and went back to his stones, and she said nothing, because a seed does not need to be watched to grow.

The stronger sign was his children. They had learned the name in the ring-game, and they carried it home, and Alba heard them at the well one morning, saying it back and forth the way children say a new word, arguing over the sound of it, and Sander stood in the mill door listening, and did not tell them to be quiet.

Old Rolf, the headman, walked the meadow every evening now, not to watch the stone but as a man walks a path he has walked all his life. Greta's cottage was never empty. The old women came to her with the fragments they had hoarded, a rhyme here, a half-remembered line there, and she wrote them down in her careful hand, and the fragments began, one by one, to fit.

The smaller, steadier work of remembering had begun. It was not the great rite. It was the well, and the mill, and the fold, and the name said low among the ordinary sounds of the day. The village was afraid no longer. It was careful now, and care, Alba thought, is what fear turns into when it is loved instead of fed.

Chapter 34: The Thanks

The evening after, they built the bonfire at the meadow's edge, the old women's work, the same fire they had built for years without knowing why. And this year, for the first time in living memory, they built it and knew.

The whole village came at dusk. They carried things. Alba watched them come up the path from the village with their hands full, and she understood before anyone said it: bread, apples, wool, a little oil, a crock of honey, the mill's first flour of the new morning, their own goods, their hands' work, laid out at the foot of the stone like a harvest.

The headman stood before the gifts and did not know what to say. He looked at the stone, and at the shadow lying true in the ring, and at the village, and he said, "We brought—" and stopped, and the village understood, because thanks has no grammar.

Alba stepped forward. She was sixteen, small, sun-browned, and they listened to her now, all of them, and it still surprised her. "The stone needs no gifts," she said. "It needs the name. The covenant is the name. The bread is not the covenant."

The village was quiet. The fire crackled. The children stood at the ring's edge, and Piper said, "But the stone is hungry."

And the village laughed, and the laughter broke the solemnity the way rain breaks the heat of a day, and Alba laughed too, and said, "It is not hungry, Piper. It is asleep. But you may leave the bread. Thanks has hands, and it likes to give."

So they gave. They laid the bread and the apples and the wool at the stone's feet, a soft mound of the village's own goods, their half of the covenant made visible, and the stone did not move, and the shadow lay true, and the village knew the gift was received in the only way the stone could receive anything: by the keeping of the other half.

At the end, before the fire burned low, the headman said the name. He stumbled on it, half the village had stumbled on it in the practice evenings, and they laughed with him, kindly, the way you laugh at a friend who has learned to say a hard word. And the shadow lay still in the ring, and the meadow was warm, and the name had been said over bread and fire and wool, which is the proper place for a name to be said: among the ordinary good.

Chapter 35: The Giant, Asleep

Alba did not go straight home when the fire died. She went to the stone, alone, in the dark, and sat at its feet with her back against its shoulder, where the noon shadow tipped, and she listened.

The warmth was back. That was the first thing she felt, the slow, stored warmth of the day held in the stone the way a body holds it, and she leaned into it, and it was like leaning against a great sleeping flank. The shadow was gone into the dark; the ring was invisible; but she did not need to see it. She knew it was true. You know where a thing lies in the dark the way you know where a sleeping sheep lies: by the weight of the quiet.

She put her hand flat on the stone, and under her palm, very slow, she felt it: the rise and the fall. Not much. A finger's width of movement, like the breathing of a deep sleeper, moving through the stone the way a breath moves through a body, and it made her eyes sting, and she did not know why.

She said the name, low. Vaelmund. And the slow rise and fall went on, unbroken, and she understood that the answer to a spoken name, for a sleeper, is to go on sleeping.

She stayed a long time. The stars wheeled. The flock was at the fold, safe, and she was where she was supposed to be, and she thought about the giant's dream. The old story was in her now, Greta's words: the sky-edge the giants walked, the turning of the seasons held in their bodies, the fall, the last giant coming down wounded to the meadow. She felt, against her back, that he dreamed it still, the walking, the wide sky, the first village at the foot of the hill, hungry and season-mad, and the bargain struck in the long grass.

It was a peaceful dream now. That was the change. For eight hundred years he had dreamed it alone, holding a promise no one held the other end of. Now the dream had the name in it, and a name, held in another's mouth, is company.

The wind moved. The meadow sighed. Alba rose at last, stiff, and put her hand once more to the stone, and said goodnight in the old tongue, the words Greta had taught her, vae, aeld, and walked the dark path home, and behind her the giant slept on, remembered, breathing slow under the stars.

Chapter 36: The Remembering, Kept

The week after, the name went out into the village like water into dry ground, and the village softened around it.

Alba heard it at the well in the mornings, passed between women drawing water, spoken low and easy, the way you pass a greeting. She heard it at the mill, mixed with the grinding and the grain dust, Sander singing it under his breath while he worked. She heard it in the fields, in the children's mouths, where it lived most naturally of all. They had no shyness of it, no stumbling; they had learned it as they learned their letters, and it sat in their speech the way a stone sits in a field, just there.

Piper could say it perfectly now, and said it wrong on purpose, to hear the laughter. "Vaaelmund," he would say, drawing it out, and the other children would correct him, and he would grin, and Alba thought that was good too, because a name that can be played with is a name that is alive. A name you fear to touch is a name already dying.

Greta's cottage was the heart of it. The old women came with their fragments and Greta wrote them down, and the fragments were the feet of the rite, the customs that had survived without their words, the bonfire, the noon meal in the meadow, the ring-game, and now, slowly, the words came back to the feet. Alba sat with her in the evenings, and Greta taught her the old letters, one by one, sounding them, the old tongue coming alive in the old woman's mouth and passing, hand to hand, into the girl's.

And one evening, alone in Greta's cottage, with the records chest open on the table and the tallow burning low, Alba took up a fresh quill and a clean page of the new book, and she wrote, in the common tongue, in letters any child could read:

Vaelmund, "the one who remembers the agreement." The stone of the Long Meadow, the last giant of the old world. The name must be spoken at midsummer, and at the hinges of the year. If the name is lost, the giant fades.

She wrote it out in full, the covenant, the two sides, the rite, the promise of the Keeper to teach the next Keeper, and when she was done she read it back, and her voice shook a little, because she was writing down the thing that could not be lost again. The remembering, kept, and made safe.

She closed the book. The shadow was true in the ring beyond the window. She carried the book to the chest and laid it in, on top of the old scroll, and she thought: the old covenant was written where only the keepers could read it. This one is written where everyone can. That is the difference between a word kept by one and a word kept by all.

Chapter 37: The Autumn

The harvest came gold. The fields behind the village rippled and were cut, and the stooks stood in the rows, and the shadow of the Standing Stone lay true in the ring through the shortening days, and the village measured the year by it again the way they always had, and the measuring was a comfort.

Alba worked the harvest with her father and her brothers. The days were long and the sheaves were heavy, and in the evenings her hands smelled of grain and dust, and the stone, from the edge of the field, caught the last light and held it like a warm body. She watched its shadow grow long and thin toward the village as the sun sank, and she thought: it has done this for eight hundred years. It will do this now, because we keep the other half.

The harvest feast was laid on the long tables in the meadow, in sight of the stone, and the village came in its best clothes, tired and easy. And before the meal, before the bread was broken, the headman raised his hand, and the village grew quiet, and he spoke the name.

They said it together, three times, as the rite set down: Vaelmund. Not all of them said it well. Some stumbled; the old tongue sat awkward in mouths that had never held it until a month before. But they said it, all of them, the children's voices high and true, and the shadow lay steady in the ring, and it was the first hinge of the year kept.

Then they ate. The roast, the bread, the apples, the honey. The children ran between the tables and around the stone, and one of them, Alba saw it was Piper, stopped at the ring and swept it clean of leaves with his hands, carefully, not for any reason he could say, and Alba watched him and thought: care, not ritual. That is the difference. The rite is the words. The care is the life.

Old Rolf stood by the stone with his cup, looking at it the way you look at an old friend across a room, and he said to it, low, not caring who heard: "Eight hundred years of noons. And you held them all."

The sun went down. The feast wound to its end. And Alba, walking the flock home in the dark, said the name once, softly, into the autumn air, and carried it home like a warm thing, and thought of all the harvests to come, each one under a true shadow, each one begun with the name.

Chapter 38: The Rite, Yearly

The village decided it in the meeting hall, on a wet evening in the late autumn, and it was the easiest decision they had made in a year.

It was Old Rolf who proposed it. "The midsummer was the covenant's word," he said. "But the stone holds noon for us every day. It seems to me we should not wait for midsummer to speak to it. We should speak at the hinges, harvest, winter's heart, spring's turning, and every midsummer. Four times a year, the name. It is not much. It is a loaf a year to a friend."

And the village, remembering the months of fear, remembering the hand's width of shadow, remembering what it had cost them to be silent, agreed as one body, the way a field agrees to the rain.

So the rite became a rhythm, and the rhythm became the shape of the year.

Alba taught the rite's words to the youngest children in the meeting hall on winter evenings, and it was not a lesson she imposed on them. It was a game, like the ring-game, and they learned the name the way they learned the names of the stars, by saying it and saying it until it was theirs. Piper could teach it now, and did, standing on a stool, solemn as a headman, and the children laughed and said it after him.

The rite, yearly, was small and ordinary. That was the point. It did not need the great fear of the first time to make it true; it was true the way a planted field is true, by the keeping of it. The harvest feast began with the name. The winter's heart would be kept at the shortest day. The spring's turning would be kept at dawn on the first full day of spring. And midsummer would be what it had always been, the great hinge, the threefold speaking in the noon-ring, but it would no longer be the only speaking.

Alba wrote the four hinges into the new book, under the covenant, in the common tongue, so that no child would ever have to wonder what was asked of them. And she thought, closing the book, that a promise is not a stone you set once and forget. It is a path you walk, and walking it, you wear it true, the way the shadow wore the ring.

The days shortened. The wind turned cold. And the village went into the winter knowing the year's shape, and its part in it, and the name held in every mouth like a seed sown in good ground.

Chapter 39: The Winter

The first snow came in the night and lay thick on the meadow, and in the morning the village came out to look, and the stone's shadow lay blue on the white ground, true in the ring, and the ring itself was a faint line of dark where the packed earth held no snow.

Alba stood at the fold gate and looked at it for a long time. She had seen the shadow on snow before, every winter of her life, but she had never seen it the way she saw it now: as a signature. A name written in blue on white, saying, here, I am, I remember.

The shortest day came. The winter's heart.

They went out in the afternoon, when the sun stood lowest and the shadow was longest and the ring was a dark groove in the snow at the stone's feet. The whole village came, muffled, breath white, the children's faces pink over their scarves. The headman spoke the name, and the village said it after him, three times, and their breath hung in the cold air like a small cloud over the ring, and the shadow lay still, and the stillness was the answer, the way a sleeper's quiet is an answer, and the village turned and went home to their fires, warm and light.

Greta did not go home. Alba saw her leaning on the stone's flank, and came to her, and took her arm, and Greta leaned on her all the way to the cottage, and her cough was bad, worse than it had been, a deep cough that shook her thin frame.

The fire was low. Alba built it up, and put the kettle on, and sat with her at the hearth while the dusk came down and the snow began again, small and steady.

"Say it once more," Greta said. "The old tongue. The three words."

And Alba said them, one by one: vae, promise. mund, heart-memory. aeld, shadow. And she told her what they made together, the name that was a duty, the one who remembers the agreement, and Greta nodded, her eyes closed, a little smile on her mouth, and said, "Good. You say them true. I will not be here forever, child, but the words will, if you hold them."

Alba sat by the fire a long time, watching the old woman sleep, the shadow of the stone blue on the snow outside the window, true, and she thought of the giant's patience and the old woman's, and how alike they were, and how a thing held by one pair of hands must be passed, hand to hand, or it is lost.

Chapter 40: The Stone, Watched

The winter passed, and the stone stood, and the village watched it the way you watch something loved, not because it might go, but because watching is a kind of keeping.

The children made it a game. At noon, when the schoolroom let out, they went to the meadow to check the ring, running their fingers along the groove, seeing the shadow lie in it, and it was not fear that sent them; it was the pleasure of finding a thing where it ought to be. Piper made a tally of his own on a stick, a notch for every true noon, and the other children kept him honest, and the notches grew.

Old Rolf walked his evening round. Sander the miller sang under his breath. The old women sat in Greta's cottage and the fragments were nearly all fitted now, the words back in the feet of the customs, and the customs alive again.

And Alba, in the deep of the winter, opened a new book, a tally book, the first of its kind in three hundred years, and on the first clean page she marked the first noon. A small mark, in ink, beside the day's name, meaning: the shadow lay true.

She had found the old tally books in the chest, the ring-keepers' books, five centuries of noons marked one after another, and then the gap, the three hundred empty years where the marks stopped and the name went with them. She had run her finger along the last of the old marks, made by a hand three hundred years dead, and she had understood that she was joining that hand's work, continuing a line that had been broken and was now mended.

She marked the noon every day, at the hour the shadow lay true. The marks were small and even and they grew, and the book filled, and the village came to understand that the tally was the visible remembering, the noon-ring in ink, the groove made where all could read it.

On the last day of winter, she closed the first page of marks and opened the next, and she thought of the three hundred empty years, and of the long line of keepers before her, and of the one after her, whoever that child was, and she wrote her own name at the head of the book, Alba, Keeper, in the common tongue, so that the child would know whose hand had begun it again.

The shadow lay true. The stone stood warm in its ring. And the watching continued, easy now, a thing of affection, a noon at a time.

Chapter 41: The Spring

The thaw came slowly, the way it always came, the snow drawing back from the meadow a hand's width a day, and the grass greening underneath, and the stone standing black against the wet white ground with its shadow true in the ring, dark on the damp earth.

Alba watched the ring as the meadow woke. The bare packed earth of the groove, worn by eight hundred years of true shadow, had been scoured by the drifting weeks, and where the moving shadow had rubbed in its wrong path the grass was beaten down and yellow. But now, with the shadow true again, the edges were healing. The grass was creeping in at the ring's rim, small green threads taking hold where the moving shadow no longer rubbed, and she knelt and touched them, soft, and thought: it is mending. The wound is closing the way a wound closes, by the body's own patience.

The lambs came. The ewes dropped them in the fold in the mild nights, and the meadow filled with their small bleating, and the village's work turned, as it turned every year, from the hearth to the field. Alba helped with the lambing, her hands in the warm wool, the smell of milk and hay, and she thought that the year was turning, and the shadow was turning with it, true.

The first full day of spring came clear and cold, the sun rising over the Wold in a wash of gold. The spring's turning rite was kept at dawn, as the rite set down: the village gathered in the meadow while the light climbed, the children in front, and they spoke the name together, three times, their breath white on the cold air, and the shadow, long and blue, lay across the ring, and the stillness was the answer.

Then the day began. The plough went out. The sheep went up. Alba walked the flock to the green edge of the meadow and stood a moment at the stone's shoulder, the cold morning warmth of it against her, and she said the name low, to the stone alone, the way you say a word to a friend when no one is listening.

The grass crept in at the ring's edge. The shadow lay true. The year had turned.

Chapter 42: The Herder's Path

Alba's days had a shape now, and the shape was the same shape the year had: the fold, the meadow, the stone, and at the height of the day, the true shadow. The stone was her clock again, the way it had been all her life, and she read it the way she read the sky, without thinking, the way a herder reads weather.

She rose before light. She counted the sheep in the half dark by feel, the dog at her heel. She drove them out to the meadow as the sun came up over the Wold, and the flock spread into the long grass like water, and she walked the meadow's edge, and the stone stood at its heart, and its shadow lay long and cool across the dew.

The work was small and it was all of it. Mending the fold's fence where a ewe had leaned on it. Checking the lambs. The count at dusk, the dog's slow gathering, the walk home with the flock in the long light, the shadow stretched out behind the stone like a sleeper.

The small daily speech had begun, and she kept it now without thinking. In the morning, passing the stone, she greeted it in the old tongue, the words Greta had taught her: vae, she would say, promise, and aeld, shadow, and the stone would hold the day's warmth and give it back. At noon she spoke the name, low, not for anyone to hear, because the name was not for showing; it was for keeping. At dusk, on the walk home, she said goodnight, and the old tongue sat in her mouth the way it sat in Greta's, worn smooth, a pebble held for years.

Tobin watched her, and said nothing, and she felt his watching the way the flock felt her presence, a quiet steady thing. Rafe, her eldest brother, who had laughed at her finger's width, came to her one evening at the fold gate and said, not looking at her, "You mark the tally every noon," and she said, "I do," and he said, "It is good work," and that was the whole of it, and she carried it home like a warm thing.

The herder's path was not a great road. It was the worn line between the fold and the meadow and the stone, walked four times a day, eight hundred days in two years, and the walking of it, she thought, was the shape of a kept life: not the great deed, but the daily keeping, the name said low, the shadow read true, the flock counted and safe.

The dog came to her hand. The flock spread on the green. The shadow lay in the ring. The day was ordinary, and ordinary was the keeping.

Chapter 43: The Name, Learned

The teaching began where all the teaching in the village began: with the children, in the meadow, at noon.

Alba did not set it as a lesson. She made it a game, the way the old women made the ring-game, and the children came to it the way they came to the ring, by the pleasure of it. Piper was the first to learn it properly. He had said it wrong for weeks, drawing it out, Vaaelmund, making the laughter, and then one noon, in the middle of the ring-game, he said it all at once, clean and whole — Vaelmund — and the other children stopped, and said it after him, and he said it again, and it was right, and he looked up at the stone with his whole face shining, and Alba thought: that is the first seed, and it has taken.

After that they learned it as they learned their letters, a word among words, and soon the whole village could speak it. Not all of them well. Old Rolf still stumbled on the middle of it, and the village laughed with him, kindly. Sander the miller said it perfectly, having practiced at his stones, and was ashamed to be caught at it, and then was not ashamed. Even the old women, who had kept the customs without their words for so many years, said it now with the words restored, and their voices shook a little, saying what their grandmothers had said.

But the deep teaching was the other way. In the evenings, in Greta's cottage, the old woman taught Alba to read the old letters, and the tongue passed from hand to hand, from the dying mouth into the living one, the way water passes from one cup to another.

Greta taught her the three words first, sounding them, making her say them until they sat true: vae, promise. mund, heart-memory. aeld, shadow. Then the letters, one by one, the tall script of the old tongue that the shadow had written in the grass. Alba learned to shape them with her own hand, copying them into her palm with a charred twig, onto birch bark, until the shapes were as familiar as the shapes of the sheep in her flock.

And at last, one evening, with the tallow burning low, Greta sang her the half-remembered song, the old woman's voice thin and true, a song about the sky-edge and the walking, and the fall, and the bargain, and Alba listened with her eyes closed, and the song went into her the way a song goes into a child, by the sound of it, and she carried it home in her mouth.

"I have kept them for eighty years," Greta said, when the song was done. "Now you keep them. And you will keep them longer than I could, because you are young, and because you are not afraid of them."

Alba said the three words to the dark. The shadow lay true beyond the window. The tongue had passed, hand to hand, and it was alive.

Chapter 44: Alba's Role

The village saw her differently now, and she saw it in a hundred small ways.

The headman came to her before he came to anyone. When the harvest was to be laid, he asked her which day the shadow would lie truest for the speaking. When the well needed mending, he asked her whether the stone minded the noise, and she said it minded nothing, it was asleep, and he looked relieved, and she thought: they are learning to ask the stone's questions properly, through her.

The chest passed to her keeping. Greta brought it down from the shelf one afternoon and set it on the table, the old scroll, the tally books, the fragments, the new book, and she said, "It is yours now. I am too old to carry it up and down." Alba took it home, and set it on her own shelf, above the hearth, where the light could not harm it, and the weight of it was not heavy. It was a weight she had been carrying for a year without knowing it.

She was consulted, and listened to, and she did not love it. That was the strange thing. The girl who had been listened to least was listened to most, and it sat on her the way a too-big coat sits on a child, and she wore it anyway, because the keeping was the work, and the listening was part of the keeping.

The children came to her to check their tallies. The old women came to her with a fragment they had remembered, a rhyme, a saying, and she wrote them down in the new book, and they went away easier. Her father, Tobin, asked her nothing, but he watched her the way he watched the sky, and she knew he was proud, and the pride was quiet and it warmed her.

And she understood, slowly, what her role was. It was not power. The headman had power, and the mill had power, and the well had power over the village's thirst. Her role was something else. She was the one who held the name. And to hold a name is not to rule; it is to be responsible for it, the way you are responsible for a fire on a winter night, or a lamb in a cold fold. It is care, held in one pair of hands, so that it can be passed to the next.

She marked the noon that day, the small even mark in the tally book, and she thought of the long line of hands the mark had passed through, and the line it would pass to, and she was content in the middle of it, small, sun-browned, the girl who asked properly, holding the name.

Chapter 45: The Keeper

It happened in the early summer, on a soft evening, when the meadow was gold and the shadow lay long and true across the ring.

Greta called Alba to her cottage, and the fire was laid though the evening was warm, and the records chest was open on the table, and the old woman was dressed in her best shawl, and Alba knew before she spoke that this was the passing.

"Sit," Greta said. Alba sat. The old woman looked at her a long time, and the look was the look a grandmother gives, full of years and easy, and Alba looked back, and neither of them spoke, because the important words are said with the eyes first.

"Say them," Greta said at last. "The three words."

And Alba said them, one by one: vae, promise. mund, heart-memory. aeld, shadow. And then the name they made, Vaelmund, the one who remembers the agreement, and Greta nodded, and her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them.

"Now the song," Greta said. And Alba sang the song, the half-remembered song of the sky-edge and the walking and the fall, and her voice was young and it was true, and Greta closed her eyes and listened, and when it was done she said, "You have it. All of it. The words, the song, the reading. I kept them for eighty years, and I was afraid they would die with me, and you have taken them from my mouth while I was alive. That is the whole of what a keeper does, child: passes the cup before it empties."

She reached into the chest and drew out the old scroll, the covenant itself, eight hundred years old, the two hands, one in the old tongue, one in the old common tongue, half-faded. She unrolled it on the table, and Alba had read it before, but never like this: as the thing being passed.

"It is yours," Greta said. "The reading of it, and the keeping of it, and the teaching of it to the one after you. That is the keeper's charge. It is not a title, girl. It is a responsibility held in one pair of hands, to be passed, hand to hand, as long as the stone stands."

They were grandmother and granddaughter though not by blood, the old woman and the girl, and the girl said, "I will keep it," and the old woman nodded, and leaned back in her chair, and the fire settled, and a great ease came over her, the ease of a thing set down after a long carrying.

"I am tired," Greta said. "I have been tired for a long time. I think I will rest now."

And Alba sat with her through the evening, and the shadow drew long and lay true in the ring, and the keeper rested, and the new keeper began her keeping.

Chapter 46: The Second Year

The second midsummer came, and it was nothing like the first, and that was the wonder of it.

A year before, the village had come to the meadow in fear, hearts tight, not knowing if the speaking would work, the shadow lying a hand's width from the ring. Now they came easy, in their best clothes, carrying the noon meal, the children running ahead, and the fear was gone the way a year of weather goes, leaving only the memory of it, and the gratitude.

The bonfire was laid. The noon meal was spread at the meadow's edge, bread and apples and the first honey. The ring was swept clean, the children's work, done for love. And the village gathered, and no one called them; they came, because it was midsummer, and it was the great hinge, and they knew their part in it.

The headman spoke, and he spoke without stumbling now. He had practiced, as they all had, in the evenings, the old tongue awkward in his mouth for months, and now it sat as well as it ever would. "Vaelmund," he said, and the village said it after him, and the second time, and the third, the children's voices high and clear, and the shadow lay in the ring, true, and it did not tremble, and it did not lift.

That was the change. The first year, the shadow had answered, moving, drawing back into the ring, because the speaking was a mending. Now the speaking was a keeping, and the answer of a thing that is kept is stillness. The shadow lay in the ring the way a sleeper lies in a bed, and the stillness was the answer, and the village knew it, and was content.

The feast followed. The children ran the ring with new words in the game. The headman stumbled on the name in the middle of his toasting and the village laughed with him, warmly, the way you laugh at a friend. Sander stood at the mill's edge with his children, and they said the name together, and he did not hide it now.

Alba slipped away at the height of the feast, not in fear but in quiet, and went to the stone's shoulder, and sat with her back against the warm flank, the way she had sat the night after the first speaking. The warmth was against her back, solid and steady, and she felt, under her palm, the slow rise and fall of the sleeper, deep and easy, and she said the name once, low, to the stone alone, and the rise and fall went on, unbroken.

A full year of the remembering, kept. Four hinges, four speakings, and a whole turning of the seasons under a true shadow. She thought of the year's marks in the tally book, even and small, and of the long quiet work of it, and she was glad.

The feast went on behind her. The shadow lay true. The giant slept on, remembered, and the meadow was full of the sound of the village keeping its half of the covenant, and that sound, she thought, was the loveliest sound in the world.

Chapter 47: The Shadow, Steady

The tally book filled. Page by page, noon by noon, the small even marks grew, and Alba marked them every day at the hour the shadow lay true, and the book became a second ring, the noon-ring in ink, the record of the keeping.

It had been a year of marks now, every noon true, and she opened the book on the first page and ran her finger down the column, and the marks were as steady as a heartbeat, and she thought: this is what eight hundred years looked like, when the ring-keepers kept them. This is what three hundred empty years stole. And now it is mended, and the line goes on.

The stone's silence was different now, and the village read it the way you read a sleeping friend: the steadiness was the contentment. In the bad days, the stone's silence had been terrible, a silence of holding, of a cry no one could read. Now it was the silence of a thing at rest, the way the quiet of a meadow at noon is not empty but full. The shadow lay true, and the true shadow was the quiet answer, and the village did not need to ask if the giant was well. They could see it, the way you see a person's health in their face.

The noon-ring was worn again, deeper. The shadow had lain in the same groove for eight hundred years, and then for a season it had lain outside it, and the groove had begun to grass over, the wound healing. Now the true shadow lay in it day after day, and the packed earth was wearing again, the groove deepening, and Alba ran her finger along it one noon and felt the new wear, smooth and dark, and she thought: the ring is being written again, in the only way the earth writes, by the patience of a shadow.

She closed the book that evening. The marks filled a page and turned the corner. The stone stood warm in the ring. And she thought of the long patience of the thing: eight hundred years of noons to wear the ring, a season to wound it, and a year to begin the healing, and all the years to come to wear it true again, deeper than before.

The shadow lay steady. The tally grew. And the keeping went on, small and ordinary, a noon at a time, and that, she understood, was the whole of it: not the great deeds, but the steady ones, the marks in the book, the groove in the earth, the name in the mouth, kept, and kept, and kept.

Chapter 48: The Covenant, New

At the year's turning, when the harvest was in and the stooks stood in the rows and the shadow lay true in the ring, the village came together in the meeting hall, and they did something that had not been done in eight hundred years: they wrote the covenant down again, in the common tongue, where all could read it.

It was Old Rolf who called the meeting, and he was grave and easy at once. "The old covenant was written in the old tongue," he said, "and the old tongue died, and the covenant died with it, and we nearly lost the stone for want of a word we could read. We will not make that mistake again. We will write it in the words of the children, so that no future generation can lose it in silence."

Alba was given the writing of it, because she was the Keeper, and she sat at the head table with the fresh quill and the clean page, and the village watched her, and she wrote:

The covenant between the village and the Standing Stone of the Long Meadow.

The stone holds noon. It stands in the ring and holds the day true, so the days keep their length and the fields ripen, as it has held them for eight hundred years.

The village speaks the name. At the hinges of the year — harvest, winter's heart, spring's turning, and every midsummer — the village gathers in the noon-ring and speaks the name aloud, all together, three times: Vaelmund, the one who remembers the agreement.

The Keeper teaches the next Keeper. Whoever holds the name must pass it, hand to hand, so that the name is never lost again, and the rite is never again kept by silence.

She wrote it out, and read it back, and her voice shook a little, because she was writing the thing that could not be lost again, and the village heard it and was moved.

Then they signed it. The headman signed first, then the elders, then the householders, then the children, who signed with their marks, Piper's small careful X in the corner. And when all had signed, they went out into the meadow and stood in the ring, and the sun stood at noon, and the shadow lay true in the groove.

That was the stone's signature. It could not hold a quill; it held noon. The covenant had two signatures now, one in ink and one in light, and the ink one would not fade because the light one held it true.

Alba carried the new scroll to the chest and laid it on top of the old, the new covenant in the common tongue above the old covenant in the old tongue, and she thought of the two of them lying there, the old and the new, and of the eight hundred years between them, and of the long silence that had broken the first and the words that would hold the second.

The shadow lay true. The covenant was written where all could read it. And no future generation could lose it in silence again, because it was no longer kept by one; it was kept by all.

Chapter 49: The Meadow

She spent a full day in the Long Meadow, from first light to dusk, the way she had spent days as a child, and it was the day itself she was keeping, the shape of the meadow's life from one edge to the other.

She rose before light and walked out with the dog while the stars still showed. The meadow lay gray and soft, the dew heavy, and the stone stood at its heart like a dark hill, and the sheep were at the fold, and the first birds were tuning up. She drove the flock out as the sun came over the Wold, and the day began.

Morning was the grazing. The flock spread into the long grass, the lambs bouncing, the ewes slow and full, and Alba walked the meadow's edge, mending a fence post a ewe had leaned on, checking the water trough. The children came out with their noontime errands, Piper with a message from the mill, the other children on their way to the ring, and she watched them run the noon game, checking the groove, their small shadows and the stone's long one.

At noon the shadow lay in the ring, true, and the meadow stopped, the way it had stopped on the great day, and the quiet held for the length of the noon, and Alba sat at the stone's feet in the lee of its shoulder and ate her bread, and the warmth of the stone was at her back, steady as a hearth.

The afternoon was the work. The hay was cut at the meadow's edge, the scythes swinging, the smell of cut grass, and the village's work went on around the stone, the stone at the center of the meadow's life, not apart from it, the way a great tree stands at the center of a field, part of the field's weather. The shadow crawled across the grass, long and slow, and she watched it the way she had watched it all her life, and the watching was a comfort.

Dusk came. The hay was ricked. The children were called in. And Alba did the evening count, the dog's slow gathering, the flock passing the stone on their way to the fold, and as each sheep went by she counted them off, and when the count was right, she said the name, low, to the stone alone, the way she had said it every evening for a year.

Vaelmund. The shadow had gone into the dark; the ring was invisible. But she knew where it lay, the way you know where a thing lies in the dark, and she walked the herder's path home behind the flock, and the meadow closed its day around the stone, and the stone slept on at the center of it, held in the ring, held in the name, held in the ordinary keeping of the village's life.

Chapter 50: The Noon, Forever

The last noon came in the longest light, the tail of summer, and it was an ordinary noon, and that was the whole of the wonder of it.

The village was at its work. The mill turned; the well was drawn; the children were at their lessons; the hay was in. The stone stood at the heart of the Long Meadow, asleep, and its shadow lay in the ring, true, and no one stood in the meadow to watch, because there was nothing to fear and nothing to prove. The keeping had become the day itself.

Alba came alone. She had meant to mark the tally and be gone, but she lingered, and the meadow was empty and gold, and the bees were in the clover, and the flock was grazing at the far edge, and she sat in the lee of the stone's shoulder, the warm flank at her back, and she did not speak at first. She sat, and listened, and the enormous quiet where the stone was opened around her, full of the small sounds of noon.

At the height of the day, when the shadow lay shortest and truest in the groove, she spoke the name once, properly, as she was taught, as Greta had taught her, as she would teach the one after her: Vaelmund.

The stone did not answer.

She had not expected it to. The answers were long past. The shadow lay in the ring, true, and she felt, under her palm on the warm stone, a sleeper's sigh pass through it, slow, and the shadow moved a finger's width out of the groove and settled back into it, and she understood: that was the answer. It sleeps on. It is remembered. It is home.

Forever, she thought, is not a length of time. It is not the counting of years; the years could not hold it. Forever is the shape of a promise kept — the shadow true in the ring, the name known, the Keeper teaching the next Keeper, the covenant written where all can read it, the tallies going on, page after page, as long as there is a hand to mark them. It is a shape you can live inside, the way you live inside a house, or a year, or a name held in the mouth.

She rose at last, and put her hand once more to the stone, and said goodnight in the old tongue, the words Greta had given her, vae, aeld — promise, shadow — and she thought of the long line of it: the giant's patience and her own, the ring worn true again, the seed of the name passed from mouth to mouth, sown every year, never to starve again.

Then she walked the herder's path home. The meadow lay gold in the noon light. The shadow lay in the ring, true. And she carried the name in her mouth like a seed, warm and alive, down the worn path to the fold, and the stone slept on behind her, remembered, at the center of the meadow, at the center of the village's days, held by the shape of the promise, which is the only forever there is.
