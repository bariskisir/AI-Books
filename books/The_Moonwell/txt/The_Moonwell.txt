The Moonwell
A Novel
---
Chapter 1: The Dry Well

Dawn came butter-colored over Thornwater, and Wren went to the well to draw the luck.

It was the morning after the full moon, the one morning of the month she loved best. The dipping. She carried the silvered cup in both hands, the way the rite taught, her sleeves rolled to the elbow. She had done it alone since the winter she was twelve and Mell's sight left him, and she liked the quiet of it, the whole village asleep and the well breathing light.

She counted her steps down to the basin, as she always did. Fourteen. Fifteen. The keeper's stones were warm from the night, and the light of the full moon should have been pooled in the round stone basin like new milk in a pail.

It was empty.

Wren stood at the well's lip and did not understand. She put her hand into the basin and felt stone, dry as bread. No moon. No silver. Nothing at all, where for every full moon of her life there had been a thumb's depth of light.

She had hidden it first. That was the shame of it, and later she could never say why, except that a thing you cannot name is a thing you hide. She knelt and dipped the cup anyway, into emptiness, and the cup stayed cold in her hand, cold as a stone from the riverbed. It had never been cold before. It had always been warm, a little, like something that remembered being held.

"Tomorrow," she told the cup, in the low voice she used when she talked to it. "Tomorrow it will be there. I slept late, that's all."

But the moon had not waited, and the cup knew it. The cup, which hummed when it was dipped, which warmed to her hand like a small animal, lay against her palm without a sound.

She walked back up the slope to the keeper's house, and she did not count her steps. She did not tell Mell. She set the cup on its shelf and went about her chores, and all day she kept her back to the well, and all day the well kept its silence behind her.

At evening she went back down alone, because she could not help it, and stood at the lip and looked in.

It was the silence that frightened her. The well had always been a place of small sounds — the cup's hum, the water's answer, the turning of light. Now there was only the wind moving over the rim, and the dark at the bottom, and the dark said nothing back.

She counted her steps going up. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. She had never needed the sixteenth step before.

Chapter 2: The Luck Turns

It took a week for the village to notice, and a week for the noticing to become blame.

On the first day, Cole the farmer found rust on his wheat, red as iron against the green, and stood at the edge of his field with his hands in his pockets and did not understand. Wheat did not rust in a single night.

On the second day, Pella the dairymaid's milk turned. She had milked the way she always milked, and the milk soured in the pail before she could carry it to the house, and the cream rose wrong and the butter would not set. Pella said nothing to anyone. She poured the milk on the midden and washed the pail twice.

On the third day the Harr sisters, who had kept a fence feud going for eleven years through the medium of geese, threw the feud open at full voice, and half the village gathered to listen, and it was a poor week's entertainment that brought no one any joy.

On the fourth day the weather turned. The sky, which had been the color of new butter, went grey as a wet sheep, and a wind came up off the Grey Sea that smelled of salt and something older, and the crops that were not rusted stood still and would not grow.

And on the seventh day, because the well was the heart of the village and everyone knew it, they gathered at the basin.

Wren stood at the edge of the gathering and wished for a place where she could not be seen. She was small, and people overlooked her, and she had counted on it. But the well was empty, and empty wells make for full eyes, and the council found her before she could slip away.

"She tends it," said the first councillor. "She is the one with the cup."

"She is a child," said the second.

"She is a child who has let the luck run out," said the first. "And the old man taught her."

The word ran through the crowd like wind through barley: dry. Dry, dry, dry. They turned to look at the keeper's house, where Mell sat in his doorway with his blind eyes on nothing, and the looking itself was an accusation.

Wren said nothing. She held the cup behind her back, so that no one would ask why she had not told them. The cup was cold. It had been cold for seven days, and she had told no one, and the cold was hers now, a secret she had made.

That night, for the first time in a week, she went down to the well before the dark was full. She knelt at the lip and spoke to the dark the way she spoke to the cup.

"I'm sorry," she said. "Tell me what to do."

The well said nothing. But behind her, on the path, she heard the tap of a staff, and Mell's voice, low and certain, as if he could see her by the shape of her grief.

"Bring the cup," said Old Mell. "It is time I told you what I remember."

Chapter 3: Old Mell

Mell did not look at the well. He could not. He had not looked at it in nine years, not since the winter his sight left him, the winter the well first faltered. He knelt beside the basin the way a man kneels beside a grave, and he put his hands on the stone rim and read it the way he read everything — with his fingers, with his ears, with the small changes in the air sighted people never learned.

Wren watched his face. She had never watched his face like this, because she had never had reason to be afraid of what it might tell her.

"The rim," said Mell, and his voice was strange. "Run your hand along the rim. Tell me what you find."

She did. The stone was smooth, worn by a hundred years of kneeling hands. But at the north side her fingers found a crack, fine as a hair, dry. It had not been there at the last full moon.

"It's cracked," she said. "Dry. Like the bottom."

Mell was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, it was not about the crack.

"When the well was young," he said, "younger than you, younger than your grandmother, it was not a basin at all. It was a cup. The village held it. The whole village was a cup, and the moon poured into it, and it was full all the time. The light did not come to the well. It lived here."

He tapped his chest, over his heart, and the gesture was so unlike him that Wren felt the fear she had been holding since dawn settle in her like a stone.

"The rite," she said. "You said once there was a rite. The old words."

"There was a rite," said Mell. "There are fragments of a rite. I know them the way a man knows a song he has not sung since childhood — I could not sing it whole to save my life, but I know it was sung." He turned his face toward her, and his eyes were the pale grey of the sea in winter, and she saw, for the first time in her life, that Old Mell was afraid. "There is a book," he said. "In the house. In the old box under the floor. I have not opened it since before I lost my sight, because I thought the well would mend."

His hands came away from the rim. He folded them in his lap, and he looked older than he had looked that morning.

"The well is not going to mend, is it?" said Wren.

"No," said Mell. "I think it is failing, and I cannot see it, and that is the cruelty of it." He reached out, blind, and found her hand, and held it. "But you can see it, Wren. You have seen it for seven days, and you have kept it to yourself because you were afraid, and that is not a sin, that is a kindness gone wrong. Bring the book. If there is a rite, it will have a remedy, and the remedy will have a price, and we will know the price before we pay it."

Chapter 4: The Rite

The book of leaves lived in an iron box under the hearthstone of the keeper's house, wrapped in wool and then in linen, and it smelled of rain and old grass and the particular dark of a thing kept hidden.

Wren lifted it out with both hands. The leaves were not paper but pressed and cured — Mell said they were moon-leaves, the leaves of the silver tree, but Wren had never seen such a tree and thought it one of his old stories. The words were written in the old hand, in ink the color of the sea at night, and the ink had held its color because the book had been kept the way a seed is kept: dry, dark, safe.

"The rite of the Silver Stair," Mell said, when she had read him the title. And again, softer: "It exists. I had begun to think I dreamed it."

The rite was short. That was the first surprise. Instead of pages of when and how and why, there was a verse, a handful of lines, and a description of a thing so strange she read it twice before she understood it.

"When the well is tended," she read aloud, "and the moon is full, the light of the well rises. It thickens. It lifts." She looked up at Mell. "It makes a stair. Steps of silver, going up. Up to the moon."

"To the moon," said Mell. There was no surprise in his voice. He had known. "And the remedy? What must a keeper do?"

Wren read the last lines, and her voice went small.

"A keeper must walk the stair," she read, "and come to the moon's hall, and ask the Moon herself why the well has failed." She set the book down. "Mell. That's a story, the kind they tell children about the sky."

"It's the rite," said Mell. "And you are the only keeper Thornwater has."

"No," said Wren. "I'm the apprentice. I carry the cup and count my steps." She stopped. "I'm afraid of the dark, Mell. You know I'm afraid of the well's dark."

"The dark above and the dark below are the same dark," said Mell, "and only your heart stands between. That's in the book. Read the last verse."

She read it. The Verse of the Walk. Six lines, old words, half of them strange to her, and one line that came clear as water: What the moon lends, the well must keep; what the well gives, the keeper plants.

"I forbid it," said Mell.

Wren looked up. "You — what?"

"I forbid you the climb," said Mell, "because I am an old man and I have lost enough and I will not lose you to a stair made of moonlight." He was quiet a moment, his blind face toward the roof. "And I bless it, because the well is dying, and the only thing I ever taught you is that we do the hard thing when it is the only thing."

He held out his hand, and Wren put the book into it.

"Learn the verse," said Mell. "Learn it until you can say it in your sleep. You will need it on the stair, and the stair will know if you do not mean it."

Chapter 5: The Silvered Cup

That night, in the keeper's house, Wren took the cup down from its shelf and sat with it in the lamplight.

It was a plain thing. That was the whole of its history, in a way — that it was plain. Wren had known the cup all her life and had never thought to wonder why Thornwater kept it, because the villages all had their cups, and a cup was a cup.

But the book had told her, and Mell had told her, and now she looked at it with new eyes.

"Seed," she said to it, in the low voice she used when they were alone. "You were a seed. You were planted, and you grew, and you became a cup."

The cup did not answer. It lay cold in her palm, and she did not expect it to answer. She had talked to it for years, and it had never answered with anything but warmth, and now even the warmth was gone.

"The other villages melted theirs," she said. "When the luck ran thin, they melted their cups for the silver. You cannot melt a seed and expect a harvest." She had learned that line from the book, and it sat in her mouth like a stone you suck to keep from crying. "But you were too plain to sell. Too plain to melt. So you stayed."

She took her sleeve and polished the dented side, the way she did every evening, and for a moment — she could not say why — she was not polishing. She was tending. She was a girl at the rim of a seed, and the seed was listening.

The cup warmed.

It was a small thing, a warmth no larger than a thumbprint, spreading from the dent the way a thaw spreads on a frozen puddle. Wren felt it and went still, afraid to move, afraid to breathe on it and put it out.

"Stay," she whispered. "Stay. Please stay."

The warmth held. It did not grow, but it did not leave.

Mell came in from the doorway, his staff tapping, and she looked up with the cup in her hands, and she did not have to tell him. He could hear it — the way a blind man hears the difference in the air when a light has been lit.

"Put it in your pocket," said Mell. "It will keep it warm." He crossed to the wall where his staff stood — the keeper's staff, older than the cup, worn smooth by a hundred hands. He lifted it down and held it out to her. "Take it. It will be a third leg on the stair."

"The council," said Wren. "They'll say — who tends the well if I don't come back?"

"Then come back," said Mell, "and answer them."

The cup was warm in her pocket. She counted her steps to the door — twelve, thirteen — and stopped.

"Does it know I'm going?"

"It knows," said Mell. "A seed always knows when it is going to be planted. It's been waiting for you since before you were born."

Chapter 6: The Full Moon

The full moon rose over Thornwater like a penny held up to the sky, and the village came down to the well and stood silent in its light.

They came with the tight, careful quiet of people waiting for a bad thing to be confirmed. The councillors stood at the front with their arms folded. Cole stood with his hat in his hands. Pella stood at the back with the children, and the children, who did not understand silence the way the grown ones did, kept looking at the sky.

Wren stood at the well's lip with the cup in her hands and the staff at her side, and she did the thing she had done every month since she was twelve. She knelt. She set the cup on the rim. She spoke the tending verse, the words she knew by heart, low and even, so no one would hear her voice shake.

Nothing happened.

The well stayed dark. The basin stayed dry. The moon hung overhead, full and white and unreachable, and the village held its breath, and Wren felt the whole weight of them pressing down on her bent back.

She said the verse again. But her voice had a crack now, and she heard it, and the village heard it, and the silence stretched like a held breath.

Then the cup moved.

It was a tremor, the way a cup moves when the table it sits on has been jarred. Wren felt it through her fingers — warmth, sudden and flooding, the warmth of a hand held out too long and finally taken.

And in the basin, where there had been nothing, there was a drop of silver.

It lay at the bottom of the dry well like a single coin, and then it grew, not spreading but rising, and Wren understood before she saw it that it was not water and not light but something between, a silver that thickened and lifted and stood.

"The stair," said Mell's voice, from somewhere behind her. "Say the verse of the walk, girl. Now."

She said it. She did not know she knew it, but she had learned it in her sleep as he told her to, and the words came out whole, and the last line fell on the silver and the silver answered.

The stair rose over Thornwater.

It was beautiful and it was terrible: steps of moon-silver floating into the sky without rail or banister, each one hanging on nothing, going up until it ended somewhere the eye could not follow. The village went quiet in a new way — the quiet of not believing what they saw.

Pella came forward first. She pressed a loaf of bread into Wren's hands, still warm. "For the road," she said. And the littlest girl laid a moon-stone — a flat white stone she had found in the yard — on the first step, where a footprint would have gone.

Wren looked up at the stair. It went to the moon. The moon was a long way off, and she was afraid, and she could feel the cup warm and heavy in her pocket, a seed she was carrying up into the sky.

She took the first step. It held.

"Don't you dare not come back," said Pella.

Chapter 7: The Silver Stair

The first step was the easiest. It was knee-high, solid as a stone, and it stood at the well's lip where a step had no business standing, and Wren put her whole weight on it and it did not move.

The second step was harder. It was a foot above the first, and the air under it was empty, and the wind that came off the Grey Sea moved through the space where a banister should have been and found nothing to lean on.

She counted. It was what she did. She counted her steps going down to the well every morning and her steps going up at night, and now she counted the steps of the stair, and the numbers were the only thing that behaved the way they were supposed to behave.

Three. Four. Five.

The stair did not climb straight. It rose in a long spiral, turning over the village like a thread being wound, and the higher she went the wider the world got. The houses shrank to toys. The fields flattened to a green and brown quilt with the well at its center like a button. The Grey Sea came into view beyond the hills, grey as a pigeon's breast, and the wind off it was cold and carried the smell of salt and distance.

She looked down and made the mistake of seeing how high she was.

The step trembled.

It was a doubt, her own doubt, made visible and set under her. The step beneath her wavered like a reflection in a disturbed pool, and Wren understood what Mell had meant.

The stair held while she believed.

She had stopped believing, just for a breath, and the stair had felt it. One more breath and the step would have dissolved under her and she would have fallen the whole bright distance back to the well.

She closed her eyes and felt the cup in her pocket, warm, patient as a held hand. She opened her mouth, and the verse of the walk came out, whole.

"What the moon lends, the well must keep; what the well gives, the keeper plants."

The step steadied. It hardened under her feet like cream setting, like a promise kept, and the trembling ran out of it and was gone.

She opened her eyes. The stair was still there. But she had asked, and it had answered, and that was enough to walk on.

She climbed. Seven, eight, nine, ten. She did not look down again. She counted, and she kept her feet moving, and the wind off the Grey Sea sang in the empty spaces where rails should have been, and the moon got slowly, patiently, closer.

Behind her, far below, she heard the village begin to sing — the old rite, the way the children had learned it and the grown ones had half-remembered it, a ragged, hopeful sound rising on the wind.

It helped more than she could have said. She counted eleven, twelve, thirteen, and she did not stop, because the stair was holding, and she was believing, and the moon was a penny held up to the sky, and she was going to it.

Chapter 8: The Climb

Somewhere past the fourteenth step, Wren heard a sound that did not belong on a stair in the sky.

It was a goat.

She stopped climbing and turned, and there it was — Thornwater's stray goat, the grey one with the torn ear that nobody owned and everybody fed, standing four steps below her with its head tilted and its yellow eyes full of a calm that had no business on a stair of moonlight.

"Go home," said Wren.

The goat considered her. It had the look of a creature that had been following people up the well path for years and saw no reason to stop at the sky.

"Go home," she said again, firmly. "Mell will feed you. Pella will feed you. Everyone feeds you. That's the whole arrangement."

The goat came up a step. The step held. The goat looked extremely pleased with itself, and Wren understood, with the particular weariness of a person who knows she will not win, that she had an escort.

"Fine," she said. "But you'll have to keep up, and if you fall, I'm not catching you."

She climbed. The goat climbed. It was not a companionable arrangement — the goat kept stepping on the hem of her skirt and once nudged her from behind — but it was a company, and company on a stair in the sky was worth more than silver.

She counted, and the counting steadied her. The wind off the Grey Sea had teeth now, and the air was thinning, and the village below had become a smudge of light with the well at its heart like a pinprick.

It was then, with the world reduced to a button of light and the sky a blackness she was climbing into, that Mell's words came back to her, not as memory but as fact.

The dark below and the dark above are the same dark. Only your heart stands between.

She looked down at the well, at the dark circle of it, the dark that had said nothing back to her the morning it ran dry. Then she looked up, at the dark she was climbing into, the dark above, that had its own silence.

They were the same dark. She had known it the way you know a thing you are not ready to believe, and now she believed it, standing on a stair between them, and the believing was like a floor under her.

"What the moon lends," she said aloud, "the well must keep."

The goat bleated, which was not the verse, but it was something.

"Close enough," said Wren.

She climbed on. Twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty-three. The steps were farther apart now, and each one took a little more faith, and the wind sang in the empty places, and the moon hung ahead of her like a door, and behind her, impossibly, the grey goat climbed on, patient as the tide, following her into the dark.

Chapter 9: The First Step

The stair ended.

There was no warning for it. One step, then another, and then the step she stood on was the last one, and above it there was no more stair — only a space of dark, and beyond the dark, nearer than she had let herself believe, the moon.

Wren stood on the last step and did not move.

It was a step like any other: silver, knee-high, solid while she believed. But it stood at the top of the world, at the end of everything solid she had ever known, and the dark above it was not the dark of a night sky seen from a hill. It was the dark of a threshold. It said: leave everything behind, or turn around.

She turned around.

The world lay below her, small and far and dear. She could see the button of light that was Thornwater, and the pinprick that was the well, and she thought of the loaf of bread Pella had given her, still warm in her pack, and the moon-stone the littlest girl had laid on the first step, and Mell sitting in his doorway with his blind eyes toward the sky, waiting for a thing he had forbidden and blessed in the same breath.

She could go down. The stair would hold her. It had held her all the way up, and it would hold her down, and she would walk into the keeper's house and put the cup back on its shelf and never speak of the stair, and the well would stay dry, and the luck would stay spent.

She took a step toward home.

And on the last step, in the space between earth and moon, sat a white hare.

It was white as the inside of a cloud, and it sat on the very edge of the last step with its ears up and its eyes on her, and it did not move, and it did not blink. It had been waiting for her. She understood this with a certainty that had nothing to do with proof: the hare had been waiting for her longer than she had been climbing.

"It's a long way down," she said to it. "And a long way back."

The hare did not answer. It turned its head, very slowly, and looked at the moon, and then looked back at her, and the looking was an invitation and a question in one.

Wren counted her steps. One. Two. She did not know what she was counting anymore. The numbers were a rope in her hand, and she was afraid, and the hare was waiting, and the moon was a door.

"Which of us is braver?" she asked the hare. "You, who sit and wait? Or me, who has to walk into the dark?"

The hare turned and went through the dark, and the dark held it, and it was gone.

Wren stood a long moment on the last step between earth and moon, with the world at her back and the door ahead and the cup warm in her pocket, a seed she was carrying up into the sky.

Then she went after the hare. She did not stop being afraid. She stepped through the dark, and the dark held her too.

Chapter 10: The Clouds

The country between the earth and the moon was a country of cloud.

Wren had expected the dark to be empty, a nothing crossed like a river of night. But the stair had ended, and the dark had opened, and beneath her feet there was ground of a kind — grey and soft, rolling like the downs behind Thornwater, but made of cloud. Cloud fields. Cloud hills. A whole grey land under a black sky, and overhead the earth hung like a blue lamp, small and round and unbearably dear.

She had not known the earth would look like home.

She walked. The cloud held her step the way the stair had held her, but differently — not with faith but with patience, the way a cloud holds a bird that has stopped to rest. The goat walked beside her, and for a long while even the goat was quiet, and the only sound was the soft giving of the cloud underfoot and the wind, which no longer smelled of salt.

The moon was nearer now. She could see its grey seas and its silver fields, the face of a place she was almost home to.

Then the storm gathered.

It came without warning, as sky-storms do, because there is no horizon to watch in the country of cloud. One moment the black sky was still, and the next the cloud field darkened underfoot and the air went heavy and wrong, and lightning split the sky — then thunder, like a door slammed in the roof of the world.

Wren dropped to her knees. Rain came down around her in cold threads, and the cloud under her began to shift and fall, and she had the terrible sense that the ground she stood on could come apart like a loaf and drop her below.

The goat pressed against her, shaking. The cup in her pocket was warm — a steady heat like a hand held against her side.

"Say the verse," she told herself. "Say the verse, Wren."

But the words were gone. The thunder had taken them. She knelt in the cold rain on a dissolving cloud and could not remember the first line of the rite, and the dark was all around her, the same dark below and above.

Then the goat bleated.

It was not a heroic sound. It was the sound a goat makes when it has decided it has had enough of a situation. It was the sound of Thornwater, of the well path, of every ordinary morning she had ever walked to draw the luck.

Wren laughed. It came out as a sob and a laugh together, and the fear loosened.

She found a hollow of cloud, a shelf where the storm could not stand, and she crouched in it with the goat and took the cup out of her pocket. Its light spilled between her fingers, warm and steady as a hearth, and the storm went on above, and Wren held the light and waited.

When the storm passed, it left the sky rinsed and still. The moon's shore was ahead, silver and near.

She put the cup back in her pocket, where it stayed warm.

"I'm coming," she told the moon. "And I've brought a seed."

Chapter 11: The Threshold

The last step did not end in air, as she had feared. It came down at last upon a stone as wide as a harvest wagon, pale and worn, and Wren stepped off onto it with both feet and felt the world settle around her like a coat put on in winter.

Behind her, the Silver Stair had already begun to fade. It did not fall; it let go, step by step, from the bottom up, until only the last step remained, and that too loosened and drifted upward like a ribbon of light set free. She stood on the threshold stone with the cup cold in her hand and counted her heartbeats until the counting steadied her. She had three nights. The stair would return for her, Mell had said, on the third night, to carry her home — if the rite held, if she believed, if she was still a thing the world remembered.

The gate stood before her: a fence of silver reeds taller than any house in Thornwater, and they were not still. They swayed, though there was no wind, and each swaying reed gave off a low note, so that the whole gate was a song she could not keep in her head.

The white hare sat at the gate's foot, patient as bread. It looked back at her once — the same unblinking look it had given her on the last step of the stair, the look that said come or not, I am here either way — and then it slipped between two reeds and was gone into the silver grass.

Wren stood at the threshold and did not follow at once. She turned and looked behind her, down where the stair had been, down through the cloud and the weather to a world she could not see but could feel, the way a hand feels a warm cup in the dark. Somewhere down there, on a hill of new-butter light, stood a stone basin round as a penny moon, and a blind man listening at its rim, and a village that did not believe in her yet.

"What the moon lends, the well must keep," she said to the cup, to the dark, to herself. "What the well gives, the keeper plants."

The cup did not answer. But it was warm in her hand now, warm as it had been on the night of the full moon, and that was answer enough.

She turned her back on the earth and walked through the silver gate.

Chapter 12: The Moon's Land

The land beyond the gate was gentle, and that surprised her most. She had expected a bare place, a place of rock and cold. But the ground rolled in low green-grey hills, and the grass that covered them was silver, and every blade held its own light, so that the fields glowed the way a deep pool glows when the moon is on it.

Above her the sky was black as a well and soft as wool, and in it hung the earth, a round blue lamp with cloud smeared across it like the bloom on a plum. She had never seen the earth from outside before. She had never known it was beautiful. Home, she thought, and the word ached in her chest like a stitch from running.

The silence was not empty. It was a presence, the way the silence of a well is a presence when you are afraid of it — but here it was kind. It held her up. It did not ask her to fill it.

She walked. The silver grass bent away from her knees without sound. Her shadow lay along the ground, and it pointed — she looked, and saw that it ran up the slope, away from her, and its length reached toward the earth in the sky like a finger laid upon a map. She was being called home. The moon's land was under her feet, but her shadow, grown long in the moonlight of this place, would not stop reaching for the blue lamp.

She counted her steps to keep from being afraid. One, the path through the reeds. Two, the first hill. Three, the grass like spilled milk. At seventeen she stopped counting, because the counting had done its work; she was not afraid anymore, only astonished, and astonishment is a kind of faith.

The cup warmed in her hand, and she lifted it and looked into it. A thumb's depth of light lay at the bottom, her own small harvest, and it was the same spring-green as the light in Thornwater's basin on the last night the well was full. She had carried it up the stair without knowing, and it had carried her.

Ahead, low on the horizon, a grey line lay where the fields ended — a shore, of a kind, and beyond it a flat silver plain that did not move like grass.

The white hare sat on a tussock near her feet, watching her as if it had been waiting, and she understood without words that the way forward lay across that plain, and that the plain was a sea.

She walked on, and her shadow went before her, pointing home.

Chapter 13: The Grey Sea

The plain was a sea, and it was not water.

It lay grey and level to the edge of sight, and it did not break or roll; it moved the way a field of ripe wheat moves, in long slow waves that never quite arrived. As Wren drew near, she saw that it was made of dust — fine grey dust, finer than flour, and it stirred as she approached, lifting in spirals that made no sound and left no pattern.

At the water's edge, where a shore would have been, a small boat stood drawn up on the dust, woven from the same silver reeds as the gate. It had no oars. It had no sail. It had a single seat, and on the seat sat the white hare, and the hare looked at her and then looked ahead, and Wren understood that she was to get in.

She waded into the grey dust. It was shallow at the edge and gave under her feet like drifted snow, and where it touched her ankles it was neither cold nor warm but the exact temperature of forgetting. She stepped into the boat. The hare did not move. The boat did not move.

Then the sea spoke.

It did not have a voice. It had a whisper, the way a dry well has a whisper when the wind crosses its mouth — a wordless sound, and then a word, and then a question, and the question was "Who are you?"

The word rose up around the boat like cold thread, and Wren felt her name answer in her throat, rising of its own accord, wanting to be said. She bit it back. Mell's voice, from years ago, from the winter of the dropped bucket: the dark asks, and you must not answer, because to answer is to belong to it.

The boat moved. It did not push off; the shore receded, as if the sea were letting it go. The grey dust lifted and streamed past the reed-woven sides, and the whisper came again, closer, at her ear, at her other ear, at the back of her neck.

"Who are you?"

She set her teeth and held the cup against her heart and counted: one step of the stair, two steps, three. The hare sat motionless on the seat, its ears laid flat, its eyes fixed ahead on a far shore she could not yet see.

The sea pressed its question in the dust's own language. Who are you, who are you, who are you — the words spun up the sides of the boat like water climbing a rope, and one of them touched her lips, grey and soft as ash.

She nearly answered. Her mouth opened. And the cup, held against her heart, went warm, warm as a hand, and the warmth ran up her arm like a branch finding the sun, and she closed her mouth and swallowed her name whole.

The sea could not take what would not be given. The whisper fell away, and the dust settled, and the far shore came clear — dark land rising, and a forest of silver trees standing at its edge like a held breath.

The boat ran its nose up onto the grey shore, and the hare leapt out and was gone into the trees.

Wren climbed out onto the moon's land again, and the cup was warm in her hand, and she had not told the sea her name.

Chapter 14: The Silver Forest

The trees of the Silver Forest were not like the reeds of the gate. They were tall and old and branchy, and their trunks were silver the way a stream is silver — not painted, not mined, but grown, with the grain of wood running through them in pale rings. The wind moved among them, and they rang, the way a dry beech rings in autumn, a note so high and so thin it felt like the edge of hearing.

Under the trees, fruit hung. And it was not fruit.

Every branch carried berries of light. Some were pale as milk; some were gold as candle-flame; some were the deep blue of Crowhollow's well, and some the moss-green of Fellgreen's. Each one held its own glow, steady and small, and the forest was lit from within, by its own harvest, the way Thornwater's basin used to be lit on a full-moon night.

Wren stopped under the branches and looked up, and the trees looked back at her with their thousand small lights, and she understood — the way the heart understands before the mind catches up — that this was where the light of every well that had ever been on Carrow went to grow. Every full moon, every dipping, every cup of moon-silver that a keeper had ever lifted: here it hung, ripening.

And there, on a low branch before her, was a berry she knew.

It was pale as new butter, with the exact color of Thornwater's light, the color of the well on the hill when the morning sun crossed its rim. It was her village's luck, all of it, every year it had been spent and never planted, gathered and hung here to grow old.

Her hand reached out before she had decided anything.

The white hare came between her and the branch, quick as a thought, and stood up on its hind legs, and spoke, and its voice was small and clear, like a bell at the bottom of a well.

"It is not yours," it said. "It is the well's. And the well is yours."

Wren's hand fell. She looked at the berry, pale and patient, and then at her own two hands, and she saw the truth of it. She had not planted that light. Her village had only spent it. To take it now would be to dip a well that was not hers, to steal the harvest of a field she had never seeded.

The cup stirred in her hand. It did not hum; it went warm and still, and she felt it understand — she felt them both understand at the same moment, the way a door understands the key.

She put her hand at her side. "I won't take it," she said. "I'll come back for it."

The hare sat down again, satisfied, and was gone into the ringing dark of the trees.

Wren walked on, deeper, past branches heavy with the light of a hundred wells, and she did not touch a single berry. But she remembered the pale one, and where it hung, and what it was owed.

Chapter 15: The Moon's Palace

The Silver Forest ended at a wall of silver thorn.

It rose high above the treetops, a mass of brambles so dense and so silver that it glittered like a frozen sea, and Wren stood before it and understood that she had reached the palace — because there were no windows and no towers, only this vast grown thing, a single bloom that had taken centuries to flower. It was not built. It was grown, the way a well grows its cup, the way light grows its seed.

She walked around its foot, looking for a door. The thorns were long as her forearm and curved like drawn bows, and they were not still. As she passed, they turned, following her — not threatening, just watching, the way a field of sunflowers watches the sun.

And then she found the door.

It stood open, or almost open: two tall panels of silver thorn that had grown apart, leaving a gap shaped like the space a person makes when they step aside for someone. It had not been opened for her. It had been grown open, long ago, for whoever came on foot and honest.

Inside, a figure waited. Tall, taller than any man of Thornwater, and thin, dressed in grey that moved like water. It did not speak. It did not move. It only stood, with its head tilted, and let her in.

Wren stepped past it, into a room, and stopped.

The room was the night sky.

Not painted, not hung with lamps — the ceiling was a deep black velvet full of stars, and the walls were the blue of the hour before dawn, and the floor under her feet was the grey dust of the sea, perfectly level, holding her the way the night holds a lantern. She had walked into a night arranged as a room, and every room beyond, she would learn, was another night: one for the constellations of spring, one for the small bright star the farmers call the Plough's brother, one where the moon itself was young and full of light.

She passed through them, and the gardener went before her, silent, and did not once look back.

At the end of the last room a door stood ajar, and through the gap came a light she knew — the pale butter-light of Thornwater, and with it the sound of a well being drawn.

Wren's heart turned over. She pushed the door open and entered the great hall of the Moon.

The hall was huge and round, grown from the same silver thorn, and its far end was in shadow — not the shadow of an unlit room, but a darkness that had depth, a darkness that looked back. She had seen its like once before: in a well, from above, when the dark below said nothing.

And in the center of the hall stood a small well, round as a penny moon, ringed by keeper's stones — a copy of Thornwater's well, faithful in every stone.

And kneeling at its rim was a figure who drank.

Chapter 16: The Moon Herself

The figure at the well was the Moon, and Wren knew it the way a child knows the moon from any other light — by the feel of it, by the weight.

She was tall, and she was bent, and she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful: clean and white and old, with nothing left in her that was not either cold or kind. Her hair fell about her in a grey river of light, moving slowly, as if it remembered being a waterfall and had only forgotten the falling. Her eyes, when she lifted them, were the color of the sky before dawn — the pale strip of blue that comes just before the sun, and that is the loneliest color there is.

She did not see Wren. She was kneeling at the rim of the small well, and she was drinking.

Wren watched the Moon's hands cup the water of the well — and it was not water. It was light. It was pale as new butter, the exact light of Thornwater's basin on a full-moon night, and it ran between the Moon's long fingers and down her throat in a slow silver stream, and the Moon drank it the way a thirsty field drinks rain, without joy, without stopping.

And Wren understood, with a horror that had pity in it, like a cold stone with a warm side.

The small well was a copy of Thornwater's well. And the light in it was Thornwater's luck. Every full moon for years, while the village had dipped and dipped and never planted, the well had been drained by this — this drinking, this long grey mouth, this ancient hunger that did not know the wells were mortal.

Her own village's light was passing down the Moon's throat. Her own well was dry because this was full.

She made a sound. It was not a word — a breath, a small gasp, the noise a hand makes when it finds a door that has been locked.

The Moon's drinking stopped.

She looked up, and her eyes found Wren, and there was no surprise in them, only a slow gathering, the way frost gathers on a window — the acknowledgment of a thing present that she had not thought to expect. She blinked, once, like someone waking from a long and thirsty dream.

"Child," said the Moon, and her voice was like wind in a dry well, hollow and far and full of moving air. "Have you come to drink?"

The silvered cup in Wren's hand went cold.

She looked at the cup, and at the Moon, and at the pale light draining in the well between them, and she held the cup up, and her hand shook only a little.

"No," she said. "I've come to ask you why."

The Moon looked at the cup — looked at it for a long time, as if it were a word she had once known and could not quite sound out.

Then her eyes came back to Wren's face, and something passed across them, like the shadow of a cloud crossing a winter field.

"Little one," she said. "Sit with me. It has been so long since anyone asked me anything."

Chapter 17: The Thirst

They sat at the rim of the small well, the Moon and the girl, and the Moon talked, and her voice filled the hall the way a winter wind fills a dry well, with a sound that was mostly hollow and mostly moving.

The night, she said, had been growing dark for a long time. Centuries. Longer than the villages remembered, longer than the rite, longer than the last keeper — the night was thinning, the stars were going out one by one, the dark was pressing in from the edges of the sky like a tide, and she, who lights the dark, had been afraid of it. Afraid, she said, the word falling out of her like a stone from a coat pocket. She, the mother of light, afraid of the dark. It was the oldest shame she carried.

So she had sipped. At first a little, then more. From the wells. The wells were full of light — every well on Carrow, every moonwell, held the light of its village's luck, and she drank from them as the night required, a straw of silver light running from her hall to every basin, and the wells gave and gave, because that is what wells do, and she did not know they could stop.

She did not know the wells were mortal.

Her voice broke on the word, or tried to — it was too old for breaking, and it cracked instead, like ice, and the crack was worse.

Wren sat very still. The cup was cold in her hand. She thought of the basin on the hill, dry as a bone at dawn, and of Mell blind at its rim, and of the village that had blamed her for the emptiness — and the blame had been in the wrong place all along. Not at her, not at Mell. Up here. In a hall of silver thorn, at the mouth of an ancient thirst.

"One hundred years," the Moon said. "No one has climbed the stair in one hundred years. I forgot there were stairs. I forgot the planting. I forgot — " she reached out a long hand toward the small well, and let it fall, " — I forgot how to ask. I take instead. I have been taking for so long I no longer remember the shape of a request."

She turned to Wren, and her eyes were the color of the sky before dawn, and full of the same emptiness.

"Let me have the cup," she said. "The last of Thornwater's luck. Let me drink it, and the night will hold for a little longer, and then — " and here her voice dropped, and it was honest, " — and then I do not know."

The cup hummed in Wren's hand.

Not cold. Not warm. It hummed, low and steady, the way it hummed when it was dipped, and Wren felt it ask her something, and she knew what it asked.

She held it up, out of the Moon's reaching, and the hum was the only sound in the hall.

"No," she said. "It's not for drinking."

The Moon's hand stayed where it was, empty.

"Then what," she said, "is it for?"

Wren looked into the cup — a thumb's depth of green light, the seed of a harvest that had never been planted — and she heard the refrain in her head, the way she heard it at the well, at the stair, at the threshold of the world:

"What the moon lends, the well must keep. What the well gives, the keeper plants."

"It's for planting," she said.

Chapter 18: The Bargain

The Moon looked at the cup, and the cup looked back with its thumb's depth of green light, and the hall held its breath.

"Planting," said the Moon, tasting the word like a fruit she had once loved and had forgotten the sweetness of. "I have not heard that word spoken aloud in a hundred years."

"It's the other half of the rite," Wren said. "You know it. The dipping takes the light; the planting puts it back. The villages kept the dipping and forgot the planting. They spent their luck and never seeded it." She swallowed. "That's why the wells ran dry. Not because you drank them. Because we never put anything back."

The Moon was silent a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way ice is careful before it bears weight.

"And you would have the wells refill? Then I would drink them again, and they would empty again, and the dark would come anyway."

"No." Wren's hands were shaking, and she pressed them flat against her knees to stop them. "The wells refill if the villages plant. Every new moon, the keeper pours the light back into the dark well — the seed returning to the soil — and the light takes root, and at the full moon it comes back brighter than before. You would not drink the seeded light. You would drink only the overflow — the light that spills over a full well's rim. There is always overflow when a well is full. There would be more for you, not less. Forever."

The Moon's eyes narrowed to the color of the sky before dawn.

"They forgot once," she said. "They will forget again. People forget. It is what they are for. I will drink the wells and the dark will hold, and that is the only bargain that cannot be broken."

Wren felt the whole climb — the trembling stair, the grey sea's question, the forest of spent light — press down on her shoulders, and she heard Mell's voice in her memory: the dark below and the dark above are the same dark; only your heart stands between.

"Then add a thread to it," she said. "Once a year, at the full moon, a keeper will climb the stair to your hall. A Keeper's Walk. And they will share a cup with you — one cup, shared, so that you are never alone one night in the year."

The Moon went very still.

No one had offered her company. No one had ever offered her a cup to share. She had taken and taken and taken, and in all the centuries no one had offered her a thing.

She opened her mouth, and nothing came out. She closed it.

And then, because she was very old and very weary and had forgotten how to ask — because her whole tragedy was that she took instead of asking — she remembered. It came to her like the first grey note of dawn, like water finding the level, like a word she had known before the world began.

She asked.

"Child," she said, and her voice cracked the way old ice cracks, and a single tear slid down her cheek, and it was silver, "will you share a cup with me?"

The tear left her face and fell, and Wren, without thinking, held out the cup, and the tear fell into it, and the green light in the cup went silver, and the seed was made.

"Then it's a bargain," Wren said. "I'll plant this. And I'll come back."

The Moon looked at the cup, and at Wren, and at the long dark years behind her, and she smiled, the way winter smiles when it turns toward spring.

"It's a bargain," she said.

Chapter 19: The Cup Explained

The Moon took the cup from Wren's hands as if it were a bird, and turned it over in her long fingers, and smiled at its dent.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked. "Truly?"

"A cup," Wren said. "Mell says it's older than the village. Too plain to sell, so it survived when the others were melted down."

"Survived," the Moon repeated, and she laughed, a sound like wind in a dry well, but warmer. "Little one. It is a seed."

Wren stared at it — plain, a little dented, older than the village — and the word went into her like a drop of water going into dry ground.

"Every silvered cup was planted once, long ago," the Moon said. "A seed of moonlight, put into the dark earth of a new well, and watered with the new moon, and it grew. It grew into a cup. To dip it is to harvest. To pour it back is to plant. The well is the soil. The dipping is the harvest. And this — " she lifted the cup, and the green-and-silver light in it turned slow, " — is the last seed of its kind on the peninsula. The others were melted. You cannot melt a seed and expect a harvest."

She said the words like a verse, and Wren heard the shape of them and kept them.

"But I don't know how to plant," Wren said. "The rite is lost. Mell knows fragments. The book of leaves holds the whole — but he could not read me all of it before I climbed."

The Moon considered her, head tilted like a bird.

"Then I will teach you the planting verse," she said. "And you will carry it home. That is the bargain too — that the rite returns to the world."

She spoke the verse slowly, one line at a time, and Wren repeated each line until it was her own:

"Light of the well, seed of the moon,
return to the dark and take your root.
What was borrowed, return to the ground;
what was planted, rise to the crown."

In the courtyard of the palace — a round floor of grey dust open to the black-velvet sky, with the earth hanging blue overhead like a lamp in a window — Wren practiced. She held the cup over a small hollow she had scooped in the dust, and she spoke the verse, and she poured.

Nothing. The light vanished into the dust, and the dust kept it.

The tall gardener stood at the edge of the courtyard, silent, his head tilted, watching.

"Again," said the Moon from the doorway.

Wren scooped the hollow fresh, held the cup, spoke the verse, poured.

The second time, the cup went warm in her hand.

The third time, it hummed.

The fourth time, she did not pour at all. She spoke the verse and tipped the cup, and the light did not fall — it descended, slow as a settling snow, and the instant it touched the dust, it did not vanish. It took root.

A stem of silver light rose out of the grey dust, and a leaf unfolded, and the leaf was green — spring-green, the color of hope.

The first bloom in a hundred years.

The gardener bowed, very slowly, his head going down like a drawn curtain, and the Moon stood in the doorway with her hand over her mouth, and Wren knelt in the silver courtyard with a growing thing in her hands and wept without meaning to.

The cup, in her grip, was warm as a heartbeat.

Chapter 20: The Planting

The small well in the Moon's hall was not, the Moon said, a copy of Thornwater's well.

"It is the mother," she said. "Every well on Carrow is a child of this one. Their light runs to it as water runs to the sea. When this well is fed, every well is fed."

Wren stood at its rim and looked down into it. It was deep and round and dark, ringed by keeper's stones worn smooth by an age of knees, and it was empty — not dry the way Thornwater's basin was dry, with the dust of spent years at the bottom, but empty, waiting, the way a field waits in spring for the seed.

"This is where the first cup was planted," the Moon said. "Ages ago. And this is where it must be planted again."

Wren's hands were shaking, and she let them shake. She knelt at the rim, on the worn stones, the way Mell knelt at Thornwater's basin, and she held the cup out over the dark.

The light in the cup had changed. It was no longer a thumb's depth of green; it was full to the brim, green and silver together, the color of the Moon's tear and the color of hope, and it moved in the cup the way a living thing moves, slow and breathing.

She spoke the planting verse, one line at a time, in the voice she used for the tending verse, the voice that carried across a well:

"Light of the well, seed of the moon,
return to the dark and take your root.
What was borrowed, return to the ground;
what was planted, rise to the crown."

And she poured.

The light left the cup in a single silver stream and fell into the mother well, and for a moment nothing happened. The dark drank it, the way the dry basin on the hill had drunk her pouring on the night of the new moon.

Then the bottom of the mother well lit.

It was small — a coin of light, a thumb's depth of it, lying at the bottom of the dark like the first drop of rain in a dry stream. But it held. It did not go out. It lay there and breathed, and Wren felt the whole hall breathe with it, and the Moon made a sound like a held breath let go.

"Come," said the Moon, and she led Wren to the wall of the hall, and she passed her hand over the silver thorn, and the wall cleared like water, and Wren looked through it and saw Carrow — the whole peninsula, green hills and three coasts, ringed by the grey sea, from above, the way the earth hangs in the black-velvet sky.

And over it ran a web.

Silver threads, thin as spider-silk, ran from the mother well out across the whole land — one to Thornwater, one to Saltmere, one to Haybrook, one to Fellgreen, one to Crowhollow, one to Oldwick, and a hundred more to villages Wren did not know the names of. They were thin, and they were dark. They hung slack over the land like a net whose fish had all been taken.

As Wren watched, the thread that ran to Thornwater gave a small shudder — and a light began in it, at the far end, at the mother well, and traveled outward, slow as honey, a green-and-silver light creeping toward the hill where her own basin stood, dark and waiting.

"It is beginning," the Moon said softly. "The planting done right. The well is taking its root."

Wren stood with her hand on the wall of the Moon's hall, and watched her village's thread wake, and the cup, warm and empty in her other hand, was as light as a promise.

Chapter 21: The Forgotten Rite

The Moon told it as a story she had mostly forgotten, the way you forget a field you once had, or a name on a tombstone.

There had been a harvest, once, that failed. Not a crop of wheat, but a crop of light, taken thin by a long, sullen year. The villages grew afraid. Their wells were low, and low wells bred low hearts. So at the new moon, instead of planting their cups, the villages drank them. They poured the last of the seeded light into their mouths and their lamps and their little hungers, and for a season they were warm and bright.

Then the next full moon came, and there was nothing to dip.

They did not mean to forget. That is the terrible part, the Moon said; a village that forgets by intention is wicked, but a village that forgets by habit is only human. The wells emptied, one by one, over generations. The cups were sold and melted, because you cannot eat a thing you have forgotten is a seed. The verses went from the keeping into the singing, from the singing into the silence.

And the last keeper — a girl, the Moon said, not unlike Wren — had climbed, a hundred years ago, empty-handed. She had stood where Wren stood, with the mother well between them, and asked the same question, and the Moon had not known how to answer it. The girl had walked back down the stair, and gone home to a village that would not believe her, and the planting had died with her, for want of one voice to carry it.

The Moon looked at Wren across her own silver light. She had forgotten even that girl's name. She had been forgetting for so long.

"Then I will make them believe," Wren said.

The Moon was very still. Wren had never seen anything so old, or so tired, or so close to something that might, in a creature made of night, be called hope.

"How?" the Moon asked.

"I will carry the planting home," Wren said. "I will plant it in every well, and come back next year, and the year after, to prove it holds." She counted the steps of it on her fingers. "A village believes what it can see grow."

The Moon was quiet a long time. Then she reached out, and her long fingers came to rest over Wren's heart, and left a warmth there like a handprint in new wool.

"Then I will be seen," she said. "I will come to the wells' edge in my light, so the children know I am real."

Wren could not say whether she had won anything. But the dark in the far hall seemed to draw back a little, like water before a rising shore.

Chapter 22: The Promise

They made the bargain over the mother well, and the Moon bent her grey light down to it like a woman bending to a child's face.

"I will drink only the overflow," she said, "the light that spills over a full well's rim. Never the seeded light. Never the roots." Her voice was like wind in a dry well, but there was a new thing in it, a softness she seemed not to have used in a century. "And I will drink only where the keeper plants."

Wren set her hand flat on the stone rim, the way Mell set his hand on the well at home. "And every village will plant at every new moon. The cup's light poured back into the dark, so it takes root and returns brighter."

"So it returns," the Moon repeated, tasting the words as if they were new. "I had forgotten that light could return."

There was a silence. In it, Wren heard something she had not heard since she left the earth: the faint ringing of the cup against her hip, humming like a struck bell, warm. She drew it out and held it up, and the thumb's depth of moonlight in it swayed and rose, as if it remembered a hand.

"You offered me a drink," Wren said, "and I refused it, because the cup is not for drinking. But it can be shared."

The Moon's eyes widened, the color of the sky before dawn.

"Once a year," Wren said, "at the full moon, a keeper will climb the stair. Not to ask, not to plant, but to share one cup with you, and to sit with you while the night turns. So that you are never alone one night in the year. That is the Keeper's Walk."

The Moon went very still, and Wren saw that she was old, so old, and that no one had offered her a thing in a hundred years, because everyone had been afraid to ask her for anything, and she had forgotten how to ask for anything herself. She had taken instead of asking. And here was a girl holding up a plain, dented cup as if it were a star, saying: I will sit with you.

The Moon reached for the cup, slowly, and her fingers did not take it. They hovered over it, and the cup's light rose up to meet her, and the Moon said, very softly, "I remember now. I remember how to ask."

"Then ask," Wren said.

The Moon looked at her, and the night sky arranged itself into a kind of smile behind her, and one silver tear gathered at the corner of her eye, bright as a new moon, and fell into the mother well, where it lay like a seed in soil.

"I ask," she said, "for you to teach me the planting, before I forget it again."

Wren put the cup into the Moon's long hands. "We will learn it together."

And in that moment, the threads from every well on Carrow brightened, one by one, like lamps waking down a long road.

Chapter 23: The Return

She left the palace at the hour the Moon called morning, which on the moon is only a brightening of the black velvet, a lifting of the silver.

The gardener stood in the doorway of the great bloom, tall and silent, and Wren thought she saw, in the tilt of his head, a kind of approval. She paused, and did an awkward thing — she curtsied, the way the village children did to the councillors, and the gardener bowed, slow and deep, the silver of him folding like water. Then he stepped back into the dark of the palace, and the bloom closed behind him, and Wren was alone on the moon's land.

She went back the way she had come, past the fields of silver grass that whispered to her feet, to the shore of the Grey Sea. The boat of moon reeds still waited, and the white hare sat in it, grooming one ear as if she had only been passing the time.

The sea began at once, its grey dust hissing up the beach: Who are you? Who are you?

Wren set her foot in the boat, and the hare nudged the tiller with her nose, and the boat slid out over the grey. The whispers crowded the hull, patient and endless. Last time she had not answered, and the sea had had no hold on her. Now she had a name she was proud to carry.

"A keeper returning," she said. "I am a keeper, returning home."

The sea was silent a long moment. Then it made a sound like a whole coast drawing a breath, and the grey parted around the boat, smooth and generous, and the far shore came quickly.

On the far shore the Silver Forest stood full of its ringing light. Wren did not take any fruit. She walked between the trees with her hands open, and the branches bowed over her, and she understood that they were glad of her, the way a field is glad of rain. Thornwater's pale berry glowed on its branch, fat now, brighter than before, as if the planting she had promised had already begun to feed it.

The moon's land, she saw as she walked, was brighter than when she had come. The threads she could see now, at the edges of everything, running silver and clear to the small blue lamp of the earth hanging in the black.

She ran. She had not known she could run on the moon, but her feet found the way, and the last steps of the silver stair stood waiting where she had left them, three nights gone, true as a promise.

Chapter 24: The Stair Down

The stair had returned for its third and last night, and the descent was harder than the climb had been.

Going up, the world had been below her, and she had only had to believe she could rise. Going down, the world was below her too, and small, and she could see all the way to the clouds, grey and heaped like a cold sea, and beyond them, faint, the curve of the earth with its one lamp of a moon. It was one thing to leave home on a stair of faith. It was another to walk home on it, when she knew what she carried.

She set her foot on the first step, and it held. She counted them, the way she counted when she was frightened: one, two, three, down into the sky. The wind off the Grey Sea came up through the gaps between the steps, and the steps were silver, and the gaps were night, and there was nothing between her and the dark below but the width of her own two feet.

About a hundred steps down, a doubt came to her, small and quiet, the way doubt does: What if the villages do not believe you? What if the planting fails? What if you dreamed the Moon, and the well at home is still dry?

The step beneath her trembled, like a leaf about to let go. Wren went cold. She stopped counting, which frightened her more than the trembling, and she gripped the cup through her coat, and it hummed against her palm, warm as a hand.

She spoke the verse of the walk, the one Mell had taught her at the foot of the stair, her voice thin in the wind: "What the moon lends, the well must keep; what the well gives, the keeper plants."

The step steadied under her, and the next, and the next, the silver firming like cream setting.

She went on, counting again, one to two to three, until the clouds came up to meet her, soft and grey as wool, and she walked down through them, and the rain of the last storm had passed and the air was clean.

Through the last of the cloud she saw the earth growing, the hills of Carrow, the neck of the peninsula, and then the north hill, and the roofs of Thornwater, and the little lights of the village windows, all of them burning, all of them kept. They had kept watch for her. The lights did not move, but they were lit, and Wren understood that they had been lit every night for three nights, for her.

The last step was the well's rim. She stood above it, the dark water far below, and she was not afraid of the well's dark anymore.

She stepped down into the light of her own home.

Chapter 25: Thornwater

She stepped off the stair into the well at dawn, and the well was still dry.

The village had gathered. They had seen her come down out of the light, three nights gone, and they had come to the well's rim with their shawls and their bread and their hard faces. Wren stood in the basin, the silvered cup in her hand, and she saw them all: the baker with his flour-pale arms, the three councillors, Pella with her apron, the Harr sisters at each other's shoulders, Cole with his rusting wheat in his eyes. And Mell, at the front, his blind face turned up to her, listening.

She told them about the Moon. She told them about the mother well in the great bloom, and the threads to every well on Carrow, and the old, hungry, lonely thing that drank their light because the night was growing dark and she was afraid. She told them about the bargain, and the planting, and the seed the Moon had wept into the well.

The baker laughed. It was not an unkind laugh, but it was a laugh, and it carried. "A girl," he said, "gone up a stair of light for three days, and come down with a story." He looked at her empty hands. "What have you brought back, Wren? A tale for the children?"

The councillors nodded. One of them said her name as if testing a coin for weight. Wren, they said. Wren.

She had known it would be like this. Mell had warned her, in his way, with the story of the last keeper, and she had counted the steps of it all the way down the stair. So she did not argue. She reached into her coat, and drew out the thing the Moon had given her in the palace: the star-splinter, a chip of the Moon's own light, dark now, a piece of night shaped like a shard of glass.

She held it up, and it gave back nothing. The village looked at it, and saw only darkness.

"This is all I have," she said, "until the new moon." She looked at the baker, and then at Mell, and then at the children, who had pushed to the front, small and quiet. "Wait for the new moon. Then I will show you what I brought home."

The baker shrugged, and the councillors turned away, and the village began to break up, muttering, half-convinced and half-angry, the way a village is.

Only Mell did not move. He stood at the rim with his head cocked, and when the crowd had gone he said, softly, so that only she could hear, "I believe you, Wren. I believe every word of it."

He had no reason to. But Wren looked at his blind, listening face, and she felt the star-splinter warm in her pocket, small and certain as a seed.

"Then I will make the rest believe," she said, and the well's silence was no longer an enemy but a waiting.

Chapter 26: The Teaching

The days that followed were the hardest Wren had ever walked, because they asked her to be patient on the ground, where patience has no light in it.

She taught the planting rite in the keeper's stone house, in the long afternoons when the village was at its work. She taught the verses slowly, the way Mell had taught her the well: one word at a time, until the word lived in the mouth. The Planting Verse, the Verse of the Cup, the Verse of the Thread. "The dipping is the harvest," she told them. "The planting is the seed. You cannot take from the well and give nothing back."

The baker came once, and left halfway. The councillors did not come at all. But the children came, every day, and they learned the verses first, the way children learn anything — by singing it at their chores, by getting it gloriously wrong and being right anyway. Little Tam, five years old, planted his toy cup in the garden and watered it. The Harr sisters argued, at the well, about who had the right to plant first, and the argument was so loud that the goat came to see, and the goat was louder.

Only one cup in Thornwater, Wren said, and there could only be one. But Mell, in his workshop behind the house, shaped a second cup from moonwood, the pale wood of the forest's edge that holds light the way the basin holds water. He worked by feel, his hands knowing the curve the way his ears knew her voice, and when he set the second cup into her hands she saw that it was the first cup's shadow, or its echo, or its little sister.

"You will need more than one," he said, "for the village will grow into the rite, and the rite will outgrow the village." He ran his fingers over the cup's rim. "The old book of leaves has the whole rite, Wren. I have kept the memory. Lend me your eyes, and we will copy it out whole, so no one ever forgets again."

They worked by lamplight, his hands over the brittle leaves, her eyes reading the old hand aloud, and the verses came back into the air of the house one by one, the whole rite, from the Tending Verse to the Verse of Thanks, all but one, which would not be written for many years.

The children learned the cup's warmth. The baker's laugh grew quieter. The well stayed dry, and the days stayed grey, and Wren counted them.

Mell said, one evening, "You are teaching them something they cannot see, Wren. That is the hardest teaching there is. It is the only one that lasts."

Chapter 27: The First Planting

The new moon came down over Thornwater as a black penny on a silver tray of stars, and the village gathered at the well.

They had come, Wren saw, to see for themselves. The baker stood at the back with his arms folded. The councillors stood together, like three stones. Pella held a child on her hip, and the child was quiet. Cole had left his fields early. The Harr sisters stood on opposite sides of the well, which was its own kind of attendance. And Mell stood at the front, close enough to feel the warmth of the gathering, his face turned up to the dark sky as if he could already see the moon he could not look at.

Wren stepped into the basin, and the silence came down like snow.

She had rehearsed it a hundred times in the dark of her own room, counting the steps. Now, with the whole village breathing behind her, she took the star-splinter from her coat. It was dark, a piece of night shaped like a shard of glass, and she held it up over the dry well, and she spoke the Planting Verse — the words Mell's hands had taught her, the words the book of leaves had held for a hundred forgotten years:

"What the moon lends, the well must keep; what the well gives, the keeper plants."

And she opened her hand.

The star-splinter bloomed. It did not fall like a stone; it unfolded, the way the first bud of spring unfolds, a silver-green light opening out of the dark of itself, and Wren felt the Moon's warmth in it, the handprint the Moon had left over her heart. This was the seed. This was the Moon's blessing, wept into the mother well, and carried home, and kept dark until it was safe to bloom.

She poured it into the well.

The light went down like poured milk, and the well drank. Wren held her breath, and behind her the whole village held its breath, and even the Harr sisters forgot to be angry.

The well drank, and then the well was empty, and dark, and still.

For a long moment nothing happened. Wren heard the baker shift his feet. One of the councillors cleared his throat. A child whispered, "Did it work?" and a mother shushed her.

Then, at the bottom of the basin, in the deepest dark, there was a single drop of silver. It lay there like a small eye, or the first star, or a seed in black soil.

The village's breath came out all at once, like wind through a crack.

Wren knelt in the basin, and she did not touch the drop, because she knew better. She had planted it. It was not hers anymore. It was the well's.

"Now," she said, to the drop, to the dark, to the whole holding silence of her village, "we wait, and we tend, and we never forget again."

Chapter 28: The Well Refills

The well refilled the way a tree grows: so slowly that no one could say the moment it happened, and all at once, when they looked.

On the first night there was the single drop. On the second, a thin film of silver, like a skin of ice over the dark. Wren came to the well every morning and every evening, and she brought the village's children, and they knelt at the rim and watched the bottom of the basin the way you watch a pot you are willing to boil.

By the first quarter of the moon, there was a hand's depth of silver in the well. It did not move like water; it moved like light, lapping gently, giving back the sky, and when the children leaned over it they saw themselves doubled, small and silver.

The cup hummed every time Wren came near. It knew, she thought. It remembered the planting, the way a seed remembers the spring, and it was waiting.

On the night of the first quarter, when the moon stood half risen over the north hill, Wren dipped the cup.

She had never done it right before. The dipping she had grown up with, the empty dipping of an empty well, had been a habit, a thing done because it was done. Now she knelt, and she held the cup over the silver, and she spoke the Tending Verse, and she lowered the cup in.

The cup drank. It did not scoop; it drew the light up into itself, the way a root draws water, and Wren felt it, through the plain dented silver, a warmth that ran up her arm and sat behind her breastbone like a small sun.

She lifted it, and the cup held a thumb's depth of moonlight, and the light in it was green.

Not silver. Spring-green, the color of new leaves, of grass after rain, of hope. Wren had never seen the color of the planted light, because no one in living memory had planted it. The Moon's tear, she understood. The seed had been wept, and the light it returned was the color of the thing it had been born from.

"Look," she whispered, and the children looked, and one of them said, "It's the color of my garden," and another said, "It's the color of the hill in spring," and Pella, who had come with the evening milk, said, "It's the color of butter, before the salt."

The cup hummed, and the well held its green light, and Wren stood at the rim with the dipped cup in her hands, warming them, and she understood that the well would not be dry again in her lifetime, or her children's.

She poured the thumb's depth back into the well, because the cup is not for keeping, only for tending. And the well, which had held a drop, and then a film, and then a hand's depth, took the dipped light back into itself like a mother taking her child's hand, and went on growing, slowly, toward the full moon.

Chapter 29: The Luck Returns

Luck came back to Thornwater the way weather comes back to a field: not announced, just there, in the small things first.

Cole found it in his wheat. The rust that had yellowed the leaves had gone the way a bad dream goes, and the stalks stood straight, green to the ear, and he stood among them in the dusk with his hat in his hands and did not know what to do with his face.

Pella found it in her milk. The cream that had soured in the churn sweetened overnight, and the butter came up gold, and her mother, who had not smiled since the dry months began, smiled at the churn and did not say why.

The Harr sisters found it in themselves. They met at the well to draw water and, without quite meaning to, laughed — at the feud, at the geese, at the hundred small angers they had carried up the hill like buckets and emptied into the dry well. It was the first laugh between them in a year, and it made the other villagers turn their heads, because the sound was strange the way a thrush is strange after a long winter.

And the well itself. The well was not full, not yet, but the green light had risen, night by night, and the village came to stand at its rim the way people come to stand at a fire. Nobody said the verse yet. But the children sang it, and the song went up the hill and down again, and the adults found themselves humming it at their work: What the moon lends, the well must keep.

The morning the baby was born, the village named her Moon.

It was Pella who said it, at the birthing, when the child came out squalling into the dawn and the window was full of the setting moon, huge and white and low over the north hill. "Moon," Pella said. "We'll call her Moon." And the name ran through the village like the green light ran through the well, and the old women wept, because no child had been named Moon in a hundred years, not since the last one, the one whose name nobody remembered.

Wren went to the well that evening and told it. "They named her Moon," she said to the green light. "They remembered."

The cup warmed against her hip, and she took it out, and it hummed, and she thought of the Moon in her palace of silver, old and hungry and alone, and she said a small thing, not a verse, just words: "You are not alone tonight. There is a child named for you."

The well's light flickered, and steadied, and grew a little, and Wren walked home counting her steps, and behind her the new moon's first thin crescent was rising in the last of the blue, and Thornwater, for the first time in a year, went to sleep full.

Chapter 30: The Crops

The harvest came in fullest memory, and the village understood, at last, what a tended well was for.

The wheat stood so heavy that the fields leaned, and the rye silvered at dusk the way water silvers, and the apples hung on the branches like the green light of the well hung on its dark. Cole cut the first sheaf with the whole village watching, and he carried it to the well and set it at the rim, and the green light rose up over it, and no one had to say the verse. They said it anyway, all of them, the way a village says a hymn, the whole hill humming: "What the moon lends, the well must keep; what the well gives, the keeper plants."

The field was a second well, Wren thought, standing at the rim with the cup in her hands. The light soaked out of the well and into the land, and the land gave it back as grain, as butter, as the round cheeks of children. The wells of Carrow were not separate. They were one family, and the land between them was the cup.

She walked out at evening through the standing grain, and the dusk came up the hill like the tide, and the wheat went silver in it, field by field, the way the well had gone silver, night by night. It was the same light. She could see it now, with the keeper's eyes Mell had been training since she was small — the threads running from the well at the village heart, thin and bright, into the roots and the furrows and the milk-shed, into everything the village ate and built and slept under.

She had brought it back. No — she had brought the way to make it. The light had always been there, lent from the night, and the well had kept it, and she had remembered how to plant it.

The harvest moon rose, full and huge and white, and the wheat went all the way silver, and the well answered it, the green light rising to meet the white, so that it seemed for a moment that the earth and the sky were drawing one another up.

Wren knelt in the stubble at the field's edge, and the cup hummed in her hand, and she did not count her steps, because for the first time in a year she was not frightened of anything.

The village's lamps came on below, one by one, like seeds waking. And the harvest went in, and the barns were full, and the wells were full, and the luck that had run dry had been planted, and it had come back green.

Chapter 31: The Milk Sweetens

Pella's dairy had been the first thing to turn. Wren remembered the morning the milk soured in the pail — the smell of it filling the yard, and Pella standing at the door with her hands wet, saying, "The luck has gone out of it." It was the first they knew. Now, three mornings after the new moon, the milk stood in the pail as sweet as rain.

Wren came to the dairy at first light because she had promised, and because she wanted to see it herself. Pella had the butter churn going before the sun cleared the hill. "Come," she said. "Taste it."

The milk was pale and thick and tasted of clover and the faintest silver, like the air after a warm night. Wren stood in the warm dim of the dairy with the cup cold against her belt and felt the small ache of a thing gone right. She had told the village about the Moon and about planting. The baker had laughed. The councillors had huffed. Only Mell had believed. But the milk, the milk did not need to believe; it had simply sweetened.

"You always believed," Wren said.

Pella worked the churn without looking up. "I believed the well would not lie," she said. "A well does not empty without a reason, and a reason can be mended. That is all I knew." She lifted the lid and the butter came up gold as a penny. "The children have been watching the basin every evening. The little ones. They learned your verses before their letters."

Wren thought of Sorrel, twelve years old, who had followed her through the dry months and chanted the planting verse under her breath at chores. The children had been first to believe, the way they believed in the moon itself.

"There is a luck in children," Mell had said once, "because they have not learned to spend it."

The butter was wrapped in cloth. The milk was set in the cool shelf. Pella opened the door and the morning came in, and the whole valley smelled of turned earth and new green. "Whatever you did," Pella said, "however you mended the reason — keep mending it." Her voice was steady as a kept flame. "The milk is not for me. It is for the village, and for the ones who will be born into it."

Wren walked back to the well through the dew, the cup warming against her belt. She counted her steps the way she did when she was frightened, and she was not frightened now — only tender, in the way of small things that hold more than their size. Behind her, the butter churn went on its patient song. Ahead, the well stood round and quiet in the new light, waiting for the evening when the children would come to watch it fill.

Chapter 32: The Quarrels End

The Harr sisters had not spoken in nine years. Nobody could say what the fence had been built for in the beginning — a boundary, a borrowed rake, a word said at a well-side and taken wrong. The fence itself had gone from a good split-rail to a leaning thing, and the feud had gone from a fence to a law. They kept geese. The geese, who could not know they were part of a quarrel, wandered between the two fields and were claimed by whichever sister was angrier.

The week the well refilled, the fence came down. Not by violence — a storm took the last good post, and the two sisters came out at the same dawn to find the wire lying in the wet grass between their farms.

Wren watched from the lane with Sorrel. She had not planned it. But the well's light had done a quiet work in the village, the way light does — you do not see it moving, only its result. And the light had reached the fence.

For a moment neither sister moved. Then Adah Harr bent and took up the wire, and Mary Harr took up the other end, and they carried it between them, wordless, to the pile where the old rails were stacked. They worked all morning. By noon the new fence was rising straight and true, and by the time the light leaned toward evening they had mended, too, the small seam of the years between them.

"Nine years," said Adah, wiping her hands. "Nine years of geese."

They laughed. It was the first laugh either had given in the village's hearing since before the drought, and it seemed to loosen something in the air itself.

That evening the village did a strange and old thing. One by one they came to the well — not to draw, not to ask, but to lay down. The baker laid a crust of the first risen loaf. The councillor who had laughed at Wren laid a hand-carved peg, the one he had kept back when he had doubted. Wren watched them come, each carrying some small anger or small debt, and lay it at the well's rim like a stone. The well's light, spring-green and quiet, drank the shadows of them as they turned away lighter.

"The quarrels end because the luck has returned," Wren said to Mell that night, at the keeper's door.

"No," said Mell. "You have it backward. The luck returns because the quarrels end. A well cannot hold light that is not given. And the village has finally remembered how to give." His blind eyes turned toward the basin, where the light lay like a held breath. "You cannot hoard a thing into fullness, child. You can only plant it."

Wren put her hand on the cup. It was warm. Above the well, the moon was rising, thin and patient, and the web of silver threads that only she could see hummed softly all the way to the sea. The geese, settled at last in one field, made their contented low mutter in the dark.

Chapter 33: The Neighboring Villages

Saltmere lay on the west coast, and its well stood by the harbor, ringed with stones worn white by sea-salt. Its light had once been salt-white, the color of a gull's underside; now the basin was dry, and the stones were scoured clean and gray.

The people of Saltmere met Wren and Mell at the well-side, and their faces were not kind. "You have stolen our light," said the harbor-master. "Thornwater shines now, and we are dark. You have drunk our luck."

Wren had expected this. She had been afraid of it, and she counted her steps silently — eight, nine, ten — before she spoke.

"No well can steal another's light," she said. "The light is not carried over the hills in a pail. It is seeded where it grows. What the moon lends, the well must keep." She took the silvered cup from her belt. "This cup was a seed once, planted in moon-silver, and it grew. We forgot how to plant. That is all that happened. It happened to you, and it happened to us, and it can be undone."

The harbor-master spat. But an old woman among the crowd, whose face was worn the way the stones were worn, came forward and put her hand on the cup. "It is warm," she said. "A seed is warm. Our well is cold."

They taught the planting that night, in the salt wind, by the light of a single lantern. Mell spoke the verses from memory, his voice carrying the old words, and Wren knelt at the dry basin and poured the first seed of light down into the dark. The Saltmere people watched, skeptical and hungry, and the cup hummed in her hand like a struck bell.

"It will take a month," Wren told them. "A moon, maybe two. The well was dry a long time. You must plant every new moon, and dip every full, and never spend what you have not seeded."

The old woman who had felt the cup's warmth became Saltmere's keeper on the spot — there was no ceremony, only the need, and the verses passed from Mell's mouth to hers. When Wren and Mell left in the morning, the old woman stood at the well with her hands resting on the rim, and Wren saw, with her keeper's eye, the faintest thread of light beginning to run from the basin toward the hills — thin as a spider's silk, but there, and brightening.

"Each well is its own," Wren said as they climbed the road. "That is the thing they could not believe."

Mell nodded, leaning on his staff. "A well does not take. It keeps what is planted, and gives back more. That is the whole of the rite, and the whole of the world."

Behind them, the sea murmured against the salt-white stones, and somewhere above it, invisible, the thread of light ran on to the next dark basin, waiting to be seeded.

Chapter 34: The Word Spreads

The word traveled faster than Wren could walk. Runners took it up the coast; carters carried it inland with their loads of wool and salt; the lamp-lighter, who went from village to village tending the evening lamps, carried it best of all, because he went everywhere and believed everything.

Haybrook, whose well-light had been the color of pale ale, sent a girl with a carved cup of moonwood. Fellgreen, where the light had been moss-green and the keeping an old women's art, sent two keepers who had kept the dipping all along and wept when they learned the planting. Crowhollow, whose deep blue well had gone dark last harvest, sent no one — Wren walked there herself through the hollow woods, and taught the verses to a people who had nearly given up on the idea of a moon.

Oldwick, the oldest well, near the neck of the peninsula, held out the longest. Its keepers were proud of their age and their memory, and did not wish to be taught by a girl. Mell went up himself, blind and patient, and sat with their eldest keeper for a day and a night, speaking of the book of leaves, until at last the eldest keeper brought out a cup of moon-silver, dented and plain as Thornwater's own, and said, "We kept ours too. We thought we were keeping a cup. We were keeping a seed."

Everywhere Wren went, she planted. She carried the cup in her belt and the verses in her heart, and she counted her steps on the long roads, and the silver threads that only she could see brightened beneath her feet, one by one, until the peninsula was strung with them like a harp.

"Can you see them?" the children asked her in each village. They could not see the threads, but they believed in them, which was the same thing, and they learned the verses before their elders, the way the children of Thornwater had.

At night, alone, Wren would take out the cup and hold it in both hands, and the light of it — the little thumb's depth of planted light, spring-green now — would warm her palms, and she would think of the Moon, far above, drinking only the overflow, never the seeded light, keeping her side of the bargain. And she would think of the star-splinter, kept dark in her pocket until she was home, and bloomed now over the mother well, and she would be glad.

In the villages she left behind, the basins stood round and quiet under their new-moon darkness. But to Wren's eye, and to any eye that had learned to look, the threads ran from every well toward the sea, bright as drawn silver, waiting for the full moon to fill them.

Chapter 35: The Moon's Blessing

Wren told the story of the star-splinter only once, and she told it to the children, because they were the ones who could hold it without breaking it.

"The Moon gave me a chip of her own light," she said. "She told me to keep it dark until I was home, and then to let it bloom. I carried it down the stair in my pocket, dark as a stone, and I was afraid the whole way that I would lose it, or that it would go out. But a seed does not go out. A seed waits."

She told them how she had opened it over the dry well at the new moon, and how it had bloomed into the first seed — the Moon's blessing as the first seed, silver and spring-green at once — and how the well had drunk it and given back a single drop. The children listened with their mouths open, and when she finished, they asked her to tell it again.

But the story was not the whole of the blessing. The blessing was in the night itself.

That spring the nights grew bright. It was not the moon's doing alone, or the wells' doing — it was the two together, the borrowed light going back up to the sky it had come from, the peninsula giving back what it had been given. The moon, full or thin, stood brighter than anyone remembered. Children could see their own shadows by moonlight, clear as at noon. Old people came out to stand in their doorways and weep at the brightness, because it was the brightness of their young years, the nights of their grandparents, the nights the wells had been full when they themselves were full of light.

"Look," said one old man, holding his hand up to the moon. "I can see the creases of my own palm."

The moon itself seemed closer, as if it had leaned down toward the peninsula, as if the bargain had drawn it near. On the brightest nights Wren could feel it like a presence at her shoulder — not the Moon herself, but her attention, her long grey attention, turned like a face toward the brightening wells.

The silver threads hummed. The wells filled. The air had a silver grain to it at dusk, the way the fields silvered when the light soaked in.

"It is the Moon's blessing," Mell said, standing at the well-side with his face lifted, though he could not see it. "She gave us one seed. We have planted it into a hundred wells. And now the night itself is the harvest." He smiled, and Wren thought he could see the brightness even so, through the light people carry, through the silver that was everywhere now, in everything.

That night Wren sat at the well's rim and watched the moon climb the sky, and she did not count her steps at all, because she was not frightened. She was only full, the way the wells were full, the way the night was full, and she held the cup in her hands and let it warm her, and thought that this, too, was the blessing: to be at home when the light came back.

Chapter 36: The Silver Harvest

On the full moon of spring, every well on Carrow bloomed.

They had not planned it. No one had sent word for it to happen on the same night; it was simply that the moon was full, and the wells were ready, and the keepers of a hundred basins knelt at the same hour under the same moon and spoke the same tending verse, and the light answered all at once, the way a field of wheat answers a wind.

Wren climbed the hill above Thornwater to watch. She had the cup with her, warm against her belt, and the children came up after her, and the old and the young, until half the village stood on the hill in the moonlight, and no one spoke.

From the hill, Wren could see the whole peninsula of Carrow laid out below her — the green hills, the three coasts, the ring of the Grey Sea. And she could see the wells.

They bloomed one after another, running west to east like a fire on still water: Saltmere's salt-white, Haybrook's pale ale, Fellgreen's moss-green, Crowhollow's deep blue, Oldwick's oldest light. And from each basin, a thread of silver ran out and up, and the threads met and wove in the air, and the web of light that had been strung thin and dark for a hundred years flared all at once, visible at last to every eye on the peninsula, and to Wren's, and to the children's, and to the old people who wept to see it.

For a long moment the whole peninsula was a net of light, drawn over the hills like a blessing, every well a bright knot, every thread a silver rope running to the sea and beyond it, to the mother well, to the Moon herself.

Wren heard a child say, "It is a harp. The whole world is a harp."

Mell, standing at the well's rim at the foot of the hill, felt the light come up through the stones and lift his face. "The threads," he said, though he could not see them. "They are bright. They are all bright."

The light held for the length of a held breath. Then, one by one, the wells began their dipping — the harvest, the luck taken for use — and the threads dimmed to a soft steady silver, and the net lay quiet across the hills, humming.

Wren stood at the top of the hill with the wind in her hair and the cup warm in her hand, and she did not count her steps, because there were none to count. She was home, and every well on Carrow was full, and the web that joined them was shining where everyone could see it.

"The luck is not for us alone," she said to the children. "It is for the whole peninsula. It always was. We only forgot how to share it."

Below, in the basin, the light lay like a held breath, spring-green and deep, and above it the moon stood full and calm, drinking only what spilled over, keeping her side of the bargain.

Chapter 37: The Festival

The Festival of the Wells had not been held in a hundred years, and nobody in Thornwater had ever seen one, but they made it up as they went, the way a village makes up a holiday, and it came out right.

The morning began with games in the meadow — a stone-lifting contest, a foot-race down the lane, a cheese-rolling down the hill that ended in the baker's yard and the general eating of the cheese. The baker had made silver bread, loaves shaped like moons and pennies, dusted with something that glittered, and the smell of it drew the whole village to the well-side by noon.

The children's procession came at dusk. Each child carried a cup — carved moonwood, or a thimble, or a chipped cup from home, or their cupped hands — and they walked three times around the well, singing the verses in their small clear voices, getting the words wrong in charming ways and being right anyway.

"What the moon lends," sang the smallest, "the well must keep."

"The well gives back what the keeper plants," sang another, a little off.

They were not the words. But the well's light rose a finger's width to meet them, and the children shrieked with delight, and Wren, watching from the well's rim, laughed until her sides hurt.

The Harr sisters shared a dance. It was the first thing anyone could remember them sharing, and the whole village stopped to watch them circle the green, hands joined, Adah's geese pattering along behind, and when the dance ended the sisters curtsied to each other like girls, and the village applauded.

The feast was long and loud. Silver bread, and Pella's butter, and the first greens of spring, and a barrel of pale ale that went around twice. And when the moon rose, full and bright above the well, the whole village sang the old rite aloud — all seven verses, from the Tending to the Planting, the ones Mell had taught and the one Wren had added for the dry year — and their voices went up into the night like a thread of light, and Wren thought she felt, far above, the Moon lean down to listen.

"Come," said Sorrel, tugging her sleeve. "You must dance too."

Wren danced. She was small and a little clumsy, but the village made a ring for her, and the children spun her until she was dizzy, and the well's light threw their shadows long and silver across the green.

Later, when the feast had gone quiet and the children slept on the grass, Wren sat alone at the well's rim. The cup was warm in her hand. Below her, the light lay full and calm, and above her, the moon, and around her, the whole village asleep under its web.

She had left this well dry. She had walked the stair to the moon and brought back a forgotten rite. And now, she thought, now it was theirs — the rite, the well, the luck — and she was only its keeper, the way she had always wanted to be.

Home, she thought. Home.

Chapter 38: Old Mell's Sight

On the next full moon, Mell knelt at the well to speak the tending verse.

It was his place, and he had spoken it every full moon for nine years, blind, with the verse worn smooth in his mouth. Wren knelt beside him with the cup ready, and the village stood in a ring behind them, and the well's light lay spring-green and deep, full to the rim.

He spoke the verse. His voice held, the old words, and as he spoke the light rose toward him, and the cup in Wren's hand began to hum.

And then Mell opened his eyes.

He had not opened them in nine years, not truly — he had kept them closed against the dark, the way a man closes a door against a draft. Now he opened them, and the light of the well went into him like water into a dry thing, and the village, watching, saw the old keeper's face change.

For a moment he said nothing. His hands, his seeing hands, hung motionless at his sides. Then he said, very quietly, "I can see the light."

No one breathed.

"Blue," he said, wonder making his voice young. "There is blue in it, and green, like spring water over grass. I had forgotten there was blue in it."

The village wept. Wren wept, kneeling there, the cup shaking in her hand. And Mell rose, and turned, and looked at them — looked at them — his eyes taking in the faces of the people he had heard for nine years and never seen.

The children pressed forward, and he knew them by their voices and then by their faces, joining each name to a face he had only carried in the dark. "Sorrel," he said. "I always knew you were quick. I see it in your eyes."

Then he looked at his house. He had not seen his own house in nine years. He stood before it with his head tilted, taking it in — the stone, the low roof, the door he had walked through blind a thousand times. Tears ran down his face and he did not wipe them.

"Old Mell," said the baker, and could say no more.

At last Mell turned to Wren, and looked at her for the first time. She stood small before him, holding the cup, and the light of the well was behind her, and Mell looked at her for a long time.

"So," he said at last, his voice rough with joy. "This is the face of the girl who walked to the moon."

And he smiled, and the village laughed and wept all at once, and the well's light lay full and quiet between them, and somewhere far above, the Moon looked down at the brightening peninsula, at the blind keeper who could see again, and was glad.

Chapter 39: The Blind Man's Gift

The sight came back to Mell, but it did not come back empty. Nine years of blindness had left a gift in its place, and the gift showed itself in the days after the well restored him.

It began at the well-side. A boy came with a small hurt — a cut on his palm from a borrowed knife — and Mell, looking at him, went still. "You carry a light," he said, "like a candle behind glass. Everyone carries one. I can see them now."

The boy looked at his own palm, puzzled. But Mell was not speaking of the cut. He was speaking of the light people carry, the silver that had always been in everyone, visible at last to the eyes that had learned to see without them.

He walked through the village that summer like a man newly rich, and everyone came to be looked at. "You burn like a harvest moon," he told Pella, and Pella wept. "You carry the light of a low well, but it is deep," he told the baker, and the baker laughed and hid his face. He named the silver in the children like a man counting coins, and the children preened and demanded to be told again.

And to Wren, standing at the keeper's door in the evening light, he said: "You burn like a new moon. Thin, and all ahead of you."

She did not know what to say. She had walked to the moon and back, and she still could not hold a compliment without feeling the small tremor of being seen.

Mell was quiet a moment. Then he reached up and took the keeper's staff from the hook by the door — the staff he had walked with blind for nine years, the one he had leaned on through every dry month — and held it out to her.

"This is yours," he said.

Wren stared at it. "No. It is yours. You are the keeper."

"I am the remembering heart," Mell said. "That is a thing, and a good thing, and it is enough. But the staff must go to the hands that will walk with it. I have seen what you can do, child. You planted light in a hundred dark places. You are the keeper now, or you will be, and the staff should learn your hand before the village learns your name."

Wren took the staff. It was warm, and worn smooth by Mell's nine years of blind walking, and heavier than it looked.

"The dark below and the dark above are the same dark," she said, the old words coming up of themselves.

"And only your heart stands between," Mell finished. He smiled, and his new eyes were bright. "You have been the keeper for a long time, Wren. We only waited for you to believe it."

She held the staff, and the cup was warm at her belt, and the well's light lay green in the dusk, and she did not count her steps, because she was home.

Chapter 40: Wren's Mastership

The village named Wren well-keeper at the first full moon of summer, and the naming was a ceremony, and the ceremony was older than anyone could say.

They gathered at the well in the long light of evening. The councillors came, and the baker, and Pella with her best butter, and the Harr sisters arm in arm, and the children in a bright ring, each holding a cup. Sorrel stood at the front with her carved moonwood cup held out before her like a lantern.

Mell stood at the well's rim with the staff — her staff now, though he had carried it up for her — and the silvered cup in his hands. It hummed softly, the way it hummed when it remembered.

"Come forward," Mell said.

Wren came forward. She was small, and the village had forgotten her name for seventeen years, and now they were all here to say it, and the weight of that pressed on her chest like a held breath.

The cup passed from Mell's hands to hers. It was warm. It had been warm to her hand since before anyone believed her, since the dry winter, since she had stood alone at an empty basin with a cold cup and a heart full of counting steps.

"Say the verses," Mell said.

Wren spoke them. The Tending, the Planting, the Verse of the Walk, the Verse of the Cup, the Verse of the Thread, the Verse of Thanks — and then her own, the Verse of the Dry Year, the one she had added, and her voice did not shake, and the well's light rose a finger's width to meet her, and the children said the last line with her in their small clear voices.

"What the moon lends, the well must keep. What the well gives, the keeper plants."

The village blessed her. The councillors, who had laughed at her, bowed their heads. The baker pressed a silver loaf into her hands. And Wren stood at the well's rim with the cup and the staff, and the weight of the keeping settled onto her shoulders, and she was afraid.

"The well is the cup," Mell said, low, so only she could hear. "You are the seed."

Wren looked down at the light, spring-green and deep, and she thought of the dark it had come from — the dry winter, the empty basin, the cold cup, the long stair into the sky. She had been afraid of the dark of wells since the winter she dropped a bucket and the dark said nothing back. And now she was the one who planted light into dark places.

"I will keep it," she said. Not to the village, but to the well, and to the cup, and to the Moon far above who had taught her the planting.

The children cheered. The geese set up a clamor. And Wren, keeper of Thornwater, stood at the rim of the full well with the cup warm in her hand and the staff firm in her grip, and she counted her steps — one, two, three — and found that she was not frightened after all.

"To the planting," she said, and the village answered her, and the full moon rose over the full well like a benediction.

Chapter 41: The New Keeper

Sorrel was twelve the winter she followed Wren through the dry months, and she had learned the verses before anyone else in the village, singing them to the stray goat and to her own shadow. When Wren asked her, one morning at the well's rim, whether she would carry the cup, Sorrel said yes before the question had finished. She said it the way a well says yes to rain.

The first lessons were the small ones. How to hold the cup — not like a treasure, like a hand, palm open underneath, the way you hold a thing you mean to keep warm. How to read the well's face, the skin of light on the water, and to know by its color whether the night had been kind. Sorrel learned them the way Wren had learned them from Mell: by doing, and by being watched while she did.

"The well is the cup," Mell said from the keeper's house, and his restored eyes followed the girl as she crossed the yard. "You are the seed. Plant yourself."

Wren watched her apprentice dip the cup at the full moon, and something in her chest closed and opened like a shutter. The girl's small hands trembled. The cup hummed — the old hum, the harvest hum — and bloomed with a thumb's depth of moonlight, and Sorrel's face caught the light and held it, and Wren saw herself, three seasons younger, afraid of the dark of wells and walking anyway.

"Hold it steady," Wren said. "Let it be yours. The moon lends it to you because you tend it."

"It's warm," Sorrel whispered.

"Then it remembers you. It's a seed, and it knows whose soil it stands in."

They practiced until the moon set. Sorrel walked home with the cup wrapped in her sleeve, talking to it in the old way, and Wren stood at the well's rim and listened to the girl's voice fade up the lane, and she thought of how the rite had come to her — hand to hand, cup to cup, one seed planted in one girl's keeping.

That night the well held more light than it had the morning before. It did that now, always — because it was planted, and tended, and watched over by hands that loved it. Wren warmed her own hands on the cup's glow and counted the steps home, and she was not afraid.

She was not afraid because the rite had hands again. It had Sorrel's hands now, small and brave and learning.

Chapter 42: The Rite Kept

A year passed, and the rite lived in the village's bones. Children sang the verses at their chores — the tending verse at milking, the planting verse when they sowed seed in the dark soil, misremembering the words in charming ways and being right anyway. The stray goat answered the songs. The Harr sisters planted in strict turns and argued only about the weather.

Every new moon Wren poured the cup's light back into the well, and every full moon she dipped it again, and the light returned brighter each time, spring-green, the color of hope. The well was never empty now. It shone like new butter in the morning, like the memory of butter in the winter dark.

In the keeper's stone house, Mell and Wren re-copied the book of leaves. The old pages had gone soft as moth-wings, and the whole rite was written down again in Wren's careful hand — the seven verses, the two halves of the rite, the bargain struck with the Moon. When she reached the end of the book, she sat a long time with the quill in her hand, and then she turned to a clean page and wrote the eighth verse, the one that had been missing.

She called it the Verse of the Dry Year, and it was short, the way the dry year had been short of everything.

"Remember the empty basin. Remember the cold cup. What the moon lends, the well must keep; what the well gives, the keeper plants. And when the well runs dry, do not despair — the seed is still in the dark, and the dark is only the soil."

Mell read it with his fingers, for the joy of it, though his eyes were whole again. He read it twice, and he was quiet for a long while.

"It was never only about the light," he said. "It was about remembering the dark. A rite that remembers the bad year can keep the good ones."

Wren bound the book in moonwood and laid it in its place, and the children who had learned the old verses badly came to learn the new one well, and the book's pages began, at last, to be turned.

That winter the refrain went through the village like a song that belonged to everyone. She heard it at the forge, at the dairy, in the schoolhouse where the littlest children clapped the words like a counting rhyme. It was no longer Wren's saying or Mell's saying or the Moon's saying. It was the village's.

What the moon lends, the well must keep. What the well gives, the keeper plants.

Wren stood at the well's rim and listened to the whole village say it, and the cup was warm in her hand, and the book of leaves was whole, and she understood that a rite is not a thing you finish. It is a thing you keep, the way you keep a fire — by tending it, and by handing it on.

Chapter 43: The Winter Moon

Winter came down over Carrow like a lid, and the wells went under ice.

The other villages sent word, village to village, that the planting had been done through the ice — light under ice, a lantern in the dark. Wren learned the trick at Thornwater's well: you could not dip through the ice, but you could pour, and the poured light sank through the clear dark water like a slow star and settled at the bottom, waiting for the spring the way seed waits.

On the longest night of the year, Wren went to the well alone.

The village slept under a sky of hard cold stars. The well's ice was a round mirror, and under it she could see the planted light — a green glow, deep and patient, breathing like something asleep. The air was so still she could hear her own heartbeat. She counted her steps to the well's rim and knelt.

She spoke the planting verse, her breath making a small white bloom in the dark, and she poured the cup's light onto the ice. It ran to the center of the frozen basin and pooled there, and the ice began to bloom — a flower of light spreading under the surface, cracking the frost in silver veins, opening like a water-lily at midnight.

Above her the winter moon was high and white, and she felt its presence the way she felt the cup's warmth — as a thing leaning toward her, attentive, almost breathing.

She looked up. She could not see the Moon's face. But she felt the old hunger in the night — softened now, patient now, the way a well-fed thing is patient — and she felt the Moon looking down at the bloom of green light in the ice, and something passed between them that was not words.

She did not speak. The Moon did not speak. But the ice bloomed brighter, and the cup warmed in Wren's hands, and she knew she was not alone in the keeping of this night. She was the keeper below. The Moon was the keeper above. Between them the well held its green seed, and the world turned.

She stayed until the light under the ice had folded itself small and golden, like a heart, and then she walked home counting her steps, the winter moon riding the ridge of the hill behind her like a shepherd.

In the morning the children came to see the ice bloom, and the green light was still there under the frost, and the oldest of them said it looked like the village's own moon, planted deep, waiting for the thaw.

Chapter 44: The Spring Wells

The melt came, and the wells rose.

Thornwater's basin brimmed and spilled over — the first overflow in living memory — and the silvered light ran down the keeper's stones and soaked into the lane, and the lane drank it, and the lane's grass turned the color of a new penny. The village gathered at the rim and watched the water shine, and nobody said the old prayers, because there was no need to ask anymore. There was only the light, and the light was enough.

The whole peninsula woke that spring. Lambs dropped in Saltmere's salt fields. The Harr sisters' fence — mended now, and painted — held through the April winds, and the two of them leaned on it and watched the same moon rise, not speaking, which for them was peace. Two weddings were held in Thornwater in one month, and the cakes were baked with well-water, and the well-water made the cakes lighter than air.

On the first full moon of spring, Wren walked the peninsula.

She went village to village — Saltmere, Haybrook, Fellgreen, Crowhollow, Oldwick at the neck of the land — and at each well she looked down into the bright basin and saw the threads. Only she could see them: silver lines running from well to well to well, the web of Carrow lighting up under the waxing moon like veins in a leaf, thin and dark and brightening.

At Oldwick, the oldest well, she knelt at the rim and put her hand in the water, and the light came up through her fingers like milk through grass.

"It's full," said the Oldwick keeper, an old woman with her sight gone the same way Mell's had gone, and restored the same way. "It's the fullest it's been since the well was young. How does it happen, girl? How does the whole peninsula fill at once?"

"It was always one well," Wren said. "We only ever planted it in pieces. The moon gives to all of them, and they give back to each other, and the web holds."

She walked home with the cup warm against her chest, and from the north hill she stopped and turned, and she saw, with her keeper's eye, the whole web alight across the green dark of the peninsula — a net of silver thread between the villages, bright as the inside of a shell.

She counted her steps home and she was not afraid. The spring was the proof. Every well on Carrow was full because every well had been planted, and the planting was the keeping, and the keeping was hers to hand on.

Chapter 45: The Silver Trade

That summer the trade began, and it was not the trade of silver.

At the fair at Haybrook, the villages of Carrow gathered on the green between the ale-colored well and the new stalls, and they traded the things a people trade when they have had a good year: bread for honey, wool for iron, moonshine for salt. But they traded other things too, and those were the ones Wren watched.

A Saltmere woman traded a song for a cup — a moonwood cup, carved by a Haybrook carver, plain and a little dented, meant to grow a seed. An Oldwick man traded a verse for a young goat, and the goat was called Verse, and it was the finest goat in the pen. Cups and seeds moved from hand to hand. Moonwood was carved. The rite traveled the peninsula the way the silver threads traveled it — not sold, but given, and given in exchange for other gifts.

The light itself could not be traded. Wren said so at the fair, standing on a cart so the whole green could hear.

"You cannot carry another village's light home with you," she said. "It will not burn in a strange basin. It must be seeded where it grows. But the knowing — the knowing you can trade, for bread and songs and goats. The knowing is the seed that fits every soil."

She was honored at the fair. The villages had wanted to give her silver, and she had refused, and so they gave her the things that cost more: a Saltmere shawl, a Haybrook loaf baked with well-water, an Oldwick cup carved by the old woman's own hands, with a verse of thanks cut around its rim. She held them, and she felt the weight of them, and she understood what Mell had meant when he said the light is not owned.

It is borrowed from the night and returned. It is shared by being planted, and by being given away, and the wealth that hoards goes dark, and the wealth that is planted returns brighter.

She came home to Thornwater with her arms full of gifts, and the children ran out to meet her, and Sorrel took the Oldwick cup and turned it in her hands, reading the verse around the rim aloud, stumbling over a word, getting it right anyway.

"The whole peninsula knows it now," Sorrel said.

"The whole peninsula keeps it," said Wren. "That's the whole of the trade."

And she set the Oldwick cup on the shelf beside the silvered one, and the two cups stood together, plain and patient, one old and one new, and the well outside shone green in the dusk, and the night came down warm and full of the smell of bread.

Chapter 46: The Moon's Thanks

A year after the bargain, Wren made the Keeper's Walk.

She had told the village she would go alone, and they had let her, because it was the price of the bargain — one keeper, once a year, one cup shared, so that the Moon is never alone one night in the year. The stair rose over the tended well on the full moon, and she stepped onto it and did not look back, because the village had looked enough for her.

The climb was easier than the first. She knew the rule now: a step holds while the climber believes. She counted her steps. The earth dropped away below her, a blue lamp in the dark, and her shadow pointed toward it, and she let it point, because home is the thing you are called toward, and you can be called and still go.

The Moon met her at the palace threshold, and Wren saw at once that she had changed. The hunger was gone from her face, or nearly. The weariness had turned into something quieter — the weariness of a long winter that has ended, not of one that will not break. Her grey hair of light moved like a river. Her eyes were the color of the sky before dawn.

"Seed-Bearer," the Moon said, and Wren understood this was her name now, given and worn. "You have kept your promise. I have kept mine. I drink only the overflow, and I have learned to wait."

They sat together at the mother well, and Wren dipped the silvered cup, and it bloomed — a thumb's depth of moonlight, spring-green at the heart — and she held it out, and the Moon drank. It was the first cup shared in a hundred years. Wren watched the light go down and felt the circle of the year close: harvest, seed, harvest, seed, and one cup of it, once a year, given back to the one who lent it.

The Moon showed her the threads. From the mother well they ran out silver in every direction, to every well on Carrow, and every one was bright — a web of light across the peninsula, visible from the moon itself.

"The night is bright again," the Moon said. "I am not afraid of the dark anymore. You planted that, Seed-Bearer, in the dark of the dry year, and it took."

Wren knelt and spoke the verse of thanks, and the Moon listened with her head tilted, like the gardener, and when the verse was done she touched the cup with one long finger of light, and the cup rang like a bell, warm with remembering.

"Come home," the Moon said. "The stair waits. Come again next year. I will be watching on the night the well is full."

Wren walked the stair down before dawn, the earth rising to meet her, blue and warm and hers, counting her steps the whole way, not afraid — because the dark above and the dark below were the same dark, and she carried a light of her own in her chest now, and she knew its name.

Chapter 47: The Night Bright

The night itself had changed, and the village felt it before anyone could name it.

The dark was no longer the Moon's enemy. It was simply the dark — the soft, held breath between the lamps of the stars — and it carried its own silver now, the way a well carries its own light. Children saw their shadows by moonlight on the clear nights, long and strange and beautiful, and old people came out to weep at the brightness, and nobody thought it strange that they wept.

Wren watched from the hill above Thornwater.

She had walked up after the last of the village had gone in, and she stood at the top of the hill with the cup warm in her hands, and she looked out over the peninsula. The web was faintly visible now to ordinary eyes — a shimmer in the air between the villages, a silvering of the fields at dusk, the sense of a net drawn over the green land, holding everything gently, the way a net holds fruit without bruising it.

The stars seemed closer. That was not true, and she knew it was not true, but it was true the way the well's light was true — a thing you could not measure and could not argue with. The moon rode high, full and white, and the moon's light lay on the sleeping village like a hand on a child's hair.

She thought of the Moon, high above her own land, no longer afraid. She thought of the dry year, of the empty basin, of the cold cup, of Mell's blind hands finding the crack in the rim. All of it had led here, to a night so bright the village slept without candles and no one dreamed of being afraid.

The peninsula slept under its web. The wells held their green light. The night held its silver. And the moon — the moon was no longer drinking. It was looking, and what it was looking at, Wren understood, was the world it lit, the way a mother looks at a sleeping child, not wanting anything, only watching.

She stayed on the hill until the first pale thread of dawn, and then she walked down counting her steps, and the dark she had feared as a girl — the dark of wells, the dark that said nothing back — had become the dark that holds the light, and waits, and lets it grow.

Chapter 48: The Peninsula

The story went past the neck of Carrow, and the mainland heard of it as a tale — and then as more than a tale.

It traveled with the travelers: the carters who carried Saltmere salt across the neck, the fishermen who rounded the headland into the Grey Sea and told their yarns in mainland harbors, the lamp-lighter who walked from village to village with his flame and his news. They carried the verses and the cup-seeds and the way of it, and the mainland laughed, because the mainland had never seen a well fill with moonlight and had no reason to believe.

Then the ship came.

She was a deep-hulled mainland ship, come up the coast with wool and iron, and her captain stood at the Thornwater well with her hands on her hips and looked down into the green light and was, for the first time in her life, silent.

"We heard it was true," the captain said at last. "We did not believe it. My grandmother said the wells of Carrow were the luckiest place on the sea, and we laughed at her, and I am here to say we were wrong to laugh."

The village gave her bread and well-water, and the captain asked the question Wren had been waiting for.

"Can we have them? On the mainland. Wells that fill with light."

Wren thought about it a long moment. The mainland had no moonwells, no keeper's stones, no cups. It had rivers and rain and the long grey sea, and none of them held moonlight the way a tended basin could. But it had dark places. Every village had a dark place, the way every night has a far side.

"You cannot take a well home in a sack," she said. "The light must be seeded where it grows. But every village has a dark place. Plant light in it. Find a basin, or carve one, and tend it, and at the full moon offer it your cup, and see what comes."

The captain did not laugh. She wrote the words down in her log, slowly, in the lamplight, and she asked Wren to say the planting verse again so she could write it true, and she did, and the ship sailed in the morning with a moonwood cup in the captain's chest and a seed of knowing in her head.

From the hill Wren watched the ship go down the coast, small and white and brave, carrying the story out of the story's country.

"The mainland has no wells yet," said Sorrel, who had come to watch.

"Yet," said Wren. "That is the whole word. They have the dark, and the dark is only the soil. They will plant, and they will keep, and the light will know where it grew."

And she turned from the sea, and the web behind her was bright, and she counted her steps home, thinking of all the dark places in the world, and of what it would mean when they were planted, one by one, with light.

Chapter 49: The Keeper's Walk

The walk became a rite, and the rite became the village's.

At the full moon of the first summer after the trade, Wren led the whole of Thornwater up the lane to the well, and the village stood in a ring around the basin, and the silvered cup passed from hand to hand while the tending verse was said — each hand holding it a moment, each voice adding to the hum, the cup warm with all of them. The stray goat stood in the ring too, and nobody shooed it.

"Every keeper will walk once a year," Wren said, "and once in their life, climb. The climb is not for the well. The well is tended by the keeping. The climb is for the Moon — so that she is never alone one night in the year. And it is for you, so that you know the way."

The children asked her about the first climb then, the way they always asked, and Wren told the story again, from the beginning — the dry basin at dawn, the cold cup, Mell's blind hands finding the crack, the stair rising out of a drop of silver, the white hare on the last step. The children knew it by heart and listened anyway, the way you listen to a song you know, to hear it kept true.

At the edge of the light, where the well's glow faded into the dark lane, something moved.

Wren saw it first: a shape in the margin of the light, white, still. The white hare. It sat at the very edge of the well's brightness, watching her with its one dark eye, and it had been there, she understood, the whole time — on the last step, at the Grey Sea, at the fruit of the Silver Forest. It was not explained. It did not need to be. It was always going to be there.

Sorrel saw it too. Her breath caught.

"Is it real?" she whispered.

"Real as the well," Wren said.

She took the cup from Sorrel and held it toward the hare, and the hare looked at the cup, and then — in a blink, in a breath, the way it had come — it was gone. The edge of the light was empty. The dark lane was empty. But the cup was warm in Wren's hand, warmer than it had been a moment ago, and it hummed a low single note, like a word held.

Wren passed the cup to Sorrel for the closing verse, and Sorrel spoke the Verse of the Walk with her small steady voice, and the cup passed back to Wren, and the rite was kept.

That night Wren dreamed of the moon's land, and in the dream the white hare sat on the last step, and it turned to look at her, and its eye was the color of the sky before dawn.

Chapter 50: The Full Moon, Again

A year and a day after the first planting, the full moon rose over Thornwater, and the well was full.

The images of that other dawn, the dry one, were all turned about: then the basin had been empty, the cup cold, the village blaming; now the basin brimmed with green light, the cup was warm in Sorrel's hands, and the village stood at the rim the way a village stands at a fire — not to watch it go out, but to keep it, and to be warmed.

Sorrel knelt at the well alone. It was her first solo tending, and Wren stood behind her in the keeper's place and did not help, because help was the one thing a keeper must not give another keeper. Sorrel spoke the tending verse, her voice a little high, a little fast, and the well answered her — the light thickened and rose, and the cup dipped and bloomed with a thumb's depth of moonlight, spring-green, the color of hope.

The children were planting at the rim, their small moonwood cups pouring the well's light back into the dark they had made in the garden soil, the littlest one misremembering the planting verse and being right anyway. The stray goat stood among them, grave as an elder. The Harr sisters shared a dance at the edge of the light. Pella's milk had sweetened that morning, and the whole village had tasted it in their tea and known what it meant.

Wren watched it all from the well's rim, and then she looked up.

Far above, high and white and full, the Moon was looking down.

Wren could not see her face. The moon was only light. But she felt her — the old hunger gone, the loneliness gone, the long silver hair moving like a river, the eyes the color of the sky before dawn — and she felt the Moon look at the well, and at the children, and at the girl at the well's rim with her blooming cup, and the Moon was not drinking.

The Moon was smiling.

Something in Wren's chest loosened, the way a hand loosens around a seed at the moment it is planted. She warmed her hands on the cup's glow. She counted her steps, though she was not going anywhere. The night was bright. The well was full. The world was turning toward spring.

Beside her, Sorrel rose, holding the cup like a small moon of her own, and together they spoke the words that had carried the whole story — the words that were no longer the Moon's or the village's or Wren's, but everyone's, the oldest rite, the shortest prayer.

"What the moon lends, the well must keep. What the well gives, the keeper plants."

And the moon's light lay in the well like a held breath, and the night was bright, and somewhere far above, on the moon's land, the Moon kept her own small well planted with a silver tear, and she was not alone, and she would never be alone again.
